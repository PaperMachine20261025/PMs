How organizational support enhances teachers’ AI literacy: the chain mediating eﬀects of innovation capability and technology acceptance

Jianfeng Fang a,b* and Mengting Qian a,c

aFaculty of Education, East China Normal University, Shanghai, People’s Republic of China; bDepartment of Educational Planning, Shanghai Academy of Educational Sciences, Shanghai, People’s Republic of China; cBerkeley School of Education, University of California, Berkeley, CA, USA

ABSTRACT Teachers’ artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) literacy is a critical competency for building eﬀective interactive learning environments, yet a substantial gap persists between organizational support and teachers’ individual capacities. Drawing on the Technology–Organization– Environment (TOE) framework, this study employs structural equation modeling to analyze survey data from 10,683 primary and secondary school teachers in Shanghai, China, examining the mechanism through which organizational support enhances teachers’ AI literacy via innovation capability and technology acceptance. The ﬁndings reveal that organizational support exerts both a signiﬁcant direct eﬀect on AI literacy (18.2% of total eﬀect) and substantial indirect eﬀects through innovation capability (22.4%) and technology acceptance (22.8%). Most importantly, innovation capability and technology acceptance jointly form a chain mediation pathway accounting for 36.6% of the total eﬀect, revealing a complete mechanism: organizational support → innovation capability → technology acceptance → AI literacy. This study is the ﬁrst to clarify this chain transmission mechanism, oﬀering a new theoretical perspective for understanding how organizational factors systematically promote teachers’ AI literacy. Based on these ﬁndings, a four-level collaborative framework—institutional support, resource coordination, mechanism innovation, and agent empowerment—is proposed to provide actionable strategies for enhancing teachers’ AI literacy and optimizing interactive learning environments.

1. Introduction

The digital transformation of education has become a major driving force in global educational reform, profoundly reshaping learning environments and teachers’ professional practice. In this process, teachers’ artiﬁ- cial intelligence (AI) literacy is not only a core vehicle for technology use but also a key prerequisite for the sustainable development of digital education (Le et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). The Artiﬁcial Intelligence Competency Framework for Teachers, released by the United Nations Educational, Scientiﬁc and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) in 2024, explicitly identiﬁes AI literacy as a core competency for teachers in the era of intelligent education, encompassing technical proﬁciency, ethical reﬂection, and pedagogical innovation (UNESCO, 2024). In China, the Opinions on Accelerating the Advancement of Educational Digitalization and the Guidelines for General Artiﬁcial Intelligence Education in Primary and Secondary Schools, both issued in 2025, emphasize the importance of deep integration between AI and education for improving educational quality. However, a substantial gap remains between policy aspirations and implementation in practice. Relevant studies have shown that Chinese teachers still demonstrate limitations in both the breadth and depth of AI application in educational settings (Yang et al., 2023). Many teachers struggle to use AI tools proﬁciently to design inquiry-based learning activities, and most applications remain at the level of basic operation, lacking systematic pedagogical redesign and in-depth reﬂection on AI ethics (Chiu et al., 2024a). This tension is closely associated with a “dual disconnect” in organizational support. First, at the resource level, there is a structural mismatch between supply and educational needs. Although schools have generally increased investment in intelligent hardware, the development of AI curricular resources, interdisciplinary case repositories, and technical support systems has lagged behind substantially (Zeng, 2022). This conﬁguration pattern, characterized by an emphasis on hardware over content, places teachers in the dilemma of “having access to technology but being unable to use it eﬀectively,” thereby hindering the deep integration of AI into teaching. Second, at the mechanism level, school support tends to focus on short-term skills training while neglecting the synergistic relationship between the cultivation of teachers’ innovation capability and their psychological readiness to accept technology. This fragmented support model fails to stimulate teachers’ intrinsic motivation and obstructs their transition from “technology users” to “educational innovators” (Xu & Zhu, 2023).

To address this dual disconnect, the present study draws on the Technology–Organization–Environment (TOE) framework (Tornatzky et al., 1990) and proposes a four-level collaborative framework consisting of institutional support, resource coordination, mechanism innovation, and agent empowerment as a systematic solution for narrowing the gap between policy aspirations and practical implementation. By progressively addressing the directional disconnect between macro-level policy and school-level implementation, the structural mismatch inherent in the hardware-centered model, the fragmented support problem of short-term skills training, and teachers’ transformation from “technology users” to “educational innovators,” this framework oﬀers an integrated and multilevel solution that connects organizational resource provision with individual capability development, providing a clear theoretical anchor for the subsequent empirical investigation.

In the international literature, teachers’ AI literacy has rapidly emerged as a growing area of research. Recent systematic reviews have shown that, although AI literacy is receiving increasing attention in K–12 education, research speciﬁcally focused on teachers’ AI literacy remains at an early stage (Biagini, 2025; Casal-Otero et al., 2023). Studies of preservice teachers further suggest that the development of AI literacy depends not only on external support such as training and curricular resources but also substantially on learners’ motivation and attitudes (Lee & Song, 2024; Qian & Yan, 2026). By contrast, related research in the Chinese academic context has largely remained at the levels of information literacy and digital literacy, with relatively limited attention given to teachers’ AI literacy per se, especially to the role of organizational support mechanisms. Accordingly, this study raises a central question: How can school organizational support move beyond superﬁcial technological enablement and promote the deeper development of teachers’ AI literacy through the mediating roles of innovation capability and technology acceptance? Addressing this question can not only help bridge the gap between macro-level policy and micro-level practice but also provide actionable pathways for mechanism optimization and a systematic practical framework for advancing the digital transformation of education.

2. Literature review and research hypotheses

2.1. Theoretical framework: The Technology–Organization–Environment Model

The Technology–Organization–Environment (TOE) framework, proposed by Tornatzky et al. (1990), provides a systematic analytical perspective for understanding the process of technological innovation adoption at the organizational level. According to this framework, an organization’s decisions regarding technological innovation are jointly shaped by three key dimensions: the technological context, the organizational context, and the environmental context. The framework has been extensively validated in studies of information technology adoption and digital transformation (Baker, 2011; Gangwar et al., 2015) and, in recent years, has been widely applied to emerging technological domains such as blockchain adoption (Chittipaka et al., 2023) and artiﬁcial intelligence applications (Gupta et al., 2026). The TOE framework not only oﬀers a theoretical explanation for the mechanism through which organizational support operates, but also provides the theoretical foundation for understanding the chain transmission pathway of “organizational support → innovation capability → technology acceptance → AI literacy,” as shown in Figure 1.

The technological context encompasses the characteristics of technologies available both within and outside the organization, including attributes such as relative advantage, complexity, and compatibility. In the context of teachers’ AI literacy development, the technological context is reﬂected in technology acceptance, particularly in terms of the functional features of AI tools, their operational complexity, and their compatibility with existing instructional systems. These factors directly shape teachers’ perceptions of the usefulness and ease of use of AI technologies (Gangwar et al., 2015).

The organizational context reﬂects the internal characteristics and resource allocation of the organization, including management support, technological infrastructure, human resources, and organizational culture. In the present study, the organizational context is primarily reﬂected in school organizational support, encompassing dimensions such as work support, value recognition, beneﬁt-related care, interpersonal coordination, and professional development (Zhu et al., 2006). As the starting point of the chain mediation process, organizational support initiates the entire transmission mechanism by stimulating teachers’ technology acceptance and innovation capability.

The environmental context encompasses the organization’s external institutional and competitive environment, including factors such as policies and regulations, industry standards, and competitive pressure, all of which are closely related to teachers’ innovation capability. In the development of teachers’ AI literacy, the environmental context is mainly reﬂected in policy orientations toward educational digitalization, development trends in the AI education industry, and society’s acceptance of AI in education. Among these factors, an innovative climate is particularly critical to teachers’ innovation capability (Ng et al., 2023).

The TOE framework aligns closely with the chain mediation logic of the present study and also provides theoretical support for the proposed four-level collaborative framework. Speciﬁcally, the three dimensions correspond to diﬀerent stages of the chain transmission process: the organizational dimension initiates the chain, the technological dimension shapes the transformation process, and the environmental dimension reinforces the transmission mechanism. Together, these dimensions constitute a comprehensive mechanism underlying the development of teachers’ AI literacy. This multidimensional interactive mechanism provides a theoretical basis for understanding the pathways through which organizational support inﬂuences teachers’ AI literacy.

2.2. Applications of AI in education and other sectors: implications for teachers’ AI literacy

The widespread application of artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) across education and other sectors provides an important backdrop for understanding the signiﬁcance of teachers’ AI literacy. In education, AI technologies are demonstrating substantial transformative potential across multiple domains: personalized learning systems tailor content to individual students’ needs (Peng et al., 2019), intelligent tutoring systems

AI is also reshaping curriculum development and content generation, with AI-driven tools enabling more responsive and learner-centered curricula (Abbasi et al., 2025) and generative AI assisting teachers in creating diverse instructional resources (Long & Magerko, 2020). In assessment, automated scoring and feedback systems provide timely, scalable evaluation while generating formative data to inform instructional improvement (Qian & Yan, 2026). Learning analytics technologies support more precise instructional decisionmaking by mining educational data to reveal patterns in student learning behaviors (Tang et al., 2023). Furthermore, AI-integrated virtual learning environments are creating immersive and interactive spaces that extend the boundaries of conventional classroom instruction (Wang & Huang, 2025).

From a cross-sector perspective, successful AI applications in other ﬁelds also oﬀer important insights for the development of AI in education and underscore the broader signiﬁcance of AI literacy. In healthcare, AI- driven diagnostic systems and clinical decision-support tools demonstrate how human expertise can be enhanced through human–AI collaboration, requiring professionals to develop the kind of AI literacy needed to work eﬀectively with intelligent systems rather than merely operate tools (Liaw et al., 2022; Topol, 2019; Zöller et al., 2025). In ﬁnance, algorithmic trading and risk assessment models illustrate that AI integration requires not only technical competence but also ethical awareness regarding algorithmic bias and decision transparency (Vuković et al., 2025; Yeo et al., 2025). In manufacturing, Industry 4.0 initiatives suggest that successful AI adoption depends on a comprehensive organizational support system, including systematic training, adaptive management structures, and an innovation-supportive culture (Ghobakhloo et al., 2022; Nayernia et al., 2022). Across these sectors, the evidence consistently indicates that AI literacy has become a foundational competency in contemporary society that transcends sectoral boundaries, and that its development depends critically on the organizational ecosystem rather than on individual eﬀort alone (Casal-Otero et al., 2023).

These cross-sector insights suggest that the development of teachers’ AI literacy must be understood within the broader context of professional transformation, in which learning technologies are reshaping the fundamental nature of teaching practice (Strielkowski et al., 2025). Eﬀective AI literacy development requires simultaneous attention to technological understanding, evaluative skills, practical application, and ethical considerations (Ng et al., 2023; Pinski & Benlian, 2024), while the rapid iteration of AI technologies demands continuous professional learning (Su et al., 2023).

This context highlights the critical role of organizational support in the development of teachers’ AI literacy. In response to the growing diﬀusion of AI technologies and the urgent demands of educational digital transformation, school organizations must provide systematic support to ensure that teachers are able to respond eﬀectively to the educational challenges of the intelligent age.

2.3. Research hypotheses

2.3.1. The eﬀect of organizational support on teachers’ AI literacy Perceived organizational support (POS), deﬁned as individuals’ overall perception that the organization values their contributions and cares about their well-being, is reﬂected in educational settings through the resource support, value recognition, beneﬁt-related care, and professional empowerment that schools provide to teachers (Rhoades & Eisenberger, 2002). Prior research has shown that high levels of organizational support can foster a positive instructional ecology by providing explicit resources, such as technical training and equipment provision, as well as implicit support, such as cultivating a culture that encourages innovation, thereby strengthening teachers’ conﬁdence in using intelligent technologies to improve instruction (Shen et al., 2024). Particularly in the context of rapid AI development, organizational support provides teachers with the necessary conditions and motivational backing to access, learn, and apply AI tools. According to the TOE framework, when teachers strongly perceive organizational support, they are more likely to engage in activities that enhance their competence, strive to meet organizational expectations, and realize their self-worth (Chiu et al., 2024b). Empirical studies further indicate that perceived organizational support serves as a key mediating mechanism through which leadership behaviors are translated into positive professional outcomes (Sluss et al., 2008). H1: Organizational support has a signiﬁcant positive eﬀect on teachers’ AI literacy.

2.3.2. The mediating role of innovation capability Innovation capability refers to teachers’ core ability to identify problems, generate creative ideas, and implement novel solutions in instructional practice, encompassing innovative thinking, practical transformation, and adaptability (Lin & Liu, 2025; Qin et al., 2025). Organizational support can eﬀectively stimulate teachers’ creative exploratory behavior by providing professional development resources, such as interdisciplinary collaboration platforms and technological experimentation spaces, as well as innovationoriented incentive mechanisms, such as incorporating AI application into performance evaluation (Zargar et al., 2025). Research has shown that, in educational contexts shaped by the rapid development of AI, teachers with stronger innovation capability are more likely to move beyond traditional instructional frameworks and proactively explore innovative applications of AI in education (Chen et al., 2020). For example, they may use intelligent learning analytics tools to design personalized learning pathways or employ generative AI to create interactive instructional content. This innovation-oriented process of practice not only deepens teachers’ understanding of the functions and potential of AI technologies but also promotes the comprehensive development of their AI literacy through an iterative cycle of technological exploration, pedagogical innovation, and reﬂective improvement (Tang et al., 2023).

Furthermore, teachers with stronger innovation capability demonstrate greater adaptability in response to evolving AI technologies, enabling them to integrate tools creatively into instructional design (Dilek et al., 2025; Mazman Akar, 2019; Scherer et al., 2019). Innovation capability thus serves as a critical bridge linking organizational support and AI literacy by transforming external resources into teachers’ intrinsic motivation to learn and apply AI technologies. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H2: Innovation capability mediates the relationship between organizational support and teachers’ AI literacy.

2.3.3. The mediating role of technology acceptance Technology acceptance refers to teachers’ psychological level of acceptance of AI technologies and is driven by perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). Recent research has shown that teachers’ digital literacy can signiﬁcantly inﬂuence their willingness to use AI-based instructional tools through pathways such as perceived usefulness, behavioral attitudes, and subjective norms (Du, 2025). Organizational support enhances teachers’ recognition of the educational value of new technologies, such as precise learning analytics and personalized feedback, through systematic training, while also lowering barriers to use by simplifying technological procedures. Research further indicates that the widespread application of AI has brought transformative changes to education, from personalized learning to intelligent assessment, all of which require teachers to possess an adequate level of technology acceptance (Chen et al., 2020). Teachers with higher levels of technology acceptance are more likely to embed AI tools into instructional design and assessment, thereby accumulating practical experience and developing a more sophisticated understanding of AI technologies (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Dilek et al., 2025).

Moreover, teachers’ technology acceptance directly aﬀects the eﬀectiveness of their AI-supported instructional practices and their capacity for continuous improvement (Long & Magerko, 2020; Tang et al., 2023). Therefore, technology acceptance may serve as a key psychological pathway through which organizational support is translated into AI literacy. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H3: Technology acceptance mediates the relationship between organizational support and teachers’ AI literacy.

Organizational support empowers teachers’ innovation capability by providing resources and incentive mechanisms (Hammond et al., 2009), while enhanced innovation capability further promotes deeper understanding and acceptance of AI technologies, facilitating teachers’ transition from “passive learning” to “active innovation” (Chen et al., 2020), as shown in Figure 2. This chain pathway of “innovation-driven technological internalization” enables multidimensional alignment among resource allocation, capacity building, and psychological acceptance, reﬂecting the developmental trajectory from basic tool use to deep integration that characterizes AI adoption in education (Dilek et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2023).

3. Method

3.1. Data source

The research team conducted a large-scale survey of primary and secondary school teachers across all 16 districts of Shanghai to systematically examine the current status of teachers’ organizational support, AI literacy, and related factors. A total of 11,581 questionnaires were collected through the Wenjuanxing platform. After a rigorous quality-control procedure, including the removal of questionnaires containing logical inconsistencies, excessive missing data, and highly repetitive response patterns, 10,683 valid responses were retained, yielding an eﬀective response rate of 92.25%.

The survey employed a stratiﬁed random sampling method. First, the 16 districts of Shanghai were divided into three tiers according to their level of economic development: central urban districts (Huangpu, Jing’an, Xuhui, and Changning; four districts), inner suburban districts (including Minhang, Baoshan, Jiading, and Pudong; eight districts), and outer suburban districts (Jinshan, Chongming, Fengxian, and Qingpu; four districts), thereby ensuring that the sample covered areas with diﬀerent levels of development. Within each district, a second stage of stratiﬁcation was conducted by school type (public vs. private) and educational level (primary school, junior secondary school, and senior secondary school), after which teachers were randomly selected from each school to participate in the survey. To ensure data quality, the research team established strict quality-control criteria, including a minimum completion time of 8 min, logical consistency checks across related items, and outlier screening, with questionnaires excluded when the proportion of consecutively selecting the same option was excessively high.

3.2. Variable selection

3.2.1. Organizational support This study adopted the classic Perceived Organizational Support (POS) scale developed by Eisenberger et al. (1986), drawing on its revised version for the Chinese organizational context and adapting the wording of the items to reﬂect the professional characteristics of teachers. Speciﬁcally, the adaptation involved four steps. First, linguistic localization was conducted through a translation–back-translation procedure, in which two doctoral students in education independently translated the items and cross-checked the results to ensure semantic accuracy. Second, contextualization was implemented by replacing generic organizational expressions in the original scale with education-speciﬁc terminology; for example, “organization” was uniformly changed to “school,” and “job performance” was revised to “teaching performance.” Third, item screening was carried out to remove statements irrelevant to the teaching profession while retaining 15 core items in light of the work characteristics of Chinese teachers. Fourth, some items were culturally reﬁned; for instance, “management” was replaced with “school leadership” to better align with the cultural context of Chinese education. These adaptations were necessary because the original scale developed by Eisenberger et al. (1986) was designed for general organizational settings and did not fully capture the distinctive features of organizational support for teachers in schools, including instructional leadership, the provision of teaching resources, and support from professional learning communities. A pilot test involving 120 teachers from two schools in Shanghai conﬁrmed that the adapted items demonstrated better face validity and comprehensibility than a direct translation of the original version.

The scale consisted of 15 items (A1–A15) covering ﬁve dimensions: work support, value recognition, beneﬁt-related care, interpersonal coordination, and professional development. All items were measured on a ﬁve-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), with higher total scores indicating stronger perceived organizational support. All items were positively keyed. Reliability and validity tests showed that the Cronbach’s α coeﬃcients for the ﬁve dimensions were 0.949, 0.962, 0.965, 0.970, and 0.957, respectively. The conﬁrmatory factor analysis showed a good model ﬁt (GFI = 0.932, RMSEA = 0.079, CFI = 0.977, NFI = 0.976), indicating strong internal consistency and structural validity.

Innovation capability The innovation capability scale was adapted from the teacher innovation capability scale proposed by Sharma and Sharma (2018) to reﬂect the characteristics of teachers’ instructional practice. The revision process involved four steps. First, translation and cultural adaptation were conducted through a standard procedure of forward translation, back translation, and expert review to ensure the accuracy and comprehensibility of the items in the Chinese context. Fourth, linguistic localization was applied to make the wording more natural and ﬂuent in accordance with Chinese teachers’ expression habits. These revisions were necessary because the original scale was designed for general organizational settings and did not fully capture the distinctive features of instructional innovation in K–12 education. The reduction from 12 to 9 items was guided by item – total correlations and factor loadings from an exploratory factor analysis based on a pilot sample of 150 participants, as well as substantive judgments regarding relevance to the Chinese teaching context.

The scale comprised 9 items (C1–C9) across three dimensions: innovative thinking (e.g. “I often think about how to make teaching more creative”), innovative practice (e.g. “I try to use new technologies to support teaching”), and innovative disposition (e.g. “I value continuous learning and improvement”). All items were measured on a ﬁve-point Likert scale. Reliability testing showed that the Cronbach’s α coeﬃcients for the three dimensions were 0.975, 0.947, and 0.975, respectively. The conﬁrmatory factor analysis yielded excellent ﬁt indices (GFI = 0.946, RMR = 0.018, CFI = 0.983, NFI = 0.983), providing strong support for the reliability and validity of the scale.

3.3. Data analysis

This study used SPSS 26.0 and Amos 26.0 for data analysis. SPSS 26.0 was used for data preprocessing, including reliability analysis (Cronbach’s α), descriptive statistics, and Pearson correlation analysis. Amos 26.0 was used for conﬁrmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modeling (SEM) with maximum likelihood (ML) estimation to examine the direct and mediating pathways. The bias-corrected percentile Bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples was employed to test mediating eﬀects, with a 95% conﬁdence interval (CI) not including zero taken as evidence of signiﬁcance. Model ﬁt was evaluated using indices including CFI, NFI, and RMSEA.

4. Results

4.1. Common method bias

To control for the potential inﬂuence of common method bias on the study ﬁndings, Harman’s single-factor test was applied to the measurement items for organizational support, innovation capability, technology acceptance, and AI literacy. The results showed that ﬁve latent factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, and the ﬁrst factor accounted for 37.3% of the total variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%. This result indicates that the data were not substantially aﬀected by common method bias and that the ﬁndings are therefore credible.

4.2. Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis

The overall mean score for teachers’ AI literacy was 3.656 (SD = 0.827). Signiﬁcant diﬀerences were observed across the subdimensions. AI ethics (M = 4.091) and AI thinking (M = 3.881) showed the highest levels of performance. Teachers’ professional learning (M = 3.543) and AI tools (M = 3.513) were at a moderate level. By contrast, AI pedagogy (M = 3.497) and AI foundations (M = 3.409) were relatively weak, suggesting that technological application capability and the integration of pedagogy remain the most salient shortcomings, as shown in Table 1.

These ﬁndings reveal a structural imbalance in the development of teachers’ AI literacy and carry important theoretical implications. The relatively weak performance in AI foundations (M = 3.409) suggests that teachers have an insuﬃcient understanding of the principles and functions of AI technologies, which directly constrains their ability to use AI tools ﬂexibly in instructional practice and may result in a pattern of application characterized by procedural use without deeper conceptual understanding. This ﬁnding is consistent with the technological context dimension of the Technology–Organization–Environment (TOE) framework, which posits that technological complexity signiﬁcantly aﬀects adoption outcomes (Tornatzky et al., 1990). Without suﬃcient foundational knowledge, teachers cannot eﬀectively evaluate the suitability and limitations of diﬀerent AI tools in speciﬁc instructional contexts, causing their technology acceptance to remain at the level of superﬁcial compliance rather than deep integration.

The relatively lagging performance in AI pedagogy (M = 3.497) further indicates that teachers have not yet developed the capacity to integrate AI technologies organically with pedagogical approaches, which may lead to surface-level and fragmented applications of technology. This gap in pedagogical integration reﬂects the environmental context dimension of the TOE framework, speciﬁcally the lack of innovation-supportive mechanisms that would enable teachers to experiment with AI-enhanced instructional design in authentic teaching contexts (Kundu & Roy, 2023). These two weaknesses reinforce each other. Limited AI foundational knowledge constrains teachers’ ability to envision innovative pedagogical applications, whereas weak AI pedagogy reduces opportunities to deepen foundational understanding through practice. Together, they constitute a major bottleneck in the improvement of teachers’ AI literacy.

The chain mediation framework proposed in this study – organizational support → innovation capability → technology acceptance → AI literacy – provides a systematic pathway for addressing this structural imbalance. Through the four-level collaborative framework, organizational support can strengthen AI foundational knowledge via training platforms, stimulate innovation capability through collaborative research and interdisciplinary experiments, and gradually enhance technology acceptance, thereby promoting the coordinated development of both AI foundational and pedagogical competence. This chain mediation mechanism thus links the empirical ﬁndings to a practical intervention framework.

The correlation analysis among the variables (Table 2) showed that organizational support was signiﬁ- cantly and positively correlated with innovation capability (r = 0.636, p < 0.001), technology acceptance (r = 0.558, p < 0.001), and AI literacy (r = 0.605, p < 0.001). Innovation capability was also highly correlated with technology acceptance (r = 0.677, p < 0.001) and AI literacy (r = 0.720, p < 0.001). The correlation coeﬃ- cient between technology acceptance and AI literacy reached 0.850 (p < 0.001), indicating a strong synergistic relationship among these three factors.

4.3. Testing of the chain mediation model

Based on the research hypotheses, this study constructed a structural equation model with organizational support as the independent variable, teachers’ AI literacy as the dependent variable, and innovation capability and technology acceptance as the chain mediators, in order to systematically examine the mechanism linking these variables. The model ﬁt indices were as follows: CFI = 0.896, NFI = 0.896, RMR = 0.063, SRMR = 0.088, PGFI = 0.547, PNFI = 0.732, and PCFI = 0.732. Although the CFI and NFI values were slightly below the conventional threshold of 0.90, these indices are known to be sensitive to model complexity under largesample conditions (N = 10,683) and may therefore underestimate model ﬁt to some extent (Cheung & Rensvold, 2002). Both the RMR and SRMR were below the criterion of 0.10, and the parsimony-adjusted ﬁt indices (PGFI, PNFI, and PCFI) all exceeded 0.50, indicating that the model achieved an acceptable ﬁt while maintaining parsimony. Taken together, the overall model ﬁt was acceptable.

The path analysis results (Figure 3) showed the following standardized path coeﬃcients. First, organizational support signiﬁcantly and positively predicted innovation capability (β = 0.666, p < 0.001), technology acceptance (β = 0.192, p < 0.001), and AI literacy (β = 0.043, p < 0.001), conﬁrming its signiﬁcant predictive role. Second, innovation capability exerted cross-level transmission eﬀects on both technology acceptance (β = 0.605, p < 0.001) and AI literacy (β = 0.029, p < 0.01). Third, the standardized path coeﬃcient from technology acceptance to AI literacy reached 0.870 (p < 0.001), highlighting its reinforcing role as the core mechanism of transformation. 5. Discussion and conclusion

Based on the structural equation modeling analysis, this study elucidates the pathways and underlying mechanisms through which organizational support enhances teachers’ AI literacy. Drawing on empirical data from 10,683 teachers in Shanghai, the study conﬁrms four key ﬁndings and further clariﬁes their deeper implications for the digital transformation of education.

5.1. Core ﬁndings: the multifaceted mechanism of organizational support

The ﬁndings show that organizational support has a signiﬁcant direct eﬀect on teachers’ AI literacy (0.110, p < 0.001), accounting for 18.2% of the total eﬀect. This indicates that the provision of resources, value

Innovation capability also plays a signiﬁcant mediating role in the relationship between organizational support and AI literacy, with an indirect eﬀect of 0.136, accounting for 22.4% of the total eﬀect. By providing professional platforms, incentive mechanisms, and spaces for innovation, organizational support eﬀectively cultivates teachers’ innovative thinking and practical capability, thereby promoting their integration of AI technologies into instructional design (Lin, 2021). This pathway underscores the central importance of capacity building in technology use, suggesting that merely having access to technological tools is not suﬃcient; teachers must also develop the capability to use these tools for pedagogical innovation (Hulpia et al., 2011).

Technology acceptance likewise serves a signiﬁcant mediating function (indirect eﬀect: 0.138, 22.8% of total eﬀect), as organizational support enhances teachers’ perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (Scherer et al., 2021). Most importantly, innovation capability and technology acceptance jointly constitute the strongest chain mediation pathway (eﬀect: 0.222, 36.6% of total eﬀect), forming a progressive pathway of “innovation-driven development → technological internalization” (Chen et al., 2020; Dilek et al., 2025; Hammond et al., 2009; Tang et al., 2023). This ﬁnding highlights the critical value of the synergy between external support and teachers’ endogenous factors in technology adoption.

5.2. The four-level collaborative framework and the mechanism for advancing the digital transformation of education

Based on the empirical ﬁndings, this study proposes a four-level collaborative framework – comprising institutional support, resource coordination, mechanism innovation, and agent empowerment – as a practiceoriented support system for advancing the comprehensive digital transformation of education (see Figure 4).

This framework moves beyond a narrow focus on individual teachers’ competency development and positions organizational support as a systemic lever for institutional change. The level of institutional support provides the policy environment and normative foundation for the intelligent development of the education system, including the establishment of AI education standards, the formulation of technology integration policies, and the creation of accountability mechanisms that align institutional incentives with the goals of digital transformation (Chiu, 2024; Dilek et al., 2025; Ng et al., 2023).

The level of resource coordination provides material and soft-resource support for the intelligent transformation of the education system by prioritizing the development of curricular resources, technical support infrastructure, and professional learning communities, thereby addressing the documented imbalance of emphasizing hardware while neglecting content (Abbasi et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2024). The level of mechanism innovation stimulates stakeholders’ intrinsic motivation to participate in digital transformation by establishing incentive and evaluation systems that reward pedagogical innovation and by creating collaborative governance mechanisms that promote knowledge sharing across schools and disciplines (Lin & Liu, 2025; Qin et al., 2025). The level of agent empowerment systematically enhances teachers’ AI literacy through the chain mechanism of “innovation capability → technology acceptance,” while also fostering students’ digital competence and improving the digital adaptability and innovative capacity of all participants in the educational system (Scherer et al., 2019; Zhang & Zhang, 2024).

Importantly, this framework aligns with emerging global discourse on the digital transformation of education, which emphasizes that sustainable technology integration requires simultaneous change at the policy, institutional, pedagogical, and individual levels rather than fragmented interventions (Strielkowski et al., 2025). The four-level collaborative framework advances educational digital transformation through three core mechanisms. First, the vertical penetration mechanism ensures the eﬀective transmission and implementation of policy intentions (Shen et al., 2024). Second, the horizontal coordination mechanism creates a uniﬁed force for digital transformation (Qin et al., 2025). Third, the spiral enhancement mechanism enables cyclical feedback and iterative optimization, thereby forming a spiral trajectory for the digital transformation of education (Strielkowski et al., 2025).

This framework drives the digital transformation of education as a multilayered process that extends beyond the improvement of teachers’ AI literacy alone. At the micro level, the chain mediation pathway enables a shift from traditional knowledge transmission to intelligent and personalized teaching (Scherer et al., 2019; Zhang & Zhang, 2024). At the meso level, the framework promotes data-driven educational management, as organizations that support innovation capability simultaneously build institutional capacity for evidence-based governance (Eryilmaz et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2024). At the macro level, the framework facilitates the formation of a digital education ecosystem in which government, schools, the technology industry, and teachers converge toward shared transformation goals (Abbasi et al., 2025; Saeedikiya et al., 2025; Strielkowski et al., 2025). By embedding technological change within comprehensive reforms involving institutions, resources, mechanisms, and human agency, this framework achieves an integration of technologydriven change with the intrinsic logic of educational development (Dilek et al., 2025; Lin & Liu, 2025; Ng et al., 2023). The ﬁnding that the chain mediation pathway accounts for 36.6% of the total eﬀect provides quantitative support for the argument that digital transformation requires coordinated organizational, cognitive, and psychological mechanisms rather than isolated technological provision.

In conclusion, this study reveals the full mechanism through which organizational support enhances teachers’ AI literacy via direct eﬀects (18.2%) and indirect eﬀects (81.8%), and constructs a four-level collaborative framework that enables an organic linkage between the enhancement of teachers’ literacy at the micro level and the transformation of the education system at the macro level through vertical penetration, horizontal coordination, and spiral enhancement mechanisms.

6. Theoretical and practical implications

6.1. Theoretical implications

This study contributes to the theoretical literature on the development of teachers’ AI literacy in three major ways. First, it extends research on the antecedents of teachers’ AI literacy. Existing studies have focused primarily on teachers’ individual characteristics while paying insuﬃcient attention to organizational environmental factors. By incorporating organizational support into the analytical framework, this study veriﬁes

Second, this study reveals the mediating mechanisms through which organizational support inﬂuences teachers’ AI literacy. By introducing innovation capability and technology acceptance as mediating variables, the study not only conﬁrms their respective independent mediating eﬀects but also identiﬁes, for the ﬁrst time, the chain mediating eﬀect of “innovation capability → technology acceptance.” This ﬁnding establishes a theoretical model in which organizational factors promote teachers’ technological literacy through a pathway of “capacity building → psychological acceptance,” thereby oﬀering a new perspective for understanding the interactive mechanism between organizational environments and teachers’ individual characteristics.

Third, this study develops an integrated theoretical framework for the development of teachers’ AI literacy. Speciﬁcally, it advances a multilayered developmental model of “organizational resources → teacher capability → psychological acceptance → literacy performance,” providing teacher education theory with a new perspective on the coordinated development of external support and internal motivation. This theoretical framework helps move beyond the binary divide between “individual-centered” and “organization-centered” approaches in teacher development research and contributes to a more integrated theory of teachers’ professional development.

6.2. Practical implications

At the practical level, this study oﬀers three speciﬁc recommendations for education administrators, school leaders, and policymakers. First, the ﬁndings underscore the critical role of organizational support in fostering teachers’ AI literacy. Accordingly, education administrators are advised to move beyond a narrow focus on hardware provision and instead build a comprehensive support system that encompasses technological resources, professional training, incentive mechanisms, and an innovation-supportive culture.

Second, the mediating eﬀects of innovation capability and technology acceptance account for 22.4% and 22.8% of the total eﬀect, respectively, indicating the critical role of teachers’ endogenous factors in translating organizational support into literacy outcomes. Schools should therefore establish a dual-track teacher development system. On the one hand, they should cultivate teachers’ innovation capability through project-based learning and interdisciplinary collaboration. On the other hand, they should enhance teachers’ technology acceptance through demonstration lessons and the sharing of successful cases. Given that the chain mediating eﬀect of “innovation capability → technology acceptance” accounts for 36.6% of the total eﬀect, schools should give priority to fostering innovation capability.

Third, this study provides a practical framework for building a multi-stakeholder system to support the development of teachers’ AI literacy. Based on the four-level structure of institutional support, resource coordination, mechanism innovation, and agent empowerment, government agencies need to strengthen policy guidance and resource investment; schools need to optimize their internal incentive and support mechanisms; and teachers need to enhance their innovative awareness and self-directed learning capacity. Such a multilayered support system can generate synergistic eﬀects and provide a systematic solution for the sustainable development of teachers’ AI literacy.

7. Limitations and future research

This study has four main limitations, each of which points to directions for future research. First, the study adopted a cross-sectional design and therefore can capture only correlational relationships among variables rather than establish causality. Future studies could employ experimental or longitudinal designs to track changes in teachers’ AI literacy by intervening in levels of organizational support, thereby providing a more rigorous test of causal relationships.

Second, this study relied exclusively on questionnaire data, which may raise concerns regarding common method bias. Although this risk was addressed through Harman’s single-factor test, with the ﬁrst factor accounting for 37.3% of the variance, below the 40% threshold, future research should nevertheless consider

Third, although the sample size was relatively large (N = 10,683), the participants were limited to teachers in Shanghai, which may restrict regional representativeness. Given regional disparities across China in educational resources, levels of informatization, and support for teachers’ professional development, future research should expand the sampling frame to include teachers from central and western regions as well as rural areas. Comparative analyses across regions would help clarify how diﬀerent organizational support mechanisms inﬂuence teachers’ AI literacy and would strengthen the external validity of the ﬁndings.

Finally, this study did not take into account potential moderating variables such as teachers’ individual levels of technological competence and disciplinary backgrounds. Future research could examine the moderating roles of individual diﬀerences, such as technological proﬁciency and subject characteristics, as well as structural factors, such as policy environments and resource investment, in the pathways through which organizational support inﬂuences teachers’ AI literacy. This would enable the development of a more comprehensive theoretical model. In addition, given the rapid evolution of AI technologies, future studies could incorporate related variables such as digital literacy and computational thinking into the analytical framework in order to explore the broader mechanisms underlying the development of teachers’ digital competence.

In sum, future research should further advance the theoretical and practical study of teachers’ AI literacy development by expanding regional coverage, enriching research methods, reﬁning variable systems, and deepening comparative analysis, thereby providing more scientiﬁc and systematic support for the digital transformation of education.
