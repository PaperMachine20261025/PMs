How do artificial intelligence literacy constructs work—based on a survey of university non‑expert students

Weikang Lu1   · Chenghua Lin2

Received: 4 August 2024 / Accepted: 2 January 2025 © The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2025

Abstract Artificial intelligence is increasingly integrated into daily life, and modern educated individuals should have the ability to use AI tools correctly to improve work, study, and life efficiency. In this context, artificial intelligence literacy has been proposed. Due to the lack of consensus on the constructs of artificial intelligence literacy, this study used the scoping review to summarize the AI literacy constructs, including recognize AI, know AI, AI ethics, AI empowerment, AI self-competence and apply AI. In order to further explore the relationship between these six constructs, this study distributed an artificial intelligence literacy questionnaire to 276 non-expert university students (referring to students who have not received formal artificial intelligence education) and used structural equation modeling to verify the hypothesis. Research has found that recognize AI, know AI, AI ethics, AI empowerment, and AI self-competence all have significant positive predictive effects on apply AI. Know AI also has a significant positive predictive effect on AI ethics, AI empowerment, and AI self-competence. AI ethics, AI empowerment, and AI self-competence play a mediating role in the relationship between know AI and apply AI. The findings further improve the constructs exploration of artificial intelligence literacy in current research and provide some inspiration for teaching practice.

1  Introduction

With the advancement of computer science and technology, artificial intelligence is increasingly integrated into various aspects of human life. Human life, work, education, entertainment, etc. are all influenced by artificial intelligence, but different people have different perceptions of the impact of artificial intelligence, which leads to people unconsciously using artificial intelligence tools (Davenport & Ronanki, 2018). Indeed, artificial intelligence is reshaping human lifestyle habits, and some positive impacts are subtly liberating human labor, such as autonomous driving, intelligent translation, and generative image software. However, the negative impacts brought by artificial intelligence are often unconscious, such as personal data privacy, the spread of misinformation, and biases in AI results (Gedrimiene et  al., 2023; Kong et  al., 2021; Wang & Siau, 2019). In order to avoid the negative impact of the misuse of artificial intelligence, it is necessary to equip all educated individuals with basic knowledge, skills, and correct values of artificial intelligence to guide them in using AI tools correctly in their work and life. In this situation, the emphasis on artificial intelligence knowledge, abilities, and values has sparked the concept of artificial intelligence literacy. Long and Magerko (2020) proposed a widely accepted and used definition of artificial intelligence literacy, which is “a set of competences that enable individuals to critically evaluate AI tools, communicate and collaborate effectively with AI, and use AI as an online, home, and workplace tool”.

The impact of artificial intelligence on the field of education is particularly evident, with varying degrees of intervention in educational content, form, means, and evaluation. Therefore, artificial intelligence research in the field of education has become a hot topic. There have been measures to improve artificial intelligence literacy in the field of education, and various teaching forms and structures have been used in courses or projects that cultivate artificial intelligence literacy in higher education. In flipped classrooms, students are required to complete content related to artificial intelligence outside of class using books or online materials as support, and then engage in projects or discussions in class about the impact of their newly acquired knowledge (Bishop & Verleger, 2013). Some courses also provide programming environments, some taught in computer labs (Vazhayil et al., 2019), and some taught in sandbox programming borders of the digital world (Rodríguez et al., 2020). At a broader national and regional level, there are also many projects dedicated to developing students’ artificial intelligence literacy, such as The AI4K12 Initiative and The Big Ideas in AI (Touretzky et al., 2022), The Machine Learning Education Framework (Lao, 2020), Competencies and Design considerations for AI literacy (Long & Magerko ,2020), The Holistic Approach to the Design of AI Education for K-12 schools (Chiu,2021), UNESCO’s map of domains and subdomains for AI education (UNESCO,2022). These projects are based on different experiences and philosophical foundations. The AI4K12 Initiative and The Big Ideas in AI are based on the work of artificial intelligence education experts. The Machine Learning Education Framework is a curriculum designed for people interested in artificial intelligence. Competencies

and design considerations for AI literacy are based on a comprehensive literature review. The Holistic Approach to the Design of AI Education for K-12 schools is based on interviews with AI teachers at different levels in schools.

In recent years, many scholars have defined and summarized the constructs of artificial intelligence literacy, which can be divided into three constructs, four constructs, five constructs, and constructs greater than five based on the number of constructs. For example, scholars who divide artificial intelligence literacy into three constructs include the following. Matthias et al. (2023) divided AI literacy into technical understanding, critical evaluation, and practical application. Lu et al. (2024) divided AI literacy into AI mechanisms, AI ethics, and AI capabilities. Kong et al. (2023) divided AI literacy into cognition (conceptual understanding and skill development), emotion (empowerment) and socio-cultural (morality). Ning and James (2023) divided AI literacy into AI capability, AI problem solving, and social and cultural background. Scholars who divide AI literacy into four constructs include the following. Siu et al. (2024) divided AI literacy into cognition, emotion, society, and metacognition. Nils et al. (2024) divided AI literacy into knowledge/cognition, skills/behavior, attitudes/values, and ethics. AI-Chu et  al. (2024) divided AI literacy into knowing and understanding AI, using AI applications, evaluating AI applications, and AI ethics. Scholars who divide AI literacy into five and more than five constructs include the following. Thomas et al. (2024) divided AI literacy into technology, influence, ethics, collaboration and self-reflection. Musa et al. (2024) divided AI literacy into using and applying AI, understanding and knowing AI, detecting AI, AI ethics, creating AI, AI problem-solving, AI persuasion literacy and AI emotion regulation. Laura et al. (2024) divided AI literacy into knowledge, assessment, collaboration, contextualization, autonomy and ethics. Just as the philosophical and experiential foundations of practice vary, to our knowledge, there is no comprehensive framework for artificial intelligence literacy in literature (Siu et al., 2024), and, therefore, no research has explored the relationship between different constructs of artificial intelligence literacy. But these issues are crucial for guiding practice. In order to fill the gap in existing research, this study focuses on the following two questions: (1) What are the comprehensive constructs of artificial intelligence literacy? (2) What is the correlation between these constructs? To address these two issues, this study adopts a mixed research approach. The first question uses a scoping review to synthesize a comprehensive construct of artificial intelligence literacy from existing literature. The second question adopts quantitative research and uses structural equation modeling to explore the relationship between the constructs of artificial intelligence literacy.

The definition of AI literacy varies for different groups, mainly reflected in two aspects. One is to focus on the application and innovation of AI technology, and more on experts with computer or engineering backgrounds, emphasizing the understanding of the technology and concepts behind AI products and services, rather than just learning how to use certain technologies or current applications. According to Liu and Xie (2021), AI literacy is a comprehensive quality that includes digital literacy, computational thinking, and programming skills; Kandlhofe et al. (2016), starting from the concept of AI, believe that the definition of AI literacy includes the following main themes of AI and computer science: automata, intelligent agents,

graphs and data structures, computer science fundamentals, machine learning, etc. Secondly, it represents the majority of scholars who, from the perspective of consumers, focus on the AI capabilities that the general public should possess, paying attention to learners without a computer science background (“non-experts”). They believe that AI literacy is the development and extension of various types of “literacy”, especially information literacy and digital literacy. It is believed that it has little to do with programming or algorithm complexity and focuses more on general knowledge of AI (Ng et al.,2021a; Laupichler et al.,2022; Kong et al., 2021). For expert students, learning AI professional knowledge courses is to cultivate their AI literacy. But for non-expert students, there is currently no specific and extensive curriculum structure to support their development of AI literacy. So non-expert student’s AI literacy is more important (Matthias et al., 2023). Based on this, the focus of this study is on non-expert university students who have not received specialized artificial intelligence education.

2  Literature review

2.1  AI literacy frameworks and constructs

Long and Magerko (2020) proposed a broad framework for artificial intelligence literacy. They identified 17 abilities that people should learn, divided into five main questions: What is AI? What can AI do? How does AI works? How should AI be sued? How do people perceive AI? This framework defines the structure of artificial intelligence literacy from the perspectives of knowledge construct (What is AI? What can AI do? ), skill construct (How does AI work? How do people perceive AI? ) and ethical construct (How should AI be used? ). Kong and Zhang (2021) also proposed a broad framework for artificial intelligence literacy, which includes three constructs: cognitive, affective, and sociocultural. The cognitive construct aims to impart fundamental concepts of artificial intelligence and equip learners with the skills to apply them to understanding and evaluating the real world. The affective construct focuses on empowerment, enabling individuals to seamlessly collaborate with artificial intelligence in their daily activities. In addition, efforts should be made at the sociocultural level to promote the ethical use of artificial intelligence for global sustainable development. Compared to the frameworks of Long and Magerko (2020), this framework includes an affective construct, in addition to knowledge, skills, and ethical constructs. Another commonly used framework is The Five Big Ideas, which encompasses the following concepts: Perception, that is, how artificial intelligence systems “see” or “hear” the world around them; Representation and reasoning, that is, how computers transform, organize, and analyze data from the world; Learning, that is, how machines learn and how design and train models; Natural interaction, that is, how artificial intelligence systems communicate and interact with humans; And social impacts, including the potential benefits and harms of artificial intelligence to society (Touretzky et al., 2019). This framework focuses more on learning artificial intelligence knowledge, skills, and ethical aspects. Ng et al. (2021a, b) classified artificial intelligence abilities into four cognitive domains

based on literature and Bloom’s learning objective classification method, from low to high thinking skills: understanding and comprehension, use and application, evaluation and creation, and ethics. Wang et al. (2023) developed an artificial intelligence literacy assessment framework based on the information literacy assessment framework, which includes four constructs: artificial intelligence awareness, intelligent technology application, innovative thinking, and intelligent social responsibility. So far, it is clear that various AI literacy frameworks commonly involve at least three aspects: knowledge, skills, and ethics. Other frameworks have expanded in these three dimensions.

Siu et  al. (2024) designed a four-dimensional framework for AI literacy for high school students, including cognitive (understanding of AI concepts), affective (psychological readiness to use AI), metacognitive (use of AI concepts for problem solving), and social (ethics of problem solving with AI). The metacognitive dimension is defined here as the four stages of using artificial intelligence to solve problems. Therefore, although labeled as the metacognitive dimension in this AI literacy framework, it is essentially linked to the skill dimension. Siu et al. (2023) also refined AI empowerment into four parts: AI self-efficacy, creative self-efficacy, meaningfulness, and impact. AI self-efficacy refers to the confidence of educated citizens in successfully using artificial intelligence to solve real-life problems (Bandura, 1982), creative self-efficacy refers to the confidence in their own creativity (Tierney & Farmer, 2011), meaningfulness refers to the perception of the personal significance of using artificial intelligence to solve problems by educated citizens (Thomas & Velthouse, 1990), and impact refers to their views on how using artificial intelligence to solve problems affects society (Frymier et al., 1996). The individuals’ attitudes are also an important construct of artificial intelligence literacy. Musa et al. (2024) synthesized the frameworks of others to design an artificial intelligence literacy framework that includes eight dimensions: use and apply artificial intelligence, know and understand artificial intelligence, detect AI, AI ethics, create artificial intelligence, AI problem solving, AI persuasion literacy, and AI emotion regulation. Among them, know and understand artificial intelligence, detect artificial intelligence belong to knowledge construct, use and apply artificial intelligence, create artificial intelligence, AI problem solving belong to skill construct, AI ethics belong to ethical construct, AI persuasion literacy, and AI emotion regulation belong to affective construct. A relatively comprehensive artificial intelligence literacy framework currently comes from Astrid et al. (2023), whose contribution is mainly to incorporate a wide range of psychological factors, such as AI self-efficacy as well as AI self-competence. Among them, AI self-efficacy is similar to the affective dimension proposed by Siu et al. (2024), and AI self-competence is consistent with Musa’s et al. (2024) AI persuasion literacy and AI emotion regulation.

It is worth noting that some artificial intelligence frameworks consider create AI as an important component, with corresponding test items such as “I can design new AI application tools”, “I can write new applications in the field of artificial intelligence”, “I can develop new artificial intelligence applications”, “I can choose useful tools to program artificial intelligence”, etc. (Ng et al., 2022a). However, creating AI requires a high level of personal artificial intelligence knowledge and skills, and generally requires reaching the level of an expert. Perhaps the ‘create AI’ dimension

is more suitable for AI experts rather than non-expert AI users. There are two reasons. Firstly, according to the definition of artificial intelligence literacy, the purpose of developing AI literacy is to correctly use AI tools in different scenarios to assist in life, learning, and work, which is far from creating AI. Secondly, the prerequisite for creating AI is to have expert level knowledge and skills, but even in the AI literacy framework that includes the dimension of create AI, the knowledge and skill dimensions it contains have not reached the expert level, but only the level of daily use. Therefore, this study does not incorporate the creation of AI into the constructs of artificial intelligence literacy.

A review of AI literacy frameworks and constructs reveals that existing research has inconsistent definitions of it, and there is currently no holistic AI literacy framework that incorporates all known structures. Therefore, the first research question of this study is to obtain a relatively holistic AI literacy framework through literature review, attempting to unify the known AI literacy constructs.

2.2  AI literacy assessment

The assessment of artificial intelligence literacy is crucial for personnel selection, identification of skill and knowledge shortages, and evaluation of AI literacy intervention measures. Sorting out AI literacy assessments serves as the foundation for addressing the second research question of this study. At present, there are two types of assessments for artificial intelligence literacy: one is a small-scale assessment within a limited situational scope, usually to test the implementation effectiveness of designed artificial intelligence literacy courses. Another type of assessment is large-scale assessments based on psychology, education, and statistics, which have a wider range of contexts and objects of use. Below, we will elaborate on these two evaluation tools separately.

There are two forms of measurement used to test the effectiveness of artificial intelligence literacy, one is objective knowledge testing, as studied by Ali et  al. (2019), Kandlhofer et al. (2016), Rodríguez et al. (2021). The advantage of this evaluation form is that it can truly reflect the degree of students’ mastery of knowledge and has better testing quality, but the disadvantage is also obvious, that is, it generally needs to be combined with specific intervention courses and is not suitable for evaluation in other contexts. Another type is selfassessment, as studied by Chai et al. (2021), Dai et al. (2020), and Kim et al. (2021), which has the advantage of being easy to operate and the disadvantages mentioned above.

Another evaluation tool, designed on the basis of psychology, education, and statistics, boasts a broader range of usage scenarios and targets, while maintaining strong reliability and validity. Based on our limited knowledge, there are currently five published evaluation tools that belong to this category. The first one is the Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale for Medical Students (MAIRS- MS) developed by Karaca et al. (2021). The authors demonstrated the good reliability and validity of this assessment tool through exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis. This assessment tool divides Artificial Intelligence Readiness into four dimensions: Cognition, Ability, Vision, and Ethics. The second one is the artificial

intelligence literacy scale developed by Wang et al. (2023), which divides artificial intelligence literacy into four dimensions: Awareness, Usage, Evaluation, and Ethics. The scale has also been validated through factor analysis. The third one is a nonexpert AI literacy assessment tool developed by Pinski and Benlian (2023) for the workplace. But the second and third evaluation tools are not developed based on the conceptualization of artificial intelligence literacy, but based on other concepts. The fourth and fifth assessment tools are quite similar, developed by Musa et al. (2024) and Astrid et al. (2023) based on similar literature, respectively. These two assessment tools are more comprehensive compared to the previous three, as they include emotional attitude dimensions such as AI self-efficacy, AI Persuasion literacy, and AI emotion regulation.

Through a literature review of AI literacy measurement, it was found that there are already mature measurement methods for measuring students’ AI literacy in existing research. In addition, by comparing the advantages and disadvantages of two different measurement methods, it was found that the measurement methods based on psychology, statistics, and education are more convenient and scientific, which also provides a theoretical basis and tool reference for the measurement of AI literacy in this study.

Due to the opacity of technology, people may not be aware of the use of artificial intelligence. Know AI is the most fundamental level of artificial intelligence literacy, and educated citizens must have sufficient conceptual understanding of artificial intelligence. It includes the underlying logic of artificial intelligence algorithms such as supervised learning (linear regression and K-nearest neighbor algorithms), unsupervised learning (K-means clustering), and neural networks, as well as how to apply these algorithms to solve real-life problems (Chiu et al., 2022). Numerous studies have shown that understanding the conceptual knowledge of artificial intelligence is the foundation for using it. For example, Ai et al.‘s (2024) research suggests that as teachers go beyond understanding the conceptual knowledge of artificial intelligence, they will apply their knowledge to teaching practice and utilize its potential to enhance the teaching and learning experience. There are also some studies that focus on the relationship between knowledge, morality, and personal emotional attitudes. Musa et al. (2024) conducted a comprehensive analysis of the artificial intelligence literacy of 529 pre-service teachers at a university in Nigeria and found that a deep understanding of artificial intelligence can significantly predict positive outcomes in its use, detection, ethics, creation, and problem-solving. Based on this, the following assumptions are made:

H2: Know AI has a significant positive impact on Apply AI; H3: Know AI has a significant positive impact on AI Ethics; H4: Know AI has a significant positive impact on AI empowerment; H5: Know AI has a significant positive impact on AI self-competence.

AI ethics refers to the ethical and responsible use of artificial intelligence technology, which is an important construct that most AI literacy frameworks possess. When using artificial intelligence, human centered factors such as fairness, accountability, transparency, ethics, and safety must be given priority consideration (Ng et al., 2021b). In addition, it is necessary to identify and describe key ethical issues related to artificial intelligence, such as privacy, employment, misinformation, ethical decision-making, diversity, and bias (Long & Magerko, 2020). Having sound ethics can promote the use of artificial intelligence in a more effective and less harmful way. Meanwhile, many training programs have also enhanced the teaching of AI ethics knowledge, thereby improving students’ ability to use AI correctly (Nikou & Economides, 2017). Hua et al. (2024) found that the perception of K-12 teachers towards the use of artificial intelligence for social welfare is a direct determinant of their intention to use AI. AI literacy directly affects people’s views on the use of AI for social welfare, their self-efficacy in learning artificial intelligence, and their understanding of AI ethics, and indirectly affects their behavioral intentions. Based on this, the following assumptions are made:

H6: AI Ethics has a significant positive impact on Apply AI H7: AI Ethics mediates the relationship between Know AI and Apply AI.

AI empowerment is the psychological preparation for interaction between humans and artificial intelligence. It belongs to the emotional dimension and is also an important structure that is often overlooked by some AI literacy frameworks (Siu et al., 2024). AI empowerment enhances the confidence of educated citizens to apply artificial intelligence to solve problems. The sub-dimensions of AI empowerment have already been introduced in detail in the literature review section, and will not be repeated here. In a large number of studies on the willingness to use/accept technology, the impact of technology readiness (referring to the level of psychological preparation of users when adopting new technologies, similar to AI empowerment) on behavior occurrence and behavior intention has been discussed. For example, Nikou and Economides (2017) based on the TAM model, integrated factors such as computer anxiety, personal innovation ability, and perceived trust to study learners’ willingness to use mobile learning. As a core element of psychological preparation, empowerment enhances the confidence of educated citizens to apply artificial intelligence to solve problems (Kong et al., 2021). Empowerment can be divided into four parts, two of which are particularly crucial for using artificial intelligence to solve problems. In the context of problem-solving, the self-efficacy of AI is closely related to the sense of ability of educated citizens, as the feeling of being able to use AI to solve problems increases their self-efficacy (Chai et al., 2021). Another key empowering factor for artificial intelligence to solve problems is the creative self-efficacy of educated citizens, which refers to their confidence in their own creativity (Tierney & Farmer,2011). When students have a strong sense of creative self-efficacy, they are willing to use AI tools for creative activities. Based on this, the following assumptions are made:

H8: AI empowerment has a significant positive impact on Apply AI; H9: AI empowerment mediates the relationship between Know AI and Apply AI.

AI self-competence was formally proposed by Nils et al. (2024), based on the assumption of human-computer interaction. In human-computer interaction, humans always dominate, so they need to avoid the risk of being controlled by machines in their thoughts, emotions, and behaviors. This ability is called AI self-competence. Nils et al. (2024) divided AI self-competence into AI persuasion and AI emotion regulation. These two sub-dimensions are from the literature review. Although this is an AI literacy construct that is not often considered, empirical research has also confirmed the impact of AI literacy training courses on AI self-competence. Andr´e et al. (2024) developed two online training modules to deepen their understanding of artificial intelligence and intelligent voice assistants (IVA), and found that these two courses had no significant impact on AI persuasion and AI emotion regulation. The theory of resource allocation provides an explanation for this phenomenon (Ellis & Ashbrook,1988). When students have good AI persuasion and emotion regulation abilities, they need to allocate some cognitive and psychological resources to judge AI decisions and content, in order to avoid being completely guided by AI. In this

way, students’ internal resources will be consumed, and additional resources will not be allocated to stimulate the possibility of students using AI. Especially among non-expert students, their mastery of AI knowledge may not be profound, resulting in them needing to spend more energy on managing AI persuasion and emotional regulation. Based on this, the following assumptions are made:

H10: AI self-competence has no significant positive effect on Apply AI; H11: AI self-competence does not mediate the relationship between Know AI and Apply AI.

4  Methods

4.1  Research design

This study aims to answer two questions: (1) What are the comprehensive constructs of artificial intelligence literacy? (2) What is the correlation between these constructs? As it is an exploratory study, the following research process will be followed: Firstly, the rationality of the research topic will be identified through a literature review. Secondly, the shortcomings of existing research and the research questions of this study will be identified. Thirdly, the research hypothesis and model

4.2  Participants

The sampling in this study follows the principle of random sampling. Firstly, it is clear that the sampling target is non-STEM major university students, excluding those majoring in engineering, biological sciences, mathematics, and physical sciences. Secondly, to ensure the universality of the sample, this study distributed questionnaires online to university students in East China. Finally, to further ensure the representation of students from various non-STEM majors, this study focuses on students from five major disciplines (excluding STEM) as detailed in Table 2.

Due to the fact that this study targets non-expert university students, extra caution was taken in selecting the research subjects. Firstly, students majoring in STEM were excluded. Additionally, given China’s extensive promotion of interdisciplinary education in graduate programs, even some non-STEM majors may have received professional artificial intelligence education. Therefore, when designing the questionnaire, we first asked the respondents whether they had received professional artificial intelligence education. If they answered ‘yes’, they were automatically disqualified from continuing the survey. 4.3  Instruments

In this study, the questionnaire consists of two parts: a demographic questionnaire for students and an artificial intelligence literacy questionnaire. The students’ statistical information is presented in Table 2. The artificial intelligence literacy questionnaire consists of six scales (the AI empowerment scale includes four subscales, and the AI self-competence scale includes two subscales), each with a different number of questions and is sourced from validated research. The detailed information of the scale is presented in Table 3. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with one indicating strongly disagree and five indicating strongly agree. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients are 0.792, 0.854, 0.862, 0.842, 0.869, 0.873, 0.883, 0.924, 0.921 and 0.890 respectively, indicating that all constructs were internally consistent. All scales are derived from mature scales that have been validated, and many scholars have applied these scales for research, and the results have shown that they have good effectiveness and reliability. For example, the Recognition AI, Know AI, Apply AI, AI ethics, and AI self-competence scales used in this study were applied in Astrid et  al.‘s (2023) study, with a significant χ2-test outcome (χ2 = 886.87, p < 0.001), and the other model fit indices showed a good model fit (CFI = 0.926, RMSEA = 0.057, 95%-CI [0.051, 0.063], SRMR = 0.079), CFI > 0.9 and RMSEA < 0.08. AI empowerment scale is derived from Siu et al. (2023), where its good validity and reliability were validated.

4.4  Procedures for data collection

Before data collection, informed consent was obtained from students. Each participant read and signed the informed consent located on the first page of the online questionnaire. The survey had received approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the university the authors are affiliated with. The study’s purpose was introduced to the participants, and they were instructed on how to complete the questionnaire. Likewise, the participants were assured that their anonymity would be protected and that their participation in the study was completely voluntary. Also, participants were informed that they can terminate the questionnaire at any time if they wish. The questionnaire was conducted online and took about 30 min to complete.

4.5  Data analysis

This study’s data were analyzed with SPSS and AMOS, version 24.0. A descriptive analysis was performed after establishing the distribution’s normality and the data’s reliability. In accordance with Anderson and Gerbing (1988), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to examine the fit between observed indicators and underlying constructs prior to employing SEM. CFA, which tests the hypothesis that a relationship between the observed variables and their underlying latent construct exists (based on theory and/or previous factor analytic research), was sequentially used to verify the factor structure for these constructs.

Further, after ensuring adequate model fit and discriminant and convergent validity, SEM was used to test the hypothesized relationships in the research model (Fig. 1). SEM is a comprehensive statistical approach used to test hypotheses about relationships among observed and latent variables. Unlike traditional regression models, SEM allows for the examination of complex variable interdependencies and the inclusion of measurement error. SEM integrates factor analysis and path analysis, enabling researchers to construct, estimate, and test models that reflect both the direct and indirect relationships among variables. SEM is particularly valuable in social sciences, behavioral sciences, and market research, where it facilitates the testing of theoretical constructs that are not directly observable but inferred from multiple indicators. The method involves two main components: the measurement model, which defines the relationships between latent variables and their indicators, and the structural model, which specifies the relationships among the latent variables themselves. A key advantage of SEM is its flexibility in modeling and its ability to provide a detailed understanding of complex phenomena. However, it requires large sample sizes and careful consideration of model fit indices to ensure valid and reliable results. This study is suitable for using SEM because it meets the necessary conditions for SEM. Firstly, all data in this study were composed of observed variables combined into latent variables. Secondly, SEM can evaluate the measurement model to verify whether there is good consistency between observed variables and latent variables. This method allows this study to evaluate whether the items in the scale can accurately reflect the meaning represented by the theoretical construct. Finally, SEM can use structural models to evaluate the relationships between various latent variables, which allows this study to explore whether the constructed theoretical hypotheses are valid.

5.1  Testing the measurement model

The measurement model included all important variables and was examined using CFA with maximum likelihood estimation. First, the measurement model’s fit was evaluated using various fit indices. According to Hair et al. (2014), reporting the χ2 value and degrees of freedom, the CFI (the comparative fit index) or TLI (Tucker–Lewis index), and the RMSEA (the root mean square error of

approximation) offer enough unique data to assess a model. In our measurement model, TLI and CFI were bigger than 0.90, suggesting a satisfactory fit (Hara et al., 2010). RMSEA, with a value between 0.03 and 0.08, with 95% confidence, is considered acceptable (Hair et al., 2014). SRMR (the standardized root mean residual) with a value less than 0.09 might be used to signify an adequate model fit (Hair et al., 2014). To be acceptable, the minimum fit function (χ2) and the ratio of χ2 to its degree of freedom (χ2 /df) must be less than 3.0 (Carmines & McIver, 1981). On the basis of the aforementioned values, the measurement model used in this study fit well: χ2 = 694.93, χ2 /df = 1.143, TLI = 0.985, CFI = 0.987, SRMR = 0.066, RMSEA = 0.023.

6  Discussion

This study discusses the construct and mechanism of artificial intelligence literacy for non-experts. Through scoping review, it is found that the construct of artificial intelligence literacy mainly includes: Recognize AI, Know AI, Apply AI, AI ethics, AI empowerment, and AI self-competence. The hypothesis verification through structural equation modeling indicates the interrelationships between these six constructs.

This research found that Recognize AI has a significant positive impact on Apply AI. It is consistent with most research findings, such as Du et al. (2023), Southworth et al. (2023), Van et al. (2021), and Yetis & Rapoport (2023), all of whom believe that K12 and undergraduate students can recognize artificial intelligence tools and applications. In addition, Hemment et al. (2023) believe that the public should also recognize that artificial intelligence is an indispensable component in various applications. Therefore, in artificial intelligence education, students should first be taught how to recognize AI. In practical application, the enhancement of this recognition promotes users’ trust and acceptance of AI technology, thereby optimizing user experience and the implementation effect of AI technology. For example, in the field of customer service, when users can clearly distinguish between communication with human customer service and AI customer service, they are more inclined to adjust communication strategies based on the characteristics of the interaction objects. For AI customer service, users may pay more attention to the clarity and simplicity of instructions, thereby improving problem-solving efficiency. At the same time, the ability to recognize AI also helps users actively seek help from human customer service when encountering complex or sensitive issues, ensuring that the problems are handled properly. In addition, in the field of education, recognizing AI technology can assist in the development of personalized learning plans. If students can recognize AI elements in learning platforms, such as intelligent recommendation systems or automatic grading tools, they can be more proactive in utilizing these resources and efficiently learn based on their personal learning progress

and needs. This recognition also promotes students’ understanding and interest in AI technology, laying the foundation for the cultivation of future technological literacy.

This research found that Know AI has a significant positive impact on Apply AI. It provides a theoretical foundation for knowledge education in artificial intelligence. It is worth noting that for non-expert students, prior knowledge is not important (Ng et al., 2021b). Some researchers have also delved into which aspects of AI knowledge students should learn, mainly including machine learning (Amplo & Butler, 2023), deep learning (Kong Ng et al., 2022a), transfer learning (Van et al., 2021), and so on. Students who know AI are more proactive in utilizing AI resources for learning and enhancing their ability for self-directed learning. They can obtain rich learning materials, participate in online discussions, and even engage in interactive learning with AI through AI platforms, thereby gaining a more diverse and comprehensive learning experience. Knowing AI can also help cultivate students’ innovative thinking and problem-solving abilities. By participating in AI projects or competitions, students can personally experience the charm and challenges of AI technology, and stimulate their interest and enthusiasm for technological innovation. In this process, students need to apply their learned knowledge to solve practical problems, thereby exercising their innovative thinking and teamwork abilities.

This research found that AI Ethics has a significant positive impact on Apply AI. Ethics education has been integrated into many artificial intelligence literacy courses, covering topics such as fairness, accountability, transparency, ethics, and safety (Ng et al., 2022b). Many courses also encourage students to share their views on the impact of artificial intelligence on society, which can help identify opportunities and risks associated with its use (Kong et al., 2022b). AI ethics regulates the use of AI technology and prevents its misuse and abuse by establishing codes of conduct. In today’s increasingly popular AI, how to ensure that the use of technology complies with social ethics and legal regulations has become an urgent problem to be solved. AI ethics provide clear moral boundaries for the application of AI technology, ensuring the legality and legitimacy of its use, thereby protecting the interests and rights of the public. AI ethics promote the sustainable development of AI technology. Following ethical standards in the development and application of AI technology helps to establish trust, enhance public acceptance and satisfaction with AI technology. The establishment of this trust provides a favorable social environment for the widespread application and continuous innovation of AI technology, and promotes the sustainable development of AI technology. It also promotes the responsible use of AI technology. In the use of AI technology, following ethical norms means fully considering the social impact of the technology and ensuring that its use does not have negative consequences for humanity. This responsible use of AI technology helps to enhance its social value and positive impact, enabling AI technology to better serve human society.

This research found that AI empowerment has a significant positive impact on Apply AI. High AI self-efficacy, individuals’ confidence in applying AI, fosters exploration and innovation, driving skill improvement and widespread AI adoption. This accelerates digital transformation of individuals and organizations. Creative self-efficacy also rises, enhancing individuals’ ability to innovate with AI, broadening application scenarios and contributing to socio-economic development.

Furthermore, AI empowerment raises awareness of AI’s impact, recognizing it as a transformative force for society and quality of life. This fosters responsibility and motivation to engage in AI research and application, promoting sustainable societal development. Recent studies have echoed similar sentiments. For instance, in a study by Bandura et al. (1999), it was found that self-efficacy beliefs are crucial in determining individuals’ engagement and persistence in learning and using new technologies, including AI. This aligns with the current research’s emphasis on AI self-efficacy’s role in driving AI adoption and innovation. Furthermore, Tierney and Farmer’s (2004) work on creative self-efficacy highlights the importance of individuals’ beliefs in their innovative capabilities. The authors argue that creative selfefficacy is a significant predictor of innovative behavior and outcomes. The current research extends this by demonstrating how AI empowerment can enhance creative self-efficacy, leading to the development of new AI applications and services. Additionally, a study by Babu et al. (2024) explores the impact of AI on individuals’ perceptions of their work and its meaningfulness. The authors found that AI can positively influence individuals’ beliefs about their work’s impact on society, leading to increased motivation and engagement. This resonates with the current research’s findings that AI empowerment enhances individuals’ understanding of the meaningfulness and impact of AI applications, fostering a sense of responsibility and motivation to contribute to society’s sustainable development.

This research found that AI self-competence has a significant positive impact on Apply AI. Firstly, AI persuasion refers to the ability of AI technology to influence individual decision-making through algorithms and data analysis. Individuals with AI self-competence are able to recognize the existence of AI persuasion and conduct rational evaluations to avoid being misled or overly dependent. This ability to think independently ensures that individuals can make decisions based on their own judgment and needs when using AI technology to make decisions that best serve their own interests. Secondly, AI emotion regulation ability refers to an individual’s ability to effectively manage their own emotions when facing AI technology, avoiding the impact of emotional fluctuations that AI technology may cause. The rapid development and widespread application of AI technology sometimes raise concerns about issues such as technology loss of control and privacy breaches, leading to negative emotions such as anxiety and unease. Individuals with AI self-competence can calmly respond to these challenges, maintain a stable emotional state, and better utilize AI technology to improve their life and work efficiency.

This research found that Know AI has a significant positive impact on AI Ethics. Firstly, students who know AI are more likely to realize the importance of data privacy and security. AI systems rely on large amounts of data for training and optimization, which often contain personal privacy information. Students who know AI can have a clearer understanding of the necessity of protecting user privacy, and thus take stricter security measures to prevent data leakage and abuse. Secondly, a deep understanding of AI can help reduce bias and discrimination. The decisions of AI systems are often based on their training data, and if there is bias in the data itself, the decisions of AI systems will also be biased. Students who know AI can more sensitively identify data biases and take measures to correct these biases, ensuring that the decisions of AI systems are fairer

and unbiased. In addition, students who know AI are more likely to clarify the responsibility attribution. When AI systems encounter errors or cause accidents, there is often controversy over the attribution of responsibility. Students who know AI can have a clearer understanding of the responsibilities and obligations of all parties involved in the development and application of AI systems, thereby establishing a more reasonable accountability mechanism to ensure that responsible parties can be quickly identified and appropriate remedial measures taken when problems arise. Finally, students who know AI are more likely to focus on the autonomy and control issues of AI. With the continuous development of AI technology, the autonomy of AI systems is becoming stronger, which may pose a threat to human decision-making and autonomy. Students who know AI can have a clearer understanding of this issue and take measures to ensure human control over AI systems and avoid adverse effects on humans caused by AI systems.

This research found that Know AI has a significant positive impact on AI empowerment. After mastering AI knowledge, individuals can more confidently operate and apply AI tools to solve complex problems, thereby improving work efficiency and innovation ability. By understanding the operating mechanism of AI, individuals can better utilize AI technology for creative design and innovative practice, and stimulate their own creativity. A deep understanding of AI enables individuals to recognize the important role of AI technology in promoting social progress and economic development, and thus actively participate in the application and promotion of AI technology, feeling greater personal value and social significance.

This research found that Know AI has a significant positive impact on AI selfcompetence. Firstly, students who know AI are better able to recognize and resist its persuasive power. AI, through deep learning and natural language processing techniques, can generate delicate and persuasive language, which may to some extent influence human decision-making. However, individuals with a certain understanding of AI principles are able to analyze the information provided by AI more rationally, judge its authenticity and reliability, thereby avoiding excessive influence from AI and maintaining decision-making autonomy and independence. Secondly, knowing AI can also help individuals maintain emotional stability and avoid interference from AI emotion regulation. AI can regulate emotions by analyzing users’ emotional states and behavioral characteristics, which may have an impact on people’s emotions in certain contexts. However, individuals with a deep understanding of AI are able to recognize this and take corresponding measures, such as maintaining selfreflection, adjusting their mindset, to maintain emotional stability and autonomy. In addition, knowing AI can enhance students’ self-efficacy when using AI tools. When students have a certain understanding of the principles and applications of AI, they will be more confident in using AI tools, thereby better completing work tasks and improving work efficiency. This increase in self-efficacy also helps students remain calm and rational when facing AI, avoiding excessive influence from it. But the research results of Andr´e et al. (2024) are exactly the opposite. The two online training modules they developed aim to cultivate the ability to use intelligent voice assistants based on artificial intelligence systems, but they found that training people in AI persuasion literacy and AI emotion regulation did not significantly increase.

Therefore, further research is needed on the relationship between AI knowledge and AI self-competence.

This research found that AI Ethics mediates the relationship between Know AI and Apply AI. Yun (2024) used a double contrast teaching method to teach 147 senior primary school students’ courses on machine learning and big language model learning. The results showed that the experimental group had significantly higher moral awareness than the control group, and the experimental group also showed greater willingness to learn AI and intrinsic motivation after learning relevant knowledge, thereby improving their level of using AI. However, different genders and ages may play a controlling role in the mediating effect. As some researchers have found, developing students’ high-level moral principles in artificial intelligence courses poses challenges for K12 students (Van et al., 2021). Students’ understanding of the basic concepts, principles, and content of AI (i.e. knowing AI) is the foundation for applying AI. However, knowledge alone is not enough to ensure that students can use AI responsibly. At this point, the intervention of AI ethics becomes particularly important. It teaches students to follow ethical and moral principles such as respecting human values, protecting privacy, safeguarding human rights, ensuring safety and transparency when using AI. In addition, AI ethics not only regulate students’ behavior, but also promote their deep understanding and reflection on AI technology. By studying and discussing ethical issues related to AI, students can gain a more comprehensive understanding of the potential risks and social impacts of AI technology, leading to greater caution and responsibility when using AI.

This research found that AI empowerment mediates the relationship between Know AI and Apply AI. Kong et al. (2021) found that offering AI related courses in the form of flipped classrooms can help improve the AI empowerment of female participants, making them closer to men. Students first establish a preliminary understanding of AI technology by learning and understanding the basic concepts, principles, and content of AI (i.e. knowing AI). However, this knowledge alone is not enough to make them confident in using AI tools. At this point, the role of AI empowerment is highlighted. AI empowerment has given students confidence in using AI tools, including AI self-efficacy, creating self-efficacy, and so on. This confidence comes from students’ deep understanding of AI technology and successful experiences gained in practical operations. With this confidence, students are more willing to try and explore various applications of AI technology, thus continuously learning and growing while using AI. Therefore, AI empowerment not only promotes students’ mastery and application of AI technology, but also stimulates their creativity and innovative spirit.

This research found that AI self-competence mediates the relationship between Know AI and Apply AI. AI self-competence enables students to avoid interference from AI in self decision-making and emotional regulation. For example, when faced with persuasive information from AI, students with AI self-competence can identify and resist the potential impact of this information, thereby maintaining decision-making independence. Similarly, in terms of AI emotion regulation, they can also maintain the stability of their own emotions and avoid being influenced by AI’s emotional guidance. Therefore, AI self-competence not only deepens students’ understanding and application of AI, but also enhances their autonomy and

emotional resilience when interacting with AI. In the process of education, teachers should focus on cultivating students’ AI self-competence, guiding them to think critically about AI information, and strengthening emotional management training to help them better cope with the challenges of interacting with AI.

7  Implications and limitations

7.1  Implications

From a theoretical research perspective, this study proposes artificial intelligence literacy constructs that include knowledge, skills, ethics, and affective. From a literature review, knowledge, skills, and ethics are widely recognized as the structures of artificial intelligence literacy, while affective construct varies depending on the research objectives. Although this study did not propose a very detailed framework for artificial intelligence literacy, at least we can understand the importance of affective construct in the framework of artificial intelligence. Subsequent research can explore affective construct in more detail.

From the perspective of educational practice, this study has implications for schools to carry out artificial intelligence literacy education. On the one hand, schools should carry out different artificial intelligence literacy education according to different groups of people. The educational objectives of expert students and non-expert students are not the same, and creating AI is the main difference. The teaching of non-expert students should maintain an appropriate balance to prevent a tendency towards relying solely on technology. On the other hand, the courses offered by schools should be conducive to developing students’ moral and emotional abilities, which not only require comprehensive and down-to-earth educational content, but also innovative educational methods to enhance students’ real and effective interaction with artificial intelligence, such as using teaching methods like flipped classroom, project-based learning, problem-based learning, etc.

7.2  Limitations

Firstly, the exploration of the construct of artificial intelligence literacy in this study only comes from literature review and has not been rigorously proven empirically. Perhaps the constructs derived from literature synthesis in this article also have higher-order relationships. Secondly, the scales used in this study are all developed for English speaking countries or regions, and all participants in this study are native Chinese speakers. There may be distortion issues in the translation of the scales, and students’ cultural backgrounds may not fully apply to all scales. Although the questionnaire was translated as accurately as possible, some professional terms or expressions may have misunderstandings in the Chinese context, which can affect the ability of respondents to answer questions accurately. Thirdly, in exploring the construct relationship of artificial intelligence literacy, this study only used the Apply AI construct as the dependent variable. However, the relationship between

different constructs is complex, so future research can continue to explore the interrelationships that were not covered in this article.

8  Conclusion

This study explores the operational mechanism of the construct of artificial intelligence literacy, mainly addressing two issues: (1) what are the constructs of artificial intelligence literacy; (2) What are the relationships between these constructs. This study employed a mixed research approach to address these two questions. The first question is addressed with literature review, summarizing the current research on artificial intelligence literacy frameworks and integrating the AI literacy constructs from existing literature, including six constructs: Recognize AI, Know AI, Apply AI, AI ethics, AI empowerment and AI self-competence. The second question adopts a quantitative research method, collecting questionnaire data and using structural equation modeling to explore the relationships between different structures. The research has found that recognize AI, know AI, AI ethics, AI empowerment, and AI self-competence all have significant positive predictive effects on apply AI. Know AI also has a significant positive predictive effect on AI ethics, AI empowerment, and AI self-competence. AI ethics, AI empowerment, and AI selfcompetence play a mediating role in the relationship between know AI and apply AI. These research explorations have certain enlightening significance for both theory and practice. In terms of theory, it further improves the classification of artificial intelligence literacy constructs in current research, providing a foundation for further in-depth research in the future. For practice, different construct relationships are meaningful for the teaching of artificial intelligence literacy and curriculum design in schools. However, this study also has some limitations. For example, the exploration of the construct of artificial intelligence literacy only comes from literature and has not been proven through more scientifically rigorous empirical research. The scales used are also from existing research, so there may be differences caused by students’ cultural backgrounds. The exploration of the relationship between artificial intelligence literacy constructs has not been comprehensive, and only Apply AI is used as the dependent variable. In fact, the relationship between different structures is complex, and subsequent research can further verify the relationships that were not validated in this study.
