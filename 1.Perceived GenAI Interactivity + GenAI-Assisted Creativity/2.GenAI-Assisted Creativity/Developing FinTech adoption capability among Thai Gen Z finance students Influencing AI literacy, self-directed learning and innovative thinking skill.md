Keywords: AI literacy Self-directed learning Innovative thinking skill FinTech adoption capability Generation Z students

This study investigates the effect of AI literacy on FinTech adoption capability, while examining the mediating roles of self-directed learning and innovative thinking skills among Generation Z finance students in Thailand. A quantitative research approach was employed, with data collected via an online questionnaire administered to 391 undergraduate finance students. The data were analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM). The results reveal that AI literacy has significant positive effects on self-directed learning, innovative thinking skills, and FinTech adoption capability. Although self-directed learning does not directly influence FinTech adoption capability, it exhibits a significant indirect effect through innovative thinking skills. Further mediation analysis indicates that innovative thinking skills function as a critical mechanism through which AI literacy and self-directed learning contribute to effective FinTech adoption. These findings suggest that enhancing AI literacy alone may not be sufficient unless it is complemented by the development of self-directed learning behaviors and innovative thinking skills. This study contributes to the growing body of literature on financial technology adoption within educational settings and offers policy-relevant insights for strengthening the digital competencies of finance students in the context of ongoing digital transformation.

Furthermore, a persistent issue within the Thai education system is the underdevelopment of self-directed learning skills, which are essential for enabling learners to independently manage and advance their learning without excessive reliance on external guidance (Wu & Lin, 2009). 2. Literature review

2.1. Theoretical foundation

This study is grounded in an integrative theoretical framework that combines Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), Self-Directed Learning Theory, and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) to explain the development of FinTech capability in the digital economy. Within this framework, Social Cognitive Theory serves as the overarching perspective, emphasizing the dynamic interplay among personal factors, cognitive processes, and behavioral outcomes (Bandura, 1991). From this standpoint, AI literacy is conceptualized as a critical personal capability that shapes individuals’ ability to interpret, evaluate, and effectively utilize digital technologies. It enhances not only technical knowledge but also cognitive readiness to engage with complex and evolving technological environments.

Building on this foundation, Self-Directed Learning Theory explains the mechanisms through which individuals transform foun­ dational knowledge into higher-order competencies. Self-directed learning reflects an individual's capacity to set learning goals, regulate learning strategies, and independently evaluate learning outcomes (Garrison, 1997a, 1997b; Knowles, 1975). In this study, AI literacy is posited to facilitate self-directed learning behaviors, enabling individuals to continuously adapt and acquire new knowledge within rapidly changing digital contexts. Furthermore, innovative thinking skills represent a higher-order cognitive outcome char­ acterized by the ability to generate novel ideas, think flexibly, and solve problems under conditions of uncertainty (Runco & Jaeger, 2012; Scott et al., 2004). Within the SCT framework, innovative thinking can be viewed as a cognitive transformation process that emerges from self-directed learning and mediates the relationship between knowledge acquisition and the development of practical capabilities.

With regard to the outcome variable, this study draws on the Technology Acceptance Model, which traditionally explains tech­ nology adoption through perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (Davis, 1989). However, this study extends TAM by conceptualizing FinTech adoption capability as a more advanced form of technology engagement, encompassing not only adoption intention but also actual usage, knowledge application, and the ability to evaluate issues such as security and trustworthiness. This extension reflects a shift from perception-based adoption toward capability-based outcomes. In addition to this, the proposed framework suggests a process-oriented mechanism in which AI literacy, as a personal capability, influences self-directed learning as a learning process, which in turn fosters innovative thinking as a form of cognitive transformation, ultimately leading to FinTech adoption capability as a behavioral and skill-based outcome. By integrating these theoretical perspectives, this study provides a coherent and unified explanation of how foundational digital competencies are translated into practical technological capabilities. 2.2. AI literacy

AI literacy in this study is conceptualized as a foundational technological competence that enables individuals to understand, evaluate, and appropriately apply artificial intelligence (AI) in both learning and financial contexts. Rather than being treated as a higher-order cognitive outcome, AI literacy is positioned as a domain-specific capability that precedes and facilitates subsequent learning and cognitive transformation processes. This conceptualization is grounded in Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), which posits that knowledge structures shape individuals' cognitive processing and behavioral responses in complex environments (Bandura, 1991). From this perspective, AI literacy represents a key personal capability that facilitates individuals’ interaction with AI-driven systems and supports subsequent learning and decision-making processes.

Consistent with prior literature, AI literacy encompasses an understanding of core AI concepts, including algorithmic mechanisms, machine learning processes, and big data utilization, as well as awareness of ethical considerations and societal implications (Chiu et al., 2024; Long & Magerko, 2020). However, in contrast to perspectives that implicitly position AI literacy as an advanced cognitive capability, this study explicitly distinguishes AI literacy from higher-order constructs by framing it as an input capability that enables, rather than reflects, cognitive transformation. This distinction is particularly important given the conceptual proximity among AI literacy, self-directed learning, and innovative thinking identified in prior research.

Operationally, AI literacy comprises five dimensions: (1) AI conceptual understanding (Sarker, 2022); (2) AI technical awareness (Sarker, 2022); (3) ethical consideration (Long & Magerko, 2020); (4) AI application proficiency (Ali et al., 2024); and (5) AI communication skills (Chiu et al., 2024). Collectively, these dimensions capture individuals' practical competence in using AI without conflating it with higher-order cognitive transformation processes. Within the proposed framework, and consistent with SCT, AI lit­ eracy is expected to influence self-directed learning by enhancing individuals’ perceived capability to regulate their own learning processes. Individuals with stronger AI-related knowledge are better positioned to identify learning needs, evaluate information sources, and adapt learning strategies in technology-rich environments (Garrison, 1997a, 1997b; Knowles, 1975). Furthermore, AI literacy is expected to influence innovative thinking skills through its role in shaping cognitive processes. Exposure to AI systems and their underlying logic provides informational and analytical resources that support idea generation, problem reframing, and flexible thinking (Broekhuizen et al., 2023). In line with SCT, this reflects the transformation of knowledge into higher-order cognitive out­ comes through learning processes, rather than implying a direct equivalence between AI literacy and innovation.

Finally, AI literacy is expected to exert a direct effect on FinTech adoption capability. Individuals with stronger AI-related knowledge are better able to evaluate the functionality, risks, and reliability of AI-driven financial technologies (Pinski & Benlian, 2024). Moving beyond perception-based approaches, this study conceptualizes FinTech adoption capability as a skill-based outcome encompassing knowledge of FinTech tools, frequency of use, and awareness of security and trust considerations. Accordingly, AI literacy is theorized as a foundational input capability that drives learning mechanisms and cognitive transformation processes, ul­ timately leading to FinTech adoption capability. 2.3. Self-directed learning

Self-directed learning (SDL) in this study is conceptualized as a self-regulatory learning mechanism through which individuals actively plan, monitor, and evaluate their own learning processes, rather than as a general cognitive ability or outcome. This definition is grounded in both Self-Directed Learning Theory and Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), which emphasize that learning behavior is shaped by individuals’ capacity to regulate their cognition, motivation, and actions in response to environmental demands (Bandura, 1991; Garrison, 1997a, 1997b; Knowles, 1975). From this perspective, SDL represents a process-oriented capability that governs how individuals transform knowledge into meaningful learning outcomes.

In contrast to AI literacy, which reflects domain-specific technological competence, SDL focuses on the regulation of learning processes, including goal setting, strategic planning, self-monitoring, and the adaptive adjustment of learning strategies (Aladini et al., 2025). This distinction is essential to avoid conceptual overlap, as SDL does not capture what individuals know or produce, but rather how they manage and direct their own learning activities. By positioning SDL as a learning mechanism, this study clearly differentiates it from AI literacy (input capability) and innovative thinking skills (cognitive transformation outcomes).

From an indicator-based perspective, SDL comprises four key dimensions: (1) goal setting, referring to the ability to define clear and contextually relevant learning objectives (Garrison, 1997a, 1997b); (2) self-monitoring, involving the continuous evaluation of one's learning progress and understanding (Li et al., 2023); (3) self-motivation, reflecting intrinsic motivation to engage in learning without reliance on external incentives (Garrison, 1997a, 1997b); and (4) resource utilization, defined as the ability to identify and effectively leverage learning resources, including digital tools and expert networks (Finn et al., 2024). These dimensions capture regulatory processes rather than knowledge or creative outputs, thereby reinforcing SDL's role as a mechanism rather than an outcome construct.

2.4. Innovative thinking skill

Innovative thinking skill (ITS) in this study is conceptualized as an advanced cognitive outcome that reflects an individual's ca­ pacity to generate original ideas, reconceptualize problems, and apply flexible reasoning in complex and uncertain environments. Rather than representing a basic form of knowledge or a learning process, ITS is positioned as a higher-order cognitive capability that emerges from prior learning experiences. This conceptualization is grounded in both creativity research and Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), which posit that higher-order cognitive outcomes develop through iterative learning, self-regulation, and continuous inter­ action with environmental stimuli (Bandura, 1991; Runco & Jaeger, 2012). Accordingly, ITS is understood as a cognitive trans­ formation outcome that arises from learning processes rather than as a direct extension of technological knowledge.

In contrast to AI literacy, which captures domain-specific technological competence, and self-directed learning, which governs how individuals regulate their learning processes, ITS emphasizes the transformation of knowledge and experience into creative, adaptive, and contextually relevant solutions. This distinction is critical to avoid conceptual redundancy, as ITS reflects the application and transformation of cognition, rather than knowledge acquisition (AI literacy) or learning regulation (SDL). By explicitly defining ITS as an outcome construct, this study ensures clear theoretical separation among the three constructs within the proposed process-based framework.

From a measurement perspective, ITS is operationalized through four key dimensions: (1) idea generation, defined as the ability to produce diverse and original ideas beyond conventional patterns (Dou et al., 2021); (2) cognitive flexibility, referring to the capacity to shift perspectives and adapt problem-solving approaches (Bobrowicz & Thibaut, 2023); (3) risk-taking attitude, indicating a will­ ingness to experiment and view failure as an opportunity for learning (Caliendo et al., 2009); and (4) problem reframing, which in­ volves interpreting problems from alternative viewpoints to develop innovative solutions (Kim et al., 2020). These dimensions capture higher-order cognitive processes that are conceptually distinct from both technological competence and learning regulation mechanisms.

Within the proposed framework, ITS is expected to positively influence FinTech adoption capability. Individuals with stronger innovative thinking skills are more capable of identifying novel application opportunities, redesigning existing processes, and adapting technologies to meet specific contextual needs. Unlike perception-based technology adoption models that primarily emphasize atti­ tudes such as perceived usefulness or ease of use, ITS reflects a deeper cognitive capability that enables individuals to actively shape how technologies are utilized and integrated into practice. This perspective aligns with a capability-based view of technology use, wherein effective adoption depends not only on users’ perceptions but also on their ability to creatively apply and adapt technological tools (Cumming et al., 2023) Accordingly, ITS is positioned as a cognitive transformation outcome that mediates the relationship between learning processes and the development of practical technological capabilities within the proposed process-based model. 2.5. FinTech adoption capability

FinTech Adoption Capability (FAC) in this study is conceptualized as an individual's ability to effectively understand, utilize, and critically evaluate financial technologies (FinTech) in real-world contexts, rather than merely reflecting their intention or perception toward adoption. This definition adopts a capability-based perspective, emphasizing what individuals are able to do with technology in practice, rather than how they perceive it. In contemporary settings, FinTech encompasses a broad spectrum of digital financial services, including mobile banking, e-wallets, robo-advisory systems, blockchain-based applications, crowdfunding platforms, and online lending services (Tran et al., 2024; Wang, 2023). For finance students, FAC serves as a key indicator of their readiness to adapt to rapidly evolving financial technologies and to effectively select and apply appropriate FinTech tools in response to situational demands (Bermeo-Giraldo et al., 2023). 3.2. Measurement instruments

This study utilized a structured questionnaire as the primary instrument for collecting quantitative data. The measurement items were developed through an extensive review of prior literature related to the key constructs, namely AI literacy, self-directed learning, innovative thinking skills, and FinTech adoption capability. All items were carefully adapted to align with the context of Generation Z undergraduate finance students in Thailand, thereby ensuring both conceptual relevance and contextual appropriateness. The ques­ tionnaire was organized into two main sections. The first section collected demographic information and background characteristics related to respondents' learning experiences and technology usage. The second section assessed the core constructs using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). This scaling approach was intended to capture respondents’ selfperceived levels of capability, learning behaviors, and cognitive processes associated with each construct.

All constructs were specified as reflective measurement models, based on the assumption that the observed indicators represent manifestations of their respective latent variables. Accordingly, variations in the latent constructs are expected to be reflected in corresponding changes across their indicators, which are conceptually interchangeable within each construct. Prior to data collection, the questionnaire was subjected to a content validity assessment by subject-matter experts with expertise in finance, financial tech­ nology, and artificial intelligence. A pilot study was subsequently conducted to evaluate the clarity and reliability of the measurement items.

To further enhance measurement quality, item validation procedures were implemented. During the measurement model evalu­ ation stage, indicators with low factor loadings or insufficient contributions to construct reliability were critically assessed and, where necessary, removed in accordance with established thresholds in the PLS-SEM literature. This procedure ensured that only indicators demonstrating adequate reliability and validity were retained for subsequent analysis. In addition, nonresponse bias was examined during the data collection process to ensure the robustness of the dataset. 3.3. Data analysis methods

Data analysis in this study commenced with descriptive statistics to summarize respondents' characteristics and to examine overall patterns across the key constructs, namely AI literacy, self-directed learning, innovative thinking skills, and FinTech adoption capa­ bility. Measures such as means, standard deviations, and distributional properties were used to capture respondents’ self-reported capabilities, learning behaviors, and cognitive tendencies.

Prior to testing the structural relationships, the measurement model was evaluated in terms of reliability and validity. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach's alpha and composite reliability (CR), while convergent validity was examined through the average variance extracted (AVE). Variance inflation factor (VIF) values were also analyzed to detect potential multi­ collinearity among indicators. Given that the data were collected  תועצמאבa self-reported questionnaire, common method bias (CMB) was considered a potential concern. To mitigate this issue, the questionnaire was carefully designed with clear construct definitions and structured to minimize ambiguity in respondents' interpretations. In addition, statistical assessments were conducted to evaluate whether the observed relationships were unduly influenced by shared measurement methods rather than reflecting substantive as­ sociations among constructs.

Following confirmation of the measurement model's adequacy, Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) was employed as the primary analytical technique for hypothesis testing. The use of PLS-SEM is justified for several reasons. First, the proposed framework involves multiple direct and indirect relationships, including sequential mediation effects, which require the simultaneous estimation of interdependent structural paths. Second, the study aims not only to test theoretical relationships but also to explain and predict the development of FinTech adoption capability within a capability-based process framework. Third, PLS-SEM is particularly suitable for exploratory and prediction-oriented models in which latent constructs are measured using multiple indicators, with an emphasis on maximizing the explained variance of endogenous variables.

The structural model was evaluated by examining path coefficients, t-statistics, and p-values to determine the significance of the hypothesized relationships. Additionally, coefficients of determination (R2 and adjusted R2) were reported to assess the model's explanatory power, while effect size measures (f2) were used to evaluate the relative contribution of each predictor. 4. Results

This section presents the empirical results concerning the impact of AI literacy on FinTech adoption capability, along with the mediating roles of self-directed learning and innovative thinking skills among Generation Z finance students in Thailand. The findings are reported in a structured sequence, beginning with a description of respondents’ demographic characteristics, followed by the evaluation of the measurement and structural models, and concluding with the results of hypothesis testing and the analysis of indirect effects.

The final sample consisted of 391 Generation Z finance students. Female respondents constituted the majority of the sample (65.00%), while male respondents accounted for 35.00%. In terms of institutional affiliation, most participants were enrolled in public universities (83.40%), with the remaining 16.60% attending private institutions. Regarding internship experience, the largest pro­ portion of respondents reported internship durations of less than three months (69.60%), followed by those with internships lasting 3–5 months and 6–8 months. Regarding to academic performance, the majority of students reported a cumulative grade point average (GPA) in the range of 3.01 to 3.50 (40.20%). This was followed by students with GPAs between 3.51 and 4.00, and those with GPAs between 2.51 and 3.00. At the same time, it challenges perspectives that treat technological knowledge as the principal determinant of adoption behavior, thereby underscoring the importance of additional mediating mechanisms.

In relation to self-directed learning, the results indicate a significant positive effect on innovative thinking skills, thereby supporting prior research linking self-regulated learning to cognitive flexibility and creative problem-solving (Morris & Rohs, 2021; Tekkol & Demirel, 2018). This unexpected result suggests that self-directed learning alone may be insufficient to translate learning efforts into effective technology use, particularly in contexts involving complex decision-making and risk evaluation. One plausible expla­ nation is that self-directed learning primarily enhances the processes of knowledge acquisition and regulation, but does not directly influence the practical application of knowledge unless that knowledge is transformed into higher-order cognitive capabilities. This interpretation is consistent with prior research suggesting that learning processes must be cognitively internalized and restructured before they can shape behavioral outcomes (Rashid & Asghar, 2016). 5.1. Transforming knowledge into practical application in the digital finance era of the younger generation

The findings of this study indicate that the development of FinTech adoption capability among Generation Z finance students is not determined solely by technological knowledge, but rather by the ability to translate that knowledge into effective decision-making and problem-solving in real-world contexts. In contemporary digital financial environments, students with higher levels of AI literacy are better positioned to understand technological systems and evaluate associated risks. However, the effective application of such knowledge requires more than technical understanding; it depends on individuals’ capacity to regulate their learning processes and transform insights into actionable strategies.

Self-directed learning plays a critical role in enabling individuals to continuously acquire, update, and refine their knowledge, particularly in rapidly evolving technological contexts. Nevertheless, the findings demonstrate that self-directed learning alone does not directly lead to FinTech adoption capability unless it is complemented by higher-order cognitive abilities. This underscores the pivotal role of innovative thinking as a mechanism that bridges the gap between knowledge acquisition and practical application. Innovative thinking skills enable individuals to reinterpret problems, explore alternative solutions, and creatively apply technological tools to achieve desired outcomes.

These results reinforce a process-oriented perspective, suggesting that effective FinTech adoption is not driven solely by knowledge or learning effort, but by the transformation of these elements into adaptive, context-sensitive decision-making capabilities. From a broader standpoint, the findings reflect an evolving role of younger generations in digital societies. Rather than functioning as passive users of technology, Generation Z individuals are increasingly expected to act as active decision-makers who critically evaluate, adapt, and apply FinTech solutions in dynamic environments. These insights highlight the importance of fostering not only technological literacy but also learning and higher-order cognitive capabilities in order to better prepare students for real-world financial decisionmaking.

5.2. Implications of the study

The findings of this study contribute to the management education literature by demonstrating that the development of technologyrelated capability is not merely a function of technical knowledge acquisition, but rather the result of a structured learning process involving self-regulation and cognitive transformation. This perspective is consistent with contemporary approaches in management education that emphasize experiential learning, capability development, and the integration of higher-order thinking skills in pre­ paring students for complex and dynamic environments. From a theoretical standpoint, the results extend prior research by showing that AI literacy operates as a foundational capability that must be transformed through learning processes and innovative cognition before it can meaningfully influence practical technology use. This underscores the need to reposition technology education within management programs from a content-driven orientation toward a process-based learning framework that integrates knowledge acquisition, learning behavior, and cognitive skill development.

In terms of pedagogy, the results indicate a shift away from passive knowledge transmission toward more active and practiceoriented learning strategies. Instructional approaches such as project-based learning, problem-based learning, financial simulations, and case-based analysis can be systematically designed to foster experimentation, critical evaluation, and creative problem-solving in real-world contexts. These methods enable students to apply AI and FinTech knowledge in complex scenarios, thereby facilitating the transformation of theoretical understanding into practical capability.

Furthermore, assessment practices should be aligned with this process-oriented perspective. Rather than emphasizing knowledge recall alone, evaluation methods should focus on students’ ability to apply, adapt, and innovate in response to real-world financial challenges. This may include tasks such as designing FinTech solutions, analyzing digital financial risks, or developing data-driven decision-making strategies. Such an approach to assessment more effectively captures the development of applied competencies required in the digital economy. The findings suggest that preparing future finance professionals for the digital era requires an in­ tegrated educational approach that simultaneously fosters AI literacy, self-directed learning, and innovative thinking skills. This approach not only equips students with the knowledge necessary to understand emerging technologies, but also enables them to apply these technologies effectively in dynamic and uncertain financial environments.

5.3. Limitations and future research directions

Despite providing meaningful insights into the role of AI literacy in shaping FinTech adoption capability through the mediating mechanisms of self-directed learning and innovative thinking skills, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the research is based on cross-sectional data collected at a single point in time, which restricts the ability to capture developmental changes and long-term dynamics in learning processes and FinTech usage behavior. Although the proposed relationships are theoretically grounded, causal interpretations should therefore be approached with caution.

Second, the sample is limited to Generation Z finance students in Thailand, a group influenced by specific educational, techno­ logical, and socio-cultural contexts. This context-specific focus may constrain the generalizability of the findings to other populations, such as students from different academic disciplines, financial industry professionals, or individuals in countries with varying levels of technological advancement and economic development. Moreover, the use of convenience sampling, combined with the absence of differentiation across institutional types, geographic regions, and socioeconomic backgrounds, may further affect the representa­ tiveness of the sample. These limitations should be carefully considered when assessing the broader applicability of the proposed model.

Third, the study relies on self-reported questionnaire data, which may introduce perceptual and self-assessment biases. Although efforts were made to enhance clarity and reduce ambiguity in the measurement design, such biases may still influence the accuracy of the responses and should be acknowledged when interpreting the results.

Future research can address these limitations in several ways. Longitudinal research designs would provide deeper insights into how AI literacy, self-directed learning, innovative thinking skills, and FinTech adoption capability evolve over time, thereby strengthening causal inference. Expanding the sample to include participants from diverse academic disciplines, professional back­ grounds, and geographic contexts would enhance external validity and improve the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, future studies may incorporate additional variables—such as risk perception, trust in digital financial systems, or prior experience with FinTech usage—to develop a more comprehensive understanding of technology adoption behavior. Such extensions would contribute to a more nuanced perspective on how technological, cognitive, and contextual factors jointly shape FinTech adoption in the digital economy.

6. Conclusion

This study set out to examine the influence of AI literacy on FinTech adoption capability by incorporating the mediating roles of self-directed learning and innovative thinking skills among Generation Z finance students in Thailand. The findings demonstrate that AI literacy serves as a foundational capability that significantly enhances self-directed learning, innovative thinking skills, and FinTech adoption capability. Importantly, the results indicate that technological capability does not emerge from knowledge alone, but rather from a structured process in which learning behaviors and cognitive transformation play central roles.

Specifically, self-directed learning and innovative thinking skills operate as sequential mechanisms that translate AI-related knowledge into effective FinTech usage. While self-directed learning does not exert a direct effect on FinTech adoption capability, it contributes indirectly through innovative thinking skills, underscoring the critical role of cognitive transformation in bridging knowledge and practical application. These findings support a process-oriented perspective of technology capability development, highlighting the need to develop AI literacy, learning processes, and innovative cognition in an integrated manner.

From a theoretical perspective, this study extends the existing literature by moving beyond perception-based and technologycentric explanations of FinTech adoption toward a more comprehensive, capability-oriented framework. From a practical stand­ point, the findings suggest that developing competencies in the digital economy requires more than technical training alone.
