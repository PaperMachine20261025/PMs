1. Introduction

1.1. Curiosity as a multidimensional personality trait

Curiosity is defined as the intrinsic desire to acquire new knowledge, information, experiences, or stimulation in order to reduce uncertainty or explore the unknown (Berlyne, 1954; Litman, 2005). As one of the fundamental motivational forces underlying human exploratory behavior, curiosity plays a central role in memory, learning, and crea­ tivity (Gross et al., 2020; Gruber et al., 2014; Ten et al., 2021). In addition to being conceptualized as a cognitive–affective state, curiosity is also regarded as a personality trait (Naylor, 1981; Silvia & Chris­ tensen, 2020). A growing body of research suggests that curiosity is not a unidimensional construct, but rather a multidimensional personality trait.

From the perspective of the object of curiosity, Berlyne (1954) distinguished between perceptual curiosity and epistemic curiosity. Perceptual curiosity is driven by sensory features such as novelty, complexity, uncertainty, or stimulus conflict, motivating individuals to attend to and explore external sensory information. Curiosity is closely linked to creativity; however, given that curiosity is a multidimensional personality construct, it remains unclear which specific facets of curiosity are most strongly related to creativity. Drawing on the Five- Dimensional Curiosity Scale Revised (5DCR), the present study employed both variable-centered (multiple regression analysis) and person-centered (latent profile analysis) approaches to examine how different curiosity traits—and their combinational patterns—relate to creativity. Results from the multiple regression analysis revealed that Stress Tolerance and Joyous Exploration were the two facets most strongly associated with crea­ tivity. The latent profile analysis identified four distinct curiosity profiles across the five dimensions, and sub­ sequent comparisons showed significant differences among these profiles in divergent thinking, creative behavior, and creative achievement. Together, these findings provide new evidence for understanding the multidimensional nature of curiosity and its links to creativity, and offer empirical guidance for fostering curi­ osity traits that are most conducive to creative outcomes.

Berlyne (1954) further argued that epistemic curiosity serves as a primary driver of human learning and exploration. Grossnickle (2016) further proposed additional forms of curiosity—such as manipulatory curiosity, involving exploration of the physical environment, and social curiosity, reflecting interest in others' personal information—suggesting that curiosity comprises at least four forms: physical, perceptual, epistemic, and social curiosity.

Recent reviews converge on the conclusion that curiosity is a multidimensional psychological construct encompassing interest-type and deprivation-type curiosity, social curiosity, intrapersonal curios­ ity, and additional dimensions (Grossnickle, 2016; Wagstaff et al., 2021; Yow et al., 2022). Collectively, this body of work demonstrates the multidimensional nature of curiosity and supports the view that curi­ osity functions as a personality trait composed of multiple, distin­ guishable dimensions.

1.2. Five-dimensional curiosity

Given the multidimensional nature of curiosity, (Kashdan, 2018, Kashdan et al., 2020) developed the Five-Dimensional Curiosity Scale Revised (5DCR), which conceptualizes curiosity as comprising five relatively independent yet interrelated dimensions: Joyous Exploration, Deprivation Sensitivity, Stress Tolerance, Thrill Seeking, and Social Curiosity. Notably, Social Curiosity can be further distinguished based on the mode of engagement into Overt Social Curiosity and Covert Social Curiosity (Birenbaum et al., 2019; Kashdan et al., 2020).

Joyous Exploration refers to the tendency to experience positive emotions while engaging in the pursuit of new information or experi­ ences. Deprivation Sensitivity reflects the discomfort or anxiety trig­ gered by knowledge gaps, which in turn motivates a strong drive to acquire information. Stress Tolerance denotes an individual's capacity to withstand ambiguity, uncertainty, and complex or unpredictable situa­ tions. Thrill Seeking captures a proactive pursuit of novel, intense, or complex sensory stimulation. Social Curiosity refers to the interest in acquiring information about other people, including their thoughts, behaviors, and experiences. This curiosity can manifest in overt ways (e. g., asking questions, direct observation) or covert ways (e.g., eaves­ dropping, indirect information gathering), referred to respectively as Overt Social Curiosity and Covert Social Curiosity.

These five dimensions are differentially associated with personality traits, emotional experiences, and well-being (Kashdan, 2018). The 5DCR provides a systematic assessment of the various facets of curiosity and is considered one of the most comprehensive and representative instruments currently available for measuring curiosity.

1.3. The link between curiosity and creativity

Creativity is commonly defined as the ability to generate ideas or products that are both novel and appropriate (Hennessey & Amabile, 2010). In psychological research, assessments of creativity span multiple levels, including cognitive processes, behaviors, achievements, and personality traits, reflecting individuals' creative potential and perfor­ mance across different domains (Sternberg & Lubart, 1992). Creative thinking primarily involves divergent thinking and convergent thinking. Divergent thinking refers to the ability to generate diverse and original ideas when facing a problem (Runco & Acar, 2012) and is commonly assessed using tasks such as the Alternative Uses Task (AUT; Guilford, 1967) or creative drawing tasks (Torrance, 1966). Convergent thinking, by contrast, involves integrating or selecting the optimal solution from multiple possibilities and is often measured using the Remote Associates Test (RAT) (Marko et al., 2019). Self-report measures capture creativity through individuals' evaluations of their own creative abilities, such as the Creativity Domains Questionnaire (CDQ; Kaufman, 2012), as well as reports of their engagement in creative behaviors in daily life or achievements over a period of time, such as the Inventory of Creative Activities and Achievements (ICAA; Diedrich et al., 2018), the Creative Achievement Questionnaire (CAQ; Carson et al., 2005), and the Creative Behavior Inventory (CBI; Hocevar, 1979), thereby providing an index of creativity in both daily life and professional contexts.

Although the close relationship between curiosity and creativity has been well established, most prior research has focused on the relation­ ship between creativity and global measures of curiosity, as assessed by instruments such as the Curiosity and Exploration Inventory–I (CEI–I; Kashdan et al., 2004) and the Curiosity and Exploration Inventory–II (CEI-II; Kashdan et al., 2009). Other studies have examined single di­ mensions of curiosity, such as epistemic curiosity (Hardy et al., 2017; Li et al., 2023) or specific curiosity (Hagtvedt et al., 2019), in relation to creativity. While these studies provide valuable insights into the asso­ ciation between overall curiosity or isolated curiosity dimensions and creative outcomes, they do not clarify which curiosity dimensions are most strongly linked to creativity. Given that curiosity is a multidi­ mensional trait, different curiosity dimensions may be differentially related to creativity. For example, Koutstaal et al. (2022) found that question asking and topic-related information foraging (two behavioral measures of curiosity), together with creative performance, were asso­ ciated with trait-based interest-type curiosity but not deprivation-type curiosity, highlighting meaningful differences in how distinct curiosity types relate to creativity. Nevertheless, that study primarily focused on contrasting the associations of joyous exploration and deprivation sensitivity with creativity, without examining the roles of other curiosity dimensions. Accordingly, it is necessary to comprehensively examine the relationships between multiple curiosity traits and creativity.

From an individual difference perspective, prior research has rarely adopted a person-centered approach to explore how combinations of curiosity traits relate to creativity. Although latent profile analyses (LPA) have identified four groups characterized by distinct combina­ tions of curiosity traits, which differ in life satisfaction, well-being, and mental health (Birenbaum et al., 2019; Kashdan, 2018), the corre­ sponding differences in creativity across these profiles remain largely unexplored.

Building on the 5DCR, the present study employed both variablecentered and person-centered approaches to systematically examine the relationships between distinct curiosity dimensions, their combina­ tory profiles, and creativity. Firstly, we used multiple regression ana­ lyses to identify which curiosity traits are most strongly associated with creative outcomes. Secondly, latent profile analysis (LPA) was employed to identify potential combinations of the five curiosity dimensions across individuals and to explore differences in creativity across these profiles. Theoretically, this study aims to deepen our understanding of how the multidimensional nature of curiosity relates to creativity, addressing the core question of which types of curiosity are most conducive to creative performance. Practically, the findings have implications for the culti­ vation of creative talent and educational interventions. Identifying cu­ riosity traits and combinations that most effectively promote creativity can inform strategies to intentionally foster curiosity that supports cre­ ative thinking in educational and organizational contexts.

2. Method

2.1. Participants

A total of 465 undergraduate students from the authors' university were recruited for this study, which was conducted in a face-to-face, laboratory-based setting. All participants completed the full set of measurement instruments in a fixed sequence. Four participants were excluded due to missing data on at least one measure, resulting in a final sample of 461 participants (207 males, 254 females), aged between 17 and 27 years (M = 18.02, SD = 0.69). Participants received course credit for their participation and provided informed consent prior to the start of the study. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Department of Psychology at the authors' university. The study was not preregistered.

2.2. Measures

2.2.1. Five-dimensional curiosity scale revised(5DCR) To measure different curiosity traits, we administered the Chineselanguage version of the Five-Dimensional Curiosity Scale Revised (5DCR), which consists of 24 items assessing six dimensions: Joyous Exploration, Deprivation Sensitivity, Stress Tolerance, Thrill Seeking, Overt Social Curiosity, and Covert Social Curiosity. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “Does not describe me at all”, 7 = “Completely describes me”)(Kashdan et al., 2020). In the present study, the overall scale also showed good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.86), and each subscale demonstrated good reliability (all Cronbach's αs > 0.70; see Table 1).

2.3. Alternative uses task (AUT)

We adapted the Alternative Uses Task (AUT) to assess divergent thinking, which is considered a core component of creativity (Runco & Acar, 2012). Following our previous studies(Chen et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2016), participants were asked to generate as many unique and inter­ esting uses as possible for two common objects (a box and a can) within 6 min (3 min per object). Each task was evaluated on three dimen­ sions—originality (the novelty of ideas), flexibility (the variety of idea categories), and fluency (the total number of responses)—by four trained raters using a 5-point rating scale. The average inter-rater re­ liabilities across the two tasks were 0.79 for originality, 0.87 for flexi­ bility, and 1.00 for fluency (see Table 1).

2.4. Data analysis

2.4.1. Multiple linear regression analysis To compare the relative importance of the five curiosity traits in predicting creativity, we conducted multiple linear regression analyses using SPSS (version 27.0.1). Five creativity outcomes—creative activ­ ities (CAct), creative achievements (CAch), and the originality, flexi­ bility, and fluency scores from the AUT—served as dependent variables in the regression analyses. Given that the CAct and CAch scores were positively skewed, a signed log transformation was applied to reduce skewness and better approximate normality. There was no multi­ collinearity issue among the curiosity traits (all VIFs <2). We used the standardized regression coefficients (β) to evaluate the relative contri­ bution of each curiosity trait (Cohen et al., 2003; Keith, 2019).

2.4.2. 3. Results

3.1. Variable-centered results

3.1.1. Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis As shown in Table 1, correlations between curiosity traits and CAct (r = 0.15–0.42) and CAch (r = 0.12–0.31) were all positive and signif­ icant. For divergent thinking performance, correlations with originality (r = 0.00–0.12), flexibility (r = 0.08–0.14), and fluency (r = 0.07–0.16) were generally weaker, with some reaching significance and others not.

3.1.2. Results of multiple linear regression analysis As shown in Table 2, we compared the standardized regression co­ efficients (β) to evaluate the relative contribution of each trait to different creativity outcomes. For CAct, Stress Tolerance and Joyous Exploration not only reached statistical significance but also showed the highest standardized coefficients, emerging as the two strongest pre­ dictors (β = 0.24, 95% CI [0.10, 0.32], p < .001); β = 0.24, 95% CI [0.15, 0.33], p < .001, respectively), the same pattern was observed for CAch (β = 0.21, 95% CI [0.13, 0.34], p < .001; β = 0.16, 95% CI [0.06, 0.25], p < .01, respectively). Additionally, Thrill Seeking also showed a weaker but still significant positive association with CAct (β = 0.11, 95% CI [0.02, 0.21], p = .016). No predictors were significantly associated with originality in the divergent thinking task (all p's > 0.05). For flexibility and fluency, only Stress Tolerance showed significant positive effects (β = 0.15, 95% CI [0.05, 0.25], p < .01 and β = 0.17, 95% CI [0.07, 0.27], p < .001, respectively).

Interestingly, “Avoidant Explorers” (high Deprivation Sensitivity, high Joyous Exploration, low Stress Tolerance), despite high scores in exploratory traits, demonstrated lower creativity due to limited stress tolerance. This pattern reflects real-world phenomena in which some individuals exhibit high curiosity but fail to translate it into creative behavior. Research suggests that creative processes rely on selfregulation, where cognitive flexibility allows broad exploration of ideas, and cognitive persistence enables evaluation, refinement, and implementation in real-world contexts (Nijstad et al., 2010). Further, self-regulatory capacities, facilitate the transformation of initial ideas into tangible creative outcomes (Zieli´nska et al., 2023, 2025). Stress Tolerance represents a prototypical self-regulatory trait: individuals high in Stress Tolerance maintain emotional stability and focus on problem-solving even under complex or challenging conditions, thereby supporting creative behavior and achievement. For example, research has shown that individuals' willingness to take intellectual risks mod­ erates the relationship between creative confidence and creative behavior. Without such risk-taking, high creative confidence may not translate into creative action (Beghetto et al., 2021). In practical terms, these findings raise an important question: how can curiosity be more effectively transformed into creativity? To date, few studies have sys­ tematically addressed this issue. Insights in this area could offer valuable theoretical support for enhancing creativity, particularly in educational settings where fostering creativity is a central goal.

5. Limitations and future directions

However, several limitations of this study should be noted. First, the sample characteristics are somewhat constrained. The participant pool was relatively limited in size and predominantly composed of young university students from a Chinese cultural context, which may affect the external validity of the findings. Future research could address this limitation by increasing sample size and expanding the age range and cultural backgrounds of participants to examine the generalizability of the results. Second, the present study primarily relied on cross-sectional correlational analyses; therefore, the observed associations between curiosity traits and creativity indicators should not be interpreted as causal. In addition, curiosity traits in the present study were assessed solely through self-report questionnaires, without incorporating behavioral measures such as question asking, topic-related information foraging, or other task-based indicators of exploratory behavior (Koutstaal et al., 2022). In conclusion, based on the 5DCR construct, this study revealed that Stress Tolerance and Joyous Exploration were the two strongest pre­ dictors of creativity.
