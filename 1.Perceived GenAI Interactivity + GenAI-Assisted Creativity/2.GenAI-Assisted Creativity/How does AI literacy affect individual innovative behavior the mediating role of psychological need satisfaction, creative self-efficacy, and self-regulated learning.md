How does AI literacy affect individual innovative behavior: the mediating role of psychological need satisfaction, creative self-efficacy, and self-regulated learning

Yu Ji1 · Mingxuan Zhong1 · Siyan Lyu1 · Tingting Li1 · Shijing Niu1 · Zehui Zhan1

Received: 15 October 2024 / Accepted: 7 February 2025 © The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2025

Abstract The emergence of generative artificial intelligence (GAI) has significantly trans­ formed learning patterns and innovative approaches. Human-machine (generative artificial intelligence) co-creation will become the norm, necessitating that learn­ ers possess the requisite AI literacy (AIL) to adapt to this shift. The mechanisms by which individual AIL influences innovative behavior (IB) in human-machine collaborative environments remain unclear. Therefore, this study, grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), employs a snowball sampling method to sur­ vey 460 university students, utilizing structural equation modeling to analyze the relationships among their AI literacy (AIL), psychological need satisfaction (PNS), creative self-efficacy (CSE), self-regulated learning (SRL), and innovative behavior (IB). The results indicate that AIL does not directly influence learners’ IB. PNS, CSE, and SRL serve as mediators between AIL and IB. Furthermore, AIL influences learners’ PNS and CSE in innovative activities, which in turn affects their use of SRL to adjust their innovation processes, thereby promoting the application of GAI in generating IB.

Keywords  Generative artificial intelligence · AI literacy · Psychological needs satisfaction · Creative self-efficacy · Self-regulated learning · Innovative behavior

1  Introduction

In the post-pandemic era, blended learning has facilitated the creation of a more ubiq­ uitous and inclusive learning environment for students (Singh et al., 2021). However, the innovation process is inherently uncertain, generative, and complex. Innovation

relies on a substantial supply of information, concept integration, and distant transfer (Beaty et al., 2019; Urban et al., 2024). Learners typically rely on traditional methods such as journal searches, expert consultations, and web searches to acquire infor­ mation and resources (Wasim et al., 2024). However, these methods pose several challenges: the inefficiency in information retrieval results in the inability to receive timely support, confusion over the authenticity of information leading to cognitive conflicts, and the absence of diverse perspectives restricts divergent thinking (Yu, 2022; Ma et al., 2021). This can hinder the effective fulfillment of learners’ psycho­ logical needs for innovation and intrinsic motivation, thus making creativity develop­ ment more challenging.

The advent of generative artificial intelligence (GAI) has provided tremendous opportunities for personal innovation. Multimodal large language models exhibit strong anthropomorphic characteristics, embedding themselves into the entire learn­ ing process as quasi-agents (Li et al., 2024). GAI can learn from various sources and modalities to generate new data using this knowledge, enabling learners to receive multisensory experiences and informational feedback while engaging in personalized learning and creative problem-solving (Haleem et al., 2022). In the future, humanmachine collaborative learning will become the norm, necessitating that learners pos­ sess the requisite AI literacy (AIL) to adapt to this change. Research indicates that students with higher levels of AIL exhibit better performance in areas such as learn­ ing motivation (Schiavo et al., 2024), self-efficacy (Washington, 2023), and metacog­ nitive regulation (Xia et al., 2023). UNESCO (2024) has emphasized that cultivating creative citizens will be a primary goal of education in the AI era. It is evident that the level of AIL may influence individual Innovative behavior (IB) performance.

Nonetheless, human-machine collaborative learning encounters risks like cogni­ tive inflexibility, emotional reliance, and learning delegation (Li et al., 2024). Thus, students need to adopt specific strategies to actively leverage GAI in supporting and participating in the entire learning process, a skill referred to as self-regulated learning (SRL). SRL is a self-directed process in which learners transform their psychological capabilities into academic skills (Zimmerman, 2002). According to Self-Determina­ tion Theory (SDT), motivation stems from three fundamental psychological needs satisfaction (PNS) (autonomy, competence, and relatedness), and fulfilling these needs helps explain how students can engage their metacognitive skills via GAI for self-regulation and meaningful innovation (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Additionally, cre­ ative self-efficacy (CSE) can inspire learners to adopt a more proactive exploratory attitude when confronting novel and complex problems, thereby influencing their levels of SRL (Xu et al., 2024) and IB (Zielińska et al., 2021).

At present, robust evidence is lacking to substantiate the claim that GAI can amplify individual creativity (Benbya et al., 2024). Notably, the role of AIL as a prerequisite influencing individuals’ use of GAI tools to generate IB has been over­ looked. Research indicates that teacher support has a significant impact on students’ CSE and IB (Su et al., 2024), but these studies primarily focus on the role of human teachers and rarely address the influence of GAI directly. Thus, it seems there is a gap regarding how PNS and CSE influence SRL and, in turn, IB (Ogunleye et al., 2024). Moreover, research on AIL and SRL has largely focused on system or appli­ cation development (Järvelä et al., 2023; Casal-Otero et al., 2023), often neglecting

the psychological impacts of need satisfaction and self-efficacy (Xia et al., 2023). In summary, AIL can influence learners’ IB; however, this process may not be direct and could be affected by psychological and metacognitive factors. Thus, this research seeks to investigate how learners’ PNS, CSE, and SRL contribute to the relationship between AIL and IB, aiming to offer theoretical backing and practical insights for human-machine co-creation.

2  Literature review

2.1  AI literacy

The advent of GAI signifies the onset of the strong AI era, with AI literacy (AIL) becoming an increasingly crucial issue (Cheng et al., 2024; UNESCO, 2024). AIL is an essential element for individuals to utilize AI and develop a collaborative relation­ ship with the technology, significantly equipping them for future learning and work. Long and Magerko (2020) describe AIL as a set of abilities that enable individuals to use AI to change the way they communicate, live, and work. Ng et al. (2021) argue that AIL should encompass understanding and comprehension of AI, its usage and application, evaluation and creation of AI, as well as ethical considerations related to AI. The “Student AI Competency Framework” released by UNESCO (2024) cat­ egorizes students’ AI competencies into four areas: human-centred mindset, ethics of AI, AI techniques and applications, and AI system design, with these competencies spanning three progressive levels: understand, apply, and create. In summary, AIL is a multidimensional concept that encompasses not only the understanding, usage, and evaluation of AI technology but also awareness of ethical issues related to AI. Learners with AIL not only a comprehensive understanding of the core concepts, principles, and practical applications of AI products but also the ability to identify potential biases and risks, critically evaluating the reliability of information (Li et al., 2024; Chiu et al., 2024a, b). This capability enables them to avoid excessive reliance on AI products and to utilize AI products responsibly for problem-solving.

With the widespread adoption of GAI, it is increasingly utilized to enhance pro­ ductivity, accelerate innovation, and facilitate personalized learning. A learner’s own level of AIL influences how GAI is utilized and its potential benefits. Wang et al. (2024a) found that AIL positively affects students’ attitudes toward GAI technol­ ogy and their perceived behavioral control, with students possessing high levels of AIL being more willing to collaborate with Schiavo et al. (2024) discovered that understanding how AI operates and how to use it can enhance learners’ acceptance of GAI. It is evident that enhancing learners’ AIL can ensure their irreplaceable core competitiveness in the era of GAI. Currently, research has focused on improving the structure of AIL (Chiu et al., 2024a, b), developing measurement scales (Yuan et al., 2024), and examining the relationships between AIL and technology acceptance (Jain & Raghuram, 2024) as well as computational thinking (Celik, 2023). In the context of human-machine collaboration, there are still research gaps, such as the limited exploration of the relationships between AIL and psychological aspects (Shen & Cui, 2024), creativity (Lee et al., 2024), and metacognitive skills (Xia et al., 2023). There­

fore, this study aims to investigate how AIL influences learners’ metacognitive skills (such as SRL) through psychological dimensions (like PNS and CSE), subsequently impacting individual creativity (such as IB).

2.2  Psychological needs satisfaction

In the process of human-machine interaction, self-determined behavior begins with the information input of GAI and the individual’s need structure. In essence, psy­ chological need satisfaction (PNS) is a motivational aspect that includes autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Van den Broeck et al., 2016). When these needs are sat­ isfied, they foster intrinsic motivation and well-being, leading to increased engage­ ment and sustained effort in learning. Conversely, unmet needs may impede these processes. Therefore, PNS is defined as the extent to which individuals achieve self-motivation, social interaction, and mental health by fulfilling their intrinsic psy­ chological needs within a social environment (Ryan & Deci, 2000). In highly inter­ active and autonomous learning environments, fulfilling basic psychological needs strengthens self-regulatory motivation, resulting in greater engagement in learning tasks and enhanced creativity and analytical skills (Hsu et al., 2019). When faced with challenging innovation tasks, individuals are better equipped to regulate their emotions and experience fewer negative emotional states (e.g., anxiety, frustration, depression) and stress responses (Roth et al., 2019).

AI products primarily alter individuals’ PNS through emotional support and social interaction (Pauw et al., 2022). For example, AI voice assistants provide positive emotional feedback and interaction, helping users regulate their social lives and personal emotions (Liu et al., 2023). Conversational chatbots provide knowledge answers to students, supporting their active self-regulation by fulfilling their PNS (Xia et al., 2023). In fact, AI products foster emotional connections during the learn­ ing process by facilitating anthropomorphic emotional interactions, enabling learn­ ers to collaborate more effectively with both human and machine partners, thereby promoting positive social relationships and a climate of innovation within groups (Pelau et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024). However, Previous research on PNS has focused on social support, particularly organizational support (Lee et al., 2020) and teacher support (Chiu et al., 2023). Han et al. (2022) found that perceived teacher support promotes students’ PNS, positively influencing their IB. However, with the “human­ ization” of technology, many studies have begun to explore how GAI satisfies indi­ vidual’s psychological needs (Chiu, 2024), yet the relationships among PNS, SRL, and IB in human-machine collaborative contexts remain underexplored.

2.3  Creative self-efficacy

Creative self-efficacy (CSE) refers to an individual’s ability to create and propose unique and valuable ideas (Tierney & Farmer, 2002). CSE significantly influences an individual’s creative performance. For instance, when learners encounter prob­ lem-solving tasks that are poorly defined, complex, and cognitively demanding, CSE significantly boosts their confidence and creativity in addressing these challenges, encouraging them to adopt innovative learning methods to accomplish specific tasks

or projects (Puente-Diaz & Cavazos‐Arroyo, 2018). In the face of failure, creative self-efficacy helps them sustain motivation and exhibit greater resilience (Newman et al., 2018). Furthermore, innovation frequently relies on collaboration, and students with higher creative self-efficacy are more likely to share ideas and take on innovative tasks within teams, thereby enhancing teamwork and creativity (Park et al., 2021).

In a human-machine collaborative environment, the impact of GAI on students’ CSE is complex and multifaceted. On the one hand, GAI tools provide learners with the opportunity to actively explore and solve problems through personalized and highly interactive instructional sequences, which aids in enhancing CSE across various creative tasks (Washington, 2023). On the other hand, the inherent deficien­ cies of GAI (such as misinformation affecting cognition, inability to handle com­ plex issues, and irrelevant responses) can negatively impact learners’ experiences, thereby reducing individual’s CSE (Li et al., 2024). Although GAI enhances indi­ vidual CSE, subsequently improving the quality, precision, and originality of solu­ tions, this effect is moderated by individual’s metacognitive abilities (Urban et al., 2024). Currently, research on CSE primarily focuses on organizational psychology, examining the effects of organizational and environmental support on employees’ CSE, with relatively few studies in educational or academic settings (Islam & Asad, 2024). Therefore, this study will explore how individual’s AIL influences CSE and SRL in innovative activities within a collaborative environment, thereby facilitating the occurrence of individual’s IB.

2.4  Self-regulated learning

Self-regulated learning (SRL) refers to the ability of learners to actively engage in the learning process, set goals, monitor progress, and adjust strategies to achieve desired outcomes (Zimmerman, 2002). Regardless of the nature of the learning process, all learners inevitably engage in some form of self-regulation. Students with stronger self-regulatory abilities demonstrate greater persistence in learning and enhanced stress-coping skills (Namaziandost et al., 2023). They generate self-directed feed­ back that guides cognition and motivation, thereby ensuring the effectiveness of problem-solving. Especially when learning tasks are highly demanding or when prior knowledge is limited, learners often require external support (e.g., peer support or teacher assistance) to effectively engage in self-regulated learning (Xia et al., 2023). However, this support is typically coupled with external oversight and cannot always be available to meet students’ needs anytime and anywhere (Boekaerts, 1999).

GAI, through API, facilitates the ubiquitous “human-machine-object” inter­ connection, which can take over certain self-regulation activities of learners and, depending on the learners’ level of self-regulation, flexibly adjust the distribution of tasks, delegating some tasks to the machine or to the learner themselves as needed (Jin et al., 2023). For example, the SRLbot developed by Ng et al. (2024) can adjust its responses based on students’ learning conditions and SRL scenarios, and its per­ sonalized recommendations and flexibility are well-received by students. Similar study is found in the research of Wang and Lin (2023), focusing on the development of AI applications and programs to improve learners’ ability to reflect on, regulate, and control their learning processes. Moreover, studies have shown that ChatGPT-

assisted learning can significantly improve learners’ self-regulated learning and miti­ gate the negative effects of improper GAI usage on individual creativity (Lee et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2024). However, other studies present contrasting findings, argu­ ing that the substitutive nature of GAI products may foster learner dependency and cognitive inertia (Li et al., 2024), underscoring the necessity for learners to actively regulate the “human-machine co-creation” process. Therefore, this study attempts to explore how learners’ SRL in a human-machine collaborative environment influ­ ences their IB in innovative activities.

2.5  Innovative behavior

Innovation is a multi-phase process encompassing the generation and implementa­ tion of ideas, with distinct individual activities and behaviors at each stage (Scott & Bruce, 1994). Innovative behavior (IB) refers to an individual’s ability to demon­ strate new ideas, methods, and products in learning or work, reflecting their creativity (Salsabila & Mansyur, 2024). Currently, research on innovation behavior primarily focuses on the workplace, investigating the influence of self-efficacy and various personal and environmental factors (such as organizational climate) on innovation behavior (Balkar, 2015; Thurlings et al., 2015). With the advancement of modern information technology, an increasing number of studies have focused on the impact of technological support on innovation behavior (Yin et al., 2024). Additionally, the collaboration between individuals and technology, as well as personal information and digital literacy, can also influence an individual’s innovation behavior (creativity) (Yu, 2022). In higher education, university students play a crucial role in entrepre­ neurial incubation, research output, and product development. Learners’ innovative behavior contributes to the efficient completion of tasks by organizations, groups, and themselves, while also fostering the novelty of individual’s ideas, outcomes, and solutions.

Fostering IB necessitates a supportive environment, and the advent of GAI offers a valuable opportunity for enhancing university students’ IB (Filippi, 2023). Chat­ GPT can combine distant but related concepts, enhancing learners’ creativity in gen­ erating ideas by accelerating cognitive processes (Lee & Chung, 2024). Although ChatGPT generates problem-solving solutions based on existing datasets, it can enhance the novelty of solutions when it generates a sufficient number of concepts, helping students go beyond a comprehensive understanding of the original problem (Filippi, 2023). However, the instant responses provided by GAI can foster tech­ nological dependence, diminishing students’ opportunities for independent thinking and exploration, thereby stifling the development of their creative thinking (Li et al., 2024; Zhai et al., 2024). On one hand, the process of innovation requires learners to actively regulate their strategies, using GAI feedback to adjust their innovation approaches, thereby aligning their behaviors with practical needs and goals. On the other hand, the effectiveness of GAI in the process of generating and implementing innovative ideas is closely tied to the learner’s psychological state, as psychological energy underpins personal agency.

To sum up, within a human-machine collaborative context, an individual’s AIL could positively influences their IB. However, there are varying conclusions on how

changes at the psychological level (such as PNS and CSE) brought about by GAI affect learners’ IB, and whether SRL can facilitate IB. Therefore, this study will explore how psychological and metacognitive variables serve as mediators in the relationship between AIL and IB in a human-machine collaborative environment.

3  Research hypotheses and framework

AIL offers learners a cognitive foundation for efficient collaborative innovation with GAI. However, human-machine interaction extends beyond the exchange of knowl­ edge and information to include the profound impact of machine feedback on the individual’s psychological dimensions, such as PNS and CSE. Furthermore, the inno­ vation process necessitates that learners adaptively utilize GAI for self-regulation in the face of challenges. Consequently, AIL, PNS, CSE, SRL, and IB are interwoven, creating a dynamic innovation cycle.

3.1  AI literacy, psychological needs satisfaction and creative self-efficacy

Learners with high levels of AIL can better utilize the personalized and real-time interactive features of GAI, making the learning process more aligned with indi­ vidual’s needs and preferences, thus enhancing autonomy and increasing a sense of competence (Liang et al., 2023). As learners’ PNS is satisfied, their emotional well-being also enhances, motivating them to participate more actively in learning endeavors (Pauw et al., 2022). Related research confirms that AIL enhances learn­ ers’ perceived ease of use and usefulness, thereby increasing their PNS (Schiavo et al., 2024). However, some studies indicate that excessive reliance on AI tools (due to low AIL) may diminish learners’ autonomy and independence, leading to reduced satisfaction with GAI’s ability to meet their needs (Du & Alm, 2024). Based on this, we hypothesize H1:

H1  AIL is positively related to PNS.

Learners with higher AIL may have greater proficiency in using GAI and a better understanding of its algorithms and potential, allowing them to interact effectively with GAI to gain new ideas or unexpected perspectives (Leong, 2023). Empirical results indicate that the creative support and successful experiences provided by GAI products can bolster learners’ confidence in their problem-solving, thereby enhanc­ ing their CSE (Washington, 2023). However, Improper use of GAI, along with the inherent “flaws” of the technology, can negatively impact learners’ satisfaction with information retrieval (Li et al., 2024). Specifically, when individuals place excessive trust in the machine’s perspectives, the novelty and quality of the solutions may not be assured or acknowledged, hindering the development of their CSE (McGuire et al., 2024). Therefore, we propose the hypothesis H2:

H2  AIL is positively related to CSE.

3.2  AI literacy, self-regulated learning and innovative behavior

AIL is gradually becoming a core factor influencing learners’ SRL. Shen and Ho (2020) argue that AIL can enhance learners’ adaptability to GAI products, by actively selecting and applying GAI tools, learners can regulate and optimize their learning processes, thus improving both the efficiency and quality of their learning. Moreover, in both formal and informal learning settings, learners effectively leverage real-time data and feedback provided by GAI technologies to proactively adjust learning strat­ egies, support behaviors, and learning plans (Ng et al., 2024; Huang & Lin, 2024). This proactivity is a critical characteristic of self-regulated learning. Research has shown that learners’ AI knowledge can influence both the effectiveness and quality of SRL (Xia et al., 2023). However, AI knowledge, as a subdimension of AIL, does not fully encapsulate how AIL influences learners’ SRL. Hence, we hypothesize H3:

H3  AIL is positively related to SRL.

A study by Lin et al. (2023) found that individuals with higher information literacy are better able to demonstrate creativity in their work. In the context of GAI product application, learners with elevated AIL can effectively leverage AI tools for generat­ ing ideas and solving problems, which in turn promotes IB (Wang et al., 2023). None­ theless, AIL affects IB through multiple pathways. For instance, AI awareness can both suppress innovative behavior by increasing individuals’ emotional exhaustion and enhance creative ability by stimulating intrinsic motivation (Liang et al., 2022). Consequently, we hypothesize H4:

H4  AIL is positively related to IB.

3.3  Psychological needs satisfaction, self-regulated learning and innovative behavior

The leap in GAI products in digital production, interaction, service, and interactive capabilities may provide learners with enjoyable experiences and satisfaction during responsive interactions (Celik, 2023). In particular, GAI can tailor learning content and resources to meet learners’ needs, thereby enhancing their sense of autonomy; gain innovation or success through feedback provided by GAI to fulfill learners’ need for competence; The interactivity and collaborative features of GAI will enhance learners’ relational needs. Confirmed results show a significant relationship between PNS and learners’ SRL (Xia et al., 2023). While GAI products can promote learner engagement in SRL by fulfilling their PNS, their “feeding” capacity might also result in learners opting out (Chiu, 2024). Thus, we hypothesize H5:

H5  PNS is positively related to SRL.

In a technology-supported environment, IB typically requires a high degree of auton­ omy, competence, and belongingness (Fan et al., 2022). Meeting these needs fos­ ters learners’ IB, as it can trigger individual’s creativity and problem-solving skills

(De Jonge et al., 2018). For example, GAI satisfies learners’ basic psychological needs by providing knowledge answers, instant feedback, and facilitating interaction. Research has confirmed that with GAI’s support, the satisfaction of psychological needs transforms into students’ intrinsic motivation, which in turn triggers learners’ IB and reveals individuals’ creativity (Urban et al., 2024). Thus, we hypothesize H6:

H6  PNS is positively related to IB.

3.4  Creative self-efficacy, self-regulated learning and innovative behavior

CSE can motivate individuals to engage in and persist in creative tasks through behav­ ioral achievements, verbal persuasion, and emotional arousal (Bandura & Adams, 1977). With the assistance of GAI, learners respond to problems more flexibly and achieve success (behavioral achievement); GAI’s suggestions, counsel, and guidance can help learners adjust their task strategies (verbal persuasion); GAI’s supportive language and emotional bonds with learners can help them approach challenges with a positive emotional state (emotional arousal) (Li et al., 2024). Given the uncertainty of human-machine co-creation, learners with elevated CSE are more likely to prac­ tice self-monitoring, reflection, and strategy adjustment. Empirical results indicate a positive correlation between CSE and SRL (Xu et al., 2024). Thus, we hypothesize H7:

H7  CSE is positively related to SRL.

The potential of AI to replace human roles highlights the importance of creative work for individuals. The “materialization” of creative ideas requires individuals to pos­ sess sufficient CSE to utilize technology and tools to expedite this process. Accord­ ing to Bandura (1990), individuals draw on direct and indirect experiences to inform their sense of self-efficacy. Thus, GAI primarily arouses learners’ internal emotions by showcasing a variety of information, knowledge, and viewpoints, encouraging them to embrace risks in the innovation process and trust their capacity to generate creative results with GAI. As learners realize incremental innovations via GAI, these successful experiences bolster their CSE, thereby promoting subsequent IB (Michael et al. 2011). Thus, we hypothesize H8:

H8  CSE is positively related to IB.

3.5  Self-regulated learning and innovative behavior

Technology-enhanced learning environments provide learners ample opportunities to regulate their innovation processes. For instance, learners utilize GAI to pre­ cisely define their innovation objectives, seek external resource support, and record problems and answers throughout the innovation process, thus aiding learners in reflecting on the creative process and minimizing interruptions in the self-regulation implementation stage (Wu et al., 2024). Additionally, there are divergent conclusions regarding the ability of SRL to foster IB. Travers et al. (2003) found that individu­

als demonstrate increased flexibility through SRL, which is a key component of IB. However, Kittel and Seufert (2023) found that SRL only positively predicts IB in terms of effort regulation, and excessive focus on specific aspects of the task can prevent SRL from being activated. In fact, there is still a lack of sufficient evidence to show that SRL positively impacts individual’s innovative behavior. Therefore, we propose the hypothesis H9:

H9  SRL is positively related to IB.

3.6  Research Model

In the era of human-machine symbiosis, individuals must possess a certain level of AIL to engage in innovative activities with machines more effectively. Creation is a dynamic, self-regulated process (Li et al., 2021). Based on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), learners’ use of GAI is limited by the prerequisite of AIL, yet humans have social characteristics, meaning that individual behaviors are often affected by psychological and metacognitive levels (Conner & Armitage, 1998). In other words, the level of AIL can influence individual’s IB through mediating variables such as CSE (Michael et al. 2011), PNS (Schiavo et al., 2024), and SRL (Xia et al., 2023). With the application and popularization of GAI products, meaningful collaborative innovation between humans and machines will become the norm. 4.2  Procedure

This study adhered to a rigorous process encompassing questionnaire design, distri­ bution, data processing, and analysis, ensuring both the standardization and scientific integrity of the research, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

In the questionnaire design phase, to ensure content validity, the measurement items for the five variables—AIL, SRL, PNS, CSE, and IB—were adapted from previous studies and revised to align with the specific context of GAI products in this research. After the questionnaire was finalized, three experts from the fields of

psychology, educational technology, and artificial intelligence were invited to assess its face validity, ensuring that each item was clearly articulated and that the overall structure of the questionnaire aligned with the intended framework. Subsequently, based on the experts’ feedback, we refined the wording, sequence, and response options of the questionnaire.

During the questionnaire distribution phase, given the “barriers” and “fairness” of technology, there remains a gap in the normalization of human-machine collaborative learning. Thus, the selection of research participants is based on two criteria: (1) Par­ ticipants must have experience using GAI at least weekly, participate voluntarily, and fully comprehend the specific meanings included in the items; (2) They must be cur­ rently using GAI to solve complex problems with uncertain initial and target states, such as product design, academic writing, or project planning. Next, we invited two students to complete a pilot version of the questionnaire and made revisions to items that were unclear or difficult to understand, ensuring that the questionnaire was both readable and easy to interpret. Subsequently, we distributed the questionnaire via snowball sampling on the “So Jump” platform (https://www.wjx.cn/). After ​c​o​m​p​l​e​ t​i​n​g the questionnaire, participants who met the criteria were invited to recruit addi­ tional eligible individuals to complete the questionnaire, ensuring the reliability and authenticity of the data. Informed consent was obtained for the survey, ensuring that all participants were aware they were taking part in an evaluative study and that their data would remain anonymous and confidential. The procedures outlined above adhere to the ethical standards for research involving human participants established by the American Psychological Association.

During the data processing phase, the two university students completed the trial questionnaire in 111 s and 132 s, respectively. Therefore, the questionnaire screening criteria included samples that were completed in under 110 s and had a data standard deviation of 0. Subsequently, based on this criterion, we removed invalid question­ naires and converted qualitative data (e.g., categorical data) into quantitative data, such as coding “gender” as 0 (female) and 1 (male).

During the data analysis phase, we randomly divided the collected data into two subsets: one for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) (n = 215) and the other for confir­ matory factor analysis (CFA) (n = 245). This approach was used to assess the reliabil­ ity and validity of the questionnaire and to explore potential relationships between the variables.

4.3  Questionnaire

The questionnaire was designed using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree, covering AIL, SRL, PNS, CSE, and IB. Participants selected the option that best reflected their actual situation.

4.3.1  AI literacy

This study utilized 8 items from the AIL scale developed by Wang et al. (2022) (e.g., “I can proficiently use AI applications or products to assist my daily learning and work”; “I can select appropriate solutions from various options provided by intelli­

gent agents”). The scale was used to evaluate participants’ AIL, where higher scores reflect a greater level of AIL.

4.3.2  Psychological needs satisfaction

This study utilized 9 items from the “PNS Supported by Chatbot” scale developed by Xia et al. (2023) to assess changes in learners’ PNS in a human-machine collab­ orative environment (e.g., “I feel a great sense of autonomy in the learning process when using GAI products”, “I feel a sense of achievement when learning with GAI products”). Higher scores on this dimension indicate a higher level of PNS among participants.

4.3.3  Creative self-efficacy

This study utilized a 4-item CSE scale adapted from Gong et al. (2009) (e.g., “I feel skilled at coming up with new ideas when interacting with GAI products”, “I excel at developing my own ideas from those provided by GAI products”) to measure individuals’ beliefs in their ability to generate novel, original, and practical ideas, solutions, or actions. Higher scores on this dimension indicate a higher level of CSE among participants.

4.3.4  Self-regulated learning

The process and strategies of SRL may change with variations in the learning context. Therefore, this study developed an initial 7-item scale based on the SRL scale for blended learning environments by Sha et al. (2012) and the SRL scale for Chatbotsupported learning developed by Xia et al. (2023). (e.g., “When learning with GAI products, I set reasonable short-term or long-term goals based on different learning tasks and break them down into manageable steps to achieve them one by one.“). A higher score in this dimension reflects a greater ability in SRL.

4.3.5  Innovative behavior

This study uses the IB scale developed by Janssen (2000), which includes the stages of idea generation, idea promotion, and idea implementation. We use 6 items in this scale (e.g., “When I encounter difficulties, I often use GAI products to generate new ideas”, “I seek new methods, techniques, or tools through GAI products”). Higher scores in this dimension indicate better IB outcomes for individuals.

6  Discussion

In the intelligent era, the coupling and reshaping of human-machine relationships will have a significant impact on innovation activities. This study conducts an indepth analysis of the relationships among AI literacy (AIL), psychological needs satisfaction (PNS), creative self-efficacy (CSE), self-regulated learning (SRL), and innovative behavior (IB), leading to the following conclusions:

The overall scores for the five dimensions of AIL, PNS, CSE, SRL, and IB are significantly above the critical value of 3 (see Table 2). Among them, AIL scored the highest (3.97), indicating that the participants have certain knowledge and skills in the field of artificial intelligence, which is closely related to the popularity and application of GAI products in the field of education in recent years (Cheng et al., 2024), and users of high-frequency technology will show a higher AIL. The scores for PNS and SRL are 3.73 and 3.70, respectively, indicating a high level. Accord­ ing to SDT, the anthropomorphism and intelligence of GAI products can satisfy the psychological needs of learners, making it easier for them to develop effective SRL, which aligns with the conclusions of Xia et al. (2023) and Chiu (2024). The score for CSE (3.57) is relatively low, suggesting that learners may lack collective recognition of the novelty of the innovative concepts generated by their use of GAI products, or that the inherent “deficiencies” of GAI products diminish learners’ confidence in generating creative ideas and outcomes, which is consistent with findings by Doshi & Hauser (2024) and Duong et al. (2024). Additionally, the score for IB (3.67) is

relatively low, indicating that GAI products are influenced by multiple factors in innovation activities, and interventions at the psychological and metacognitive levels may help facilitate IB.

H1 and H2 are strongly supported, indicating that AIL is a significant predictor of both PNS and CSE. Learners’ perceptions of GAI’s ease of use and functionality positively influence their sense of autonomy, competence, and relatedness during the innovation process, which, in turn, encourages their intention to continue using the technology (Xia et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). Specifically, students with higher AIL have a clear understanding of the functionalities and limitations of GAI, enabling them to effectively use GAI to enhance their sense of autonomy in the innovation process, achieve a sense of competence in obtaining innovative results, and fulfill relational needs through collaboration with GAI. In regard to H2, the interactive capabilities of GAI products enable students to actively explore and solve problems, which, in turn, helps learners confidently advance the generation and implementa­ tion of ideas in innovation activities, leading to enhanced CSE. Research suggests that learners with high levels of AIL are better able to engage in human-machine collaboration, employing strategies such as prompting and multi-turn dialogue to elicit diverse ideas and perspectives from ChatGPT, as well as receiving timely diag­ nostics and feedback on their own ideas, concepts, and solutions (Washington, 2023; Li et al., 2024). Therefore, individuals with higher AIL are likely to be more adept at utilizing AI technology to support creative activities, believing in their ability to effectively use GAI to generate and propose unique and valuable ideas.

Hypothesis H3 is supported, indicating that AIL can significantly positively pre­ dict learners’ SRL. Studies suggest that students with a better grasp of how GAI products work are more capable of employing active strategies to engage with GAI, acquiring pertinent information and resources to address problems (Huang & Lin, 2024). As GAI products rely on historical datasets to generate outputs, learners must engage in continuous fine-tuning and multiple rounds of interaction to gradually guide GAI to provide the information and perspectives they seek, which exemplifies AIL. Furthermore, learners with high AIL can actively set learning goals and moni­ tor their progress through GAI, which allows them to promptly identify cognitive blind spots and engage in collaborative reflection and evaluation with the machine, continuously improving their learning strategies (Ng et al., 2024). This is consis­ tent with similar research indicating that digital literacy positively predicts SRL (Yu, 2022). Interestingly, hypothesis H4 is not supported, suggesting that AIL does not positively predict IB, which contradicts the findings of Lin et al. (2023) and Wang et al. (2023). Innovation behavior is inherently a complex process, encompassing stages such as idea generation, dissemination, and implementation. While GAI can provide input and feedback for creative ideas, innovation behavior is not solely dependent on information acquisition and processing; it also involves more complex social and psychological factors, such as risk-taking, resilience and intrinsic motivation in the face of failure (Kör et al., 2021). It is crucial to note that a strong understanding of AI may not necessarily foster students’ self-regulated learning. On one hand, GAI might not alter their cognitive and perceptual approach to learning, and on the other hand, excessive reliance on GAI’s suggestions and automated outputs could stifle learners’

creative thinking, resulting in a lack of innovation when addressing complex prob­ lems (Niloy et al., 2024).

Both H5 and H6 are validated, indicating that PNS positively predicts SRL and IB. When the supportive environment provided by GAI effectively meets learners’ PNS, it can significantly promote SRL (Chiu, 2022). GAI provides various resources, feed­ back, plans, and perspectives for learners’ innovative activities, engaging in dialogue from multiple identities to facilitate the generation of creative outcomes (Ji et al., 2023). This fosters a sense of autonomy, relevance, and accomplishment in students, making them more inclined to adopt active learning strategies such as goal setting, progress monitoring, and strategy adjustment. This aligns with the findings of Xia et al. (2023), which indicate that PNS positively predicts SRL. Regarding H6, IB is a product of the complex interaction between the individual and the environment. First, GAI fosters learner autonomy by providing tailored responses and resources, allowing learners to control their learning pace based on their interests, while also adapting learning paths according to their needs and goals. Second, GAI facilitates distal transfer by offering extensive information, providing timely personalized diag­ nostics to correct errors, and dynamically adjusting task difficulty, thus empowering learners to generate and implement new ideas (Filippi, 2023). Third, GAI engages in “humanized” emotional interactions with learners, fostering social connections and fulfilling their need for relatedness, thereby increasing their motivation to embrace challenges and bring their ideas to fruition.This aligns with previous research find­ ings, indicating that when learners perceive their three needs supported by GAI, their intrinsic motivation is activated, promoting the emergence of IB (Kong et al., 2023).

H7 and H8 have been confirmed, showing that CSE positively influences both SRL and IB. First, creativity is a dynamic self-regulatory process (Li et al., 2021). In a human-machine collaborative environment, individuals with higher CSE are more likely to leverage GAI to set clear short-term and long-term goals, plan and allocate study time, organize materials, monitor, reflect on, and evaluate their progress, while continuously adjusting strategies to achieve creative outcomes (Lee et al., 2024). Additionally, when individuals recognize their own creativity, learners tend to dem­ onstrate greater task engagement, higher self-confidence, and more effective learning strategies. They are more inclined to take on challenges and display higher SRL lev­ els. Therefore, H7 has been validated. Regarding H8, social cognitive theory posits that learners with strong CSE are more confident in generating and implementing innovative constructs (Newman et al., 2018). This is due to the fact that learners, motivated by their sense of efficacy, can draw upon adequate motivation and cogni­ tive resources to experiment boldly and continually refine their ideas (Michael et al. 2011). When learners encounter ambiguous, complex, and high-cognitive-demand problem-solving tasks, GAI supports them cognitively, emotionally, and behavior­ ally, helping them believe in their ability to produce creative outcomes. Moreover, individuals with higher CSE are better able to sustain high levels of focus for extended periods, are more willing to take risks in innovative tasks, and are more confident in pursuing innovative solutions (Papinczak et al., 2008). Thus, H8 is confirmed as well.

Hypothesis H9 has been validated, indicating that SRL significantly positively predicts IB. GAI is able to take over some of the learner’s regulatory activities and has the flexibility to transfer tasks to the machine or to itself. In the generation stage

of innovative ideas, learners utilize GAI to identify problems and nurture innovative concepts, the creation of new ideas consistently enhances individuals’ intrinsic moti­ vation and CSE (Zielińska et al., 2021), thereby prompting learners to adjust their strategies for using GAI to generate ideas through self-reflection and monitoring. In the promotion stage of innovative ideas, learners engage in continuous reflection and adjustment of task strategies, facilitating the flow of information, knowledge, and new ideas within the group, assisting in converting concepts into real solutions (Zimmerman & Martinez-Pons 1988). In the practice stage of innovative ideas, learn­ ers analyze generated learning logs and feedback using GAI, acquiring personalized resources and strategies tailored to their needs, while also monitoring and assess­ ing the innovative practice process. Thus, individuals demonstrate greater flexibility through SRL, which is an essential component of IB (Travers et al., 2003). Individu­ als with high autonomy can independently determine their behaviors and cognition, exhibiting higher levels of IB in innovative activities (Wu et al., 2014).

In terms of mediation effects, this study confirms three mediation paths: AIL→PNS→IB, AIL→CSE→IB, and AIL→SRL→IB. Given that the main effect AIL→IB is not significant, it indicates that AIL influences learners’ innovative behavior through mediation by psychological (PNS and CSE) and metacognitive (SRL) factors. In the path AIL→PNS→IB, PNS arises from human-machine collabo­ ration and functions as a mediating variable that influences individuals’ innovative behavior. When learners become proficient in using GAI for innovative activities, personalized support, emotional interactions, and dynamic feedback provided by GAI enhance their autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs (Schiavo et al., 2024). However, algorithmic limitations on user choices, path dependence, and lowquality feedback can undermine individual’s psychological needs (Chiu, 2024), ulti­ mately inhibiting the frequency of innovative behaviors. In the path AIL→CSE→IB, CSE stems from the support GAI provides during the innovation process. Individuals with high AIL possess the knowledge and skills necessary to increase the quantity, novelty, and quality of problem-solving solutions, making them more likely to gain unique perspectives and receive tailored support. They are highly motivated and con­ fident in their ability to leverage GAI to generate innovative outcomes (Michael et al. 2011; Urban et al., 2024). Additionally, given the inherent uncertainty and complex­ ity of the innovation process, individuals must actively self-regulate throughout the entire process (Zielińska et al., 2021). Individuals with high AIL are more inclined to use GAI products to co-regulate their learning process. For example, they engage in interactive dialogues with ChatGPT to explore innovative methods, including effec­ tive time management, goal setting, and self-assessment. These strategies enable learners to demonstrate enhanced creativity when tackling complex problems (Ng et al., 2024). Therefore, SRL may also act as a mediating variable between AIL and IB.

Additionally, this study confirms two chained mediation paths: AIL→PNS→SRL→IB and AIL→CSE→SRL→IB. For individuals with social attributes, external changes can provoke psychological shifts, influencing their metacognitive processes and motivating behavioral changes. AIL, as a new form of competence arising from human-machine collaboration, impacts the efficiency and quality of individuals’ innovation activities facilitated by technology. Machine feed­ back and support can induce changes in learners’ basic needs and intrinsic motivation.

However, individuals with high AIL are more adept at using GAI products to receive effective feedback and support, thereby strengthening their psychological needs and intrinsic motivation. Research indicates that learners whose basic needs are fulfilled also exhibit higher self-efficacy. By consciously and purposefully regulating the inno­ vation process, they enhance the likelihood of demonstrating innovative behavior in real-world contexts (Duong, 2024; Wang et al., 2024b). These findings deepen the understanding of how to effectively apply GAI in human-machine collaboration for innovation, with particular attention to the psychological and metacognitive impacts.

7  Conclusion, limitation and future study

This study explores the relationships among AI literacy (AIL), psychological needs satisfaction (PNS), creative self-efficacy (CSE), self-regulated learning (SRL), and innovative behavior (IB) in the process of college students utilizing GAI for inno­ vative activities. Findings indicate that AIL does not directly impact IB; instead, it influences IB via the mediators PNS, CSE, and SRL. In the wake of the AI wave, pro­ hibiting attempts to engage with emerging technologies is destined to fail, and every­ one must possess a certain level of AIL to share, co-create, and collaborate with GAI.

Firstly, AIL serves as the foundation for individual innovative effectiveness. In the field of innovation, humans and machines occupy distinct roles, and their capabil­ ity coupling can enhance creativity. The research findings suggest that students can enhance their AIL through human-machine interaction, learners should closely fol­ low the latest advancements in GAI, and more effectively utilize these technologies to enhance their personal creativity by mastering GAI’s core concepts, operational con­ ditions, capabilities, and application areas (Wu et al., 2023). It is crucial to recognize that unequal access to GAI could lead to digital divide challenges, which can affect learners’ acquisition of GAI knowledge and skills. Additionally, it is crucial to avoid the generation of false, homogenized, and superficial responses by GAI products, as this may diminish the intention to engage in human-machine interaction and impede the development of AIL (Biyiri et al., 2024). Therefore, educators must mitigate the negative impacts of the digital divide on innovative activities, by providing relevant training or workshops to enhance students’ knowledge and skills regarding GAI use (e.g., prompt instructions) and ethical considerations, thereby helping them under­ stand their own limitations and focus on creative problem-solving. Moreover, teach­ ers should enhance their own AIL, evaluate whether the GAI products introduced are suitable for innovation activities in specific domains, assess their limitations and advantages, and identify ways to provide effective scaffolding (e.g., questions, prompts, examples) to facilitate students’ innovation.

Secondly, an individual’s PNS and CSE help mobilize internal psychological energy, which aids learners in actively self-regulating during the innovation pro­ cess, thus promoting the occurrence of IB. Learners should focus on how machines enhance humans rather than what humans do, in order to enhance uniquely human abilities (such as well-being and self-efficacy), and connect these abilities to the “self” (Northoff et al., 2022). It is crucial to recognize that if learners cannot confront the inherent flaws of GAI tools, and develop feelings of aversion or distrust due

to subpar experiences, this will hinder the accumulation of psychological energy, as the relationship between human intelligence and machine intelligence should be 1 + 1 > 2. For students using GAI products for the first time, teachers should pro­ vide essential support to strengthen students’ psychological needs and self-efficacy, as these have been identified as direct predictors of SRL and IB. Individuals with higher self-efficacy are more inclined to acquire the knowledge and skills required for using GAI products and believe in their ability to succeed, which stimulates curi­ osity, enhances enjoyment, and fosters a sense of control (Xia et al., 2023). There­ fore, teachers can introduce small, incremental tasks to enhance students’ successful experiences in generating innovative ideas with GAI and effectively implementing them. Furthermore, students should be encouraged to share their experiences of using GAI to achieve creative outputs or failures through periodic feedback, summarizing techniques for GAI product usage. Initially, their motivation may stem from curios­ ity, but over time, the frequency of human-machine collaboration often decreases (Li et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2023).

Finally, the diversification of GAI application scenarios and the ubiquity of its functionalities can effectively support SRL. Our research findings highlight that AIL, as a foundational competence for human-machine collaboration, is crucial in deter­ mining the effectiveness of using GAI products for SRL. In a human-machine col­ laborative environment, learners can continuously adjust their strategies and methods for using GAI based on their expected innovation goals, and can utilize GAI to con­ tinuously monitor, assess, evaluate, reflect on, and adjust the goals and progress of their innovative tasks. However, this does not imply that all students will purpose­ fully use GAI products to regulate their learning and innovation processes. This is because current GAI products are primarily general-domain large language models, and without pre-defined instructions, their feedback may not effectively support selfregulated learning. Therefore, educators should consider training GAI products in specialized domains and provide feedback that is more emotionally engaging, rel­ evant, and frequent to encourage learners to actively regulate their innovation pro­ cesses (Ng et al., 2024; Rejeb et al., 2024). It can be said that GAI-supported SRL can effectively facilitate the occurrence of IB, and currently, there is little research examining the relationship between IB and the SRL process in a human-machine col­ laborative environment (Rubenstein et al., 2018), this study fills that gap.

This study finds that individual AIL plays a foundational role in utilizing GAI for innovative activities. The level of AIL influences IB by affecting learners’ psy­ chological aspects (CSE and PNS) and metacognitive aspects (SRL). However, it is important to note four limitations of this study. First, the study participants were indi­ viduals with prior experience using GAI and possessed a certain level of AIL; future research could consider how changes in learners’ AIL due to the digital divide affect psychological, metacognitive, and IB. Second, the experimental group predomi­ nantly consisted of females (63.2%) and was mainly composed of humanities and social sciences students, which may affect the study’s conclusions; future research should focus on gender differences to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Third, considering that SRL and IB are relatively long-term processes, a longitudinal research design is recommended for long-term tracking, experimentation, or surveys to further clarify their relationship. Fourth, in higher education, IB primarily involves

research innovation, entrepreneurship, and product innovation, but different groups engage in different types of innovative activities; therefore, there may be variations in the stages of using GAI for IB, and future research should address this issue to ensure consistency in innovative tasks and expected outcomes.
