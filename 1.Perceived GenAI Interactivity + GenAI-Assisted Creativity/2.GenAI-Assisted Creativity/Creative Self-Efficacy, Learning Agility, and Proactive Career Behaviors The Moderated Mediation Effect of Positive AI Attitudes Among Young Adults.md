A B S T R A C T

Keywords: Creative self-efficacy Proactive career behaviors Learning agility Positive AI attitudes

In the context of rapidly changing work environments influenced by technological advancements such as artificial intelligence (AI), career development has shifted from traditional paths to dy­ namic, long-term personal growth. Learning agility, characterized as the ability to quickly adapt to changes, is considered an essential component in fostering proactive career behaviors. The study emphasizes the significance of positive perceptions of AI in reducing negative attitudes, such as employment insecurity, and promoting career behaviors. This research investigates the relationship between creative self-efficacy and proactive career behaviors among university students from a developing country, showing the mediating role of learning agility and the moderated mediation effect of positive AI attitudes. The study data was gathered from two points (T1 and T2) and included 303 university students. Hypothesis testing was conducted with the SPSS AMOS. The results indicate that positive AI attitudes have a moderated mediation effect on the relationship between creative self-efficacy and proactive career behaviors, with learning agility acting as a mediator. The findings of this study underline the importance of promoting creative self-efficacy and positive attitudes toward AI, as these have the potential to increase students’ learning agility, thereby encouraging proactive career behaviors in AI-integrated work settings.

1. Introduction

Transformations in work environments and the rapid advancement of technology have resulted in major changes in labor markets and organizational structures. Traditional career paths have been replaced by modern career models, in which personal development is considered a growing, continuous effort (Briscoe & Hall, 2006; Hirschi & Koen, 2021; Wilhelm & Hirschi, 2019). Thus, individuals adapting to the rapidly evolving work environments of today are discovering that their confidence in their creative potential and ability for lifelong learning is becoming increasingly important. Recent projections regarding the influence of technology improve­ ments on employment indicate that a significant segment of the workforce will require reskilling and upskilling due to changes in their responsibilities (World Economic Forum, 2023). Proactive career behaviors (PCB), demonstrated via rapid and creative actions, are essential for entrants to the workforce. In a time characterized by unforeseen developments, exploring alternative career trajectories, pursuing novel learning and development pos­ sibilities, and creating radical solutions have emerged as crucial competitive advantages for labor market candidates. “An individual’s belief regarding the ability to produce creative outcomes” is known as creative self-efficacy (CSE) (Tierney & Farmer, 2002: 1138). Thus, since it enables people to think creatively, question norms, and adjust to changing circumstances, it promotes proactive career be­ haviors. Although studies have indicated that self-efficacy is an important determinant of PCB (Do˘ganülkü & Korkmaz, 2023; Jiang et al., 2023), the development of knowledge regarding the mechanism between CSE and PCB still appears to be in the developmental stage.

Learning tendencies also play a significant role in the emergence of PCB. Learning agility (LA), which refers to the ability to quickly and flexibly adapt to changes in learning processes, represents an important potential resource in career development (DeRue et al., 2012). Furthermore, LA is associated with intellectual curiosity (Sung, 2021), which refers to the inclination to explore, engage in, and pursue chances for cognitive activities. CSE strengthens confidence in creative capabilities, whereas adaptability in learning processes could promote the development of LA in dynamic environments. Recent studies indicate that LA has emerged as a crucial attribute for addressing difficulties and promoting innovation in organizational settings (Lee & Song, 2022; Smith & Watkins, 2024; Tripathi & Kalia, 2024). Thus, LA has become a very desirable trait for the emerging workforce, particularly as a possible antecedent to proactive career behaviors.

The widespread use of AI has initiated various transformations, particularly in higher education institutions (Wang et al., 2023). New AI-focused programs have been established, and existing departments have revised their curriculum to include AI-related courses worldwide (Southworth et al., 2023). Consequently, there has been a significant increase in research on AI attitudes across fields such as education, psychology, and management (Schepman & Rodway, 2020). Also, career planning processes can no longer be considered independent of AI (Bankins et al., 2024; Moran & Ackerman, 2024). However, AI skepticism is a major issue in career development (Adepoju et al., 2024), and negative AI attitudes could result in concerns about job insecurity (Brougham & Haar, 2018). Thus, comprehending AI and reducing these negative attitudes is important for both employees and emerging adults. Based on the assumption that PCB are regarded as an indicator of adaptivity within the framework of career construction theory (CCT) (Hirschi et al., 2015) and self-efficacy influences PCB (social cognitive model of career self-management, Lent et al., 2022), this study focuses on the role of LA in the relationship between PCB and CSE among university students in a developing country, as well as the moderating role of positive AI attitudes in this mediation. This study aims to offer theoretical and practical insights by examining the effect of CSE and LA on PCB while also revealing the role of AI attitudes in this relationship. Also, this research encompasses several fields, such as counseling psychology, career psychology, industrial and organizational psychology, and educational psychology, providing new perspectives on self-perceptions and career behaviors in the era of AI.

2. Theoretical background

2.1. Creative self-efficacy and proactive career behaviors

Following the 1990s, career management has shifted, placing responsibility primarily on individuals due to external factors beyond individuals’ control, such as globalization, the rise of multinational corporations, and economic crises (Hirschi, 2018). A widely accepted assumption in the career literature is that individuals are the primary agents responsible for actions such as identifying potential job opportunities, enhancing employability, and planning their careers (Briscoe & Hall, 2006). In this context, the concept of proactivity emerges as a crucial factor in enabling individuals to more effectively manage their own careers. Proactive career behaviors (PCB), rooted in Career Construction Theory (CCT), are defined as “the degree to which somebody is proactively developing his or her career as expressed by diverse career behaviors” (Hirschi et al., 2014, p. 577). CCT posits that certain self-regulation skills could motivate individuals and positively influence person-environment fit throughout the career development process. PCB relate to how individuals cope with the changes and challenges associated with career development and progression (Savickas & Porfeli, 2012). Within the CCT framework, PCB are considered indicators of adaptability, as individuals employ these behaviors to address career development goals and challenges (Hirschi et al., 2015).

There are two main approaches to proactivity. The first views proactivity as a stable personality trait, while the second considers it a behavior that could be developed (Strauss et al., 2012). Recent studies have further emphasized the importance of proactivity for careers. For instance, Lent et al. (2022) integrated PCB into the social cognitive career self-management CSM model, suggesting that it could provide a framework for enhancing person-organization fit within organizations. PCB are positively related to career success (De Vos et al., 2009) and career satisfaction (Herrmann et al., 2015). Additionally, PCB are positively linked to self-efficacy (Hirschi et al., 2014). According to the social cognitive model of CSM, proactive career behavior is influenced by a complex mechanism composed of various cognitive, social, and personality traits (Lent et al., 2022). The processes of exploration, goal setting, and decision-making, which fall under the scope of PCB, require specific cognitive elements. A fundamental component, as emphasized in the social cognitive model of CSM, is self-efficacy. Self-efficacy beliefs pertain to an individual’s con­ fidence in their capacity to effectively execute a certain task or career-related activity, influencing their career decisions, objectives, chances for success, and ability to cope with challenges (Bandura, 1986). Within the unpredictability, volatility, and uncertainty introduced by macro actors affecting labor markets, creativity is another crucial element for navigating uncertainty and the effec­ tiveness of proactivity.

Creativity is relatively overlooked in career literature; however, in today’s dynamic and competitive labor markets, it has become an essential tool for developing unconventional working arrangements that differ significantly from those of a decade ago. Tierney and Farmer (2002) introduced the concept of creative self-efficacy (CSE), grounded in Bandura’s (1986) social cognitive theory, as a measure of an individual’s confidence in their ability to produce creative outcomes. Given that the rising number of candidates with similar qualifications increases competitiveness, especially when labor supply exceeds demand, creativity emerges as an important tool for effective career management despite the current competition, rapid shifts, and uncertainty in the labor markets. In such an environment, the capacity for the formulation of different alternatives becomes critical. Hence, research has demonstrated a close relationship between creativity and proactivity (Fuller et al., 2018). CSE has primarily been studied in the context of employees, examining outcomes such as creative performance (Choi et al., 2021; Tierney & Farmer, 2002), innovation (Ahlin et al., 2014), and rewards (Li et al., 2018). In the context of students, CSE is positively associated with entrepreneurial intentions (Laguía et al., 2019), learning outcomes (Shafait & Huang, 2022), and student engagement (Alvarez-Huerta et al., 2021). A recent study by Alvarez-Huerta et al. (2022) found CSE as a significant predictor of entrepreneurial self-efficacy, emphasizing its vital function in determining career-related self-perceptions. This aligns with the current research, which emphasizes CSE’s impact on PCB. In the study by Do˘ganülkü and Korkmaz (2023) involving university students, self-efficacy was identified as an antecedent to PCB. The study indicated that self-efficacy mediates the link between proactive personality and PCB. These results imply that career-related behaviors are significantly influenced by self-efficacy. Given that proactivity involves the anticipation and resolution of potential challenges prior to their emergence, rather than a passive response to events as they occur, CSE could be a significant factor. Drawing on the social cognitive model of CSM’s assertion that self-efficacy influences PCB, it was hypothesized:

H1: CSE is positively related to PCB.

2.2. Mediating role of learning agility

In complex, rapid, and ambiguous work environments, agility is a crucial concept in addition to creativity and proactivity. In­ dividuals must not only develop innovative ideas but also quickly adjust and remain open to change to keep pace with changes in work settings. Thus, those who are unable to foresee or adapt to changes, or who neglect to take the necessary steps quickly, are unlikely to navigate their careers successfully. To achieve this, learning abilities have an important role, enabling individuals to transfer con­ ceptual and practical knowledge into action. Learning agility (LA) refers to “a constellation of individual characteristics and attributes that enable people to develop or refine their knowledge and skills in response to changes” (DeRue et al., 2012:256). Lombardo and Eichinger (2000: 323) define LA as “the willingness and ability to learn from experience and subsequently apply that learning to perform successfully under new or first-time conditions.” Individuals with high LA are more likely to successfully fulfill their tasks, and they are likely to be high-potential talents. LA encompasses not only learning from experiences but is also shaped by contextual influences. The learning capacities of individuals are related to their search for opportunities and role models during challenging times (McKenna et al., 2007). Unlike traditional learning, LA emphasizes the speed of learning (DeRue et al., 2012). Thus, it is the element of speed that makes LA particularly valuable in today’s work environments. Individuals with agile learning capabilities could process information more rapidly, enabling faster action. The concept of LA encompasses not only speed but also flexibility (Lee & Song, 2022). Flexibility is necessary for individuals to see connections between experiences and to draw useful insights from seemingly unrelated events.

In the comprehensive LA model developed by DeRue et al. (2012), individual characteristics are identified as determinants of LA. According to this model, cognitive elements influence an individual’s cognitive and behavioral processes, thereby affecting individual performance. Among the key cognitive elements, self-efficacy beliefs play a crucial role in enabling individuals to have better outcome expectations, which positively impacts the likelihood of success in their efforts within a specific domain Bandura (1989). Furthermore, self-efficacy not only has the potential to positively influence cognitive development by affecting cognitive and motivational func­ tioning but also impacts effort and persistence in learning processes (Zimmerman, 2002). Thus, self-efficacy beliefs, as an indicator of LA, might affect cognitive processes (Jian, 2022). The fundamental aspect of CSE is confidence in one’s ability to generate innovative solutions, which facilitates individuals’ openness to new experiences and could positively influence LA (Xia et al., 2022). Moreover, a high level of CSE could enhance an individual’s willingness to take risks when necessary, fostering curiosity and a sense of exploration in the face of change, thereby increasing LA (Shin & Eom, 2014). Individuals with high LA are better equipped to manage complexity and ambiguity (Lombardo & Eichinger, 2000). LA differs from cognitive ability and personality, encompassing open-mindedness, seeking feedback, and assessing strengths and weaknesses (Bedford, 2011), which are crucial for career decision-making in dynamic environments. Previous studies indicate that LA is a crucial factor in career advancement (Dries et al., 2012) and an individual’s perceived ability to advance up the career ladder (Spreitzer et al., 1997:24). Dai et al. (2013) suggested that challenging experiences could be effective in the acquisition of new skills, abilities, and knowledge that new roles often require. Therefore, an individual’s current performance could not consistently offer enough insight into their future performance, emphasizing the need for LA as a crucial concept in identifying high potential. In this context, although high performance and high potential are separate concepts, LA has been recognized as an important predictor of potential (Dries et al., 2012). Agile learners could search for further opportunities for career growth, which is a critical trait for dynamic economies where continuous learning and development are required for the workforce (Eby et al., 2003). Unanticipated changes in external environ­ mental conditions could occur, leading to the emergence or disappearance of certain employment prospects (Dai et al., 2013). Moreover, the demand for digital competencies grows because of technological advancements and increased digitalization. The rapid changes in career opportunities and employers’ expectations for diverse skill sets are particularly significant for undergraduates ready to enter the labor market and are in the early stages of their career development. For instance, Lee (2020) found that LA positively affects the career decision levels of undergraduates. Undergraduates who learn from their experiences increase the speed and flexi­ bility of their learning processes, become more enthusiastic about acquiring new abilities and could consider a more proactive approach to career planning, goal setting, skill development, and networking. Thus, it was hypothesized:

H3: LA is positively related to PCB.

The social cognitive model of CSM (Lent et al., 2022) shows how self-efficacy beliefs, outcome expectations, and personal goals influence career development through certain cognitive abilities and contextual factors. CSE has been found to be associated with actions that contribute to career development, such as problem-solving (Royston & Reiter-Palmon, 2019). Additionally, since the social cognitive model emphasizes learning experiences and adaptability, LA could serve as a key variable in the relationship between self-efficacy and PCB. The capacity to quickly derive meaning from experiences and learn effectively in uncertain or dynamic contexts is a crucial factor that could influence the relationship between confidence in generating novel ideas and engaging in PCB. LA, which underscores adaptability and flexibility (Bedford, 2011), may assist individuals in overcoming employment problems. Lee and Song (2022) established a conceptual model that integrates adult learning theories with organizational behavior theories. This model suggests that an individual’s adaptive capability may affect their coping strategies and determine their reactions to different stresses and uncertainty. It is argued that creative behaviors might be improved by LA. Similarly, empirical research indicates that LA is significantly associated with the generation and implementation of novel ideas (Tripathi & Kalia, 2024). Consequently, LA is widely acknowledged as an essential notion for maintaining skill applicability and promoting innovation within organizations. Recent studies highlight that in volatile, uncertain, complex, and ambiguous (VUCA) work environments, the capacity to evolve and adapt is essential, and continuous learning is vital for current employees and those entering the labor market (Smith & Watkins, 2024). The critical importance of LA within labor markets emphasizes its considerable potential, especially with PCB. Through learning, in­ dividuals can develop new strategies more quickly as they progress along their career trajectories. As CSE enhances individuals’ confidence in seeking innovative solutions, their adaptability to changing circumstances is similarly significant in participating in PCB. Thus, it was hypothesized:

H4: LA mediates the relationship between CSE and PCB.

2.3. Moderated mediation role of positive AI attitudes

The use of AI has expanded rapidly, becoming an integral part of daily life. Individual attitudes toward AI are significant, as they influence its acceptance and increased use (Schepman & Rodway, 2020). AI is defined as “a system’s ability to interpret external data correctly, to learn from such data, and to use those learnings to achieve specific goals and tasks through flexible adaptation.” (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2019: 17). Due to its capacity to interpret large datasets, make predictions, and generate new insights, AI technologies are increasingly being used in the business world, affecting the lives of employees as well (Park et al., 2024). Thus, there has been a growing interest in examining the attitudes of individuals who either use or have the potential to use AI (Brougham & Haar, 2018).

They assessed AI capability through awareness and skills, demonstrating that an increase in students’ AI capabilities correlates with increased creativity. Similarly, Shahzad et al. (2024) found that generative AI-based technologies improve learning performance in higher education through self-efficacy. Within the framework of the social cognitive theory of CSM, self-efficacy is critical for a successful learning environment, making the use of generative AI useful in fostering active participation in learning processes and increasing students’ belief in their own abilities. Thus, students possessing increased CSE, in addition to a positive attitude towards AI, could be more inclined to perceive AI as a beneficial instrument that improves their flexibility and learning. This approach helps individuals to utilize AI technology efficiently, therefore strengthening the connection between their creative confidence and their ability to learn and adapt. McGuire et al. The application of AI in organizations could possibly be examined using two different perspectives. From one perspective, AI increases productivity by rapidly automating repetitive jobs. On the other hand, it could lead to adverse employee attitudes (Prentice et al., 2020). For instance, AI awareness could lead to concerns about job security (Brougham & Haar, 2018). This implies that positive views regarding AI among employees could lead to more favorable perceptions of the organization. Moreover, generative AI is increasingly seen as an effective tool that empowers individuals to enhance their critical thinking and creativity in their approach to work problems (Korayim et al., 2025). 3. Method

3.1. Analytical procedure

This study aims to test the moderated mediation effect of positive AI attitudes on the relationship between CSE and PCB, with LA acting as a mediator. The initial analysis includes the calculation of descriptive statistics, such as means, standard deviations, skew­ ness, kurtosis, and reliability values, for the data collected, which were examined using SPSS. Additionally, alternative measurement models were constructed to assess the validity and reliability of the data collecting tools; confirmatory factor analysis was conducted with SPSS AMOS, and hypothesis testing was performed with SPSS AMOS. In this study, AMOS was used for structural equation modeling (SEM), with maximum likelihood estimation as the estimation method. The model fit was evaluated using several goodnessof-fit indices, including χ²/df, CFI (Comparative Fit Index), SRMR (Standardized Root Mean Square Residual), and RMSEA (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation). The cut-off criteria for these indices were χ²/df 〈2, CFI 〉0.90, SRMR < 0.08, and RMSEA < 0.08, based on the recommendations by Hu and Bentler (1999). For assessing construct validity, the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) was examined for each construct, with an AVE value greater than 0.50 indicating good convergence (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Addi­ tionally, to examine the distinctiveness of the research variables, alternative measurement models were tested, and their fit indices were compared (Bagozzi & Edwards, 1998).

3.2. Participants

The study sample included 303 university students enrolled in management programs at two public universities in Türkiye. A convenience sample approach was chosen for its efficiency and cost-effectiveness. The online survey form included details on voluntary involvement in the study and ethical approval. This study obtained ethical approval from the higher education institution of the host country and complies with institutional standards for ethics and publication. Data were collected at two time points. The initial data collection (T1) occurred in April 2024, followed by the subsequent data collection (T2) in June 2024 to reduce common method variance. The first data collection included CSE and positive AI attitudes, while the second data collection included LA and proactive career behavior scales. At T1, 480 data points were gathered, while at T2, 339 data points were collected. The data from individuals who replied at both T1 and T2 were identified using their participation IDs. Consequently, 303 survey forms, including results from both T1 and T2, were used in the current study. In terms of gender, 187 (61.7 %) of the participants were female, while 116 (38.3 %) of them were male. Regarding the participants’ ages, 201 (66.3 %) were between the ages of 18 and 20, 86 (28.4 %) were between the ages of 21 and 23, and 16 (5.3 %) were ages 24 and above. The mean age of the participants was 20.14 years (range: 18 to 26 years, SD = 1.681).

3.3. Measures

Creative self-efficacy. CSE was measured with the “Creative Self-Efficacy Scale” developed by Tierney and Farmer (2011). A sample item of the four-item scale is “…tries out new ideas and approaches to problems”. CSE increases as the scale scores increase, with a range of 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree). The scale’s internal consistency value ranges from 0.66 to 0.94 (e.g., Raihan & Uddin, 2023; Tantawy et al., 2021). The internal consistency value for the CSE scale in this study was 0.82.

Positive AI attitudes. Positive AI attitudes were measured with “The General Attitudes Towards Artificial Intelligence Scale” developed by Schepman and Rodway (2020). The positive AI attitudes dimension of this scale, which includes 12 items, was used in this study. A sample item is “There are many beneficial applications of Artificial Intelligence.” Positive AI attitudes increase as the scale scores increase, with a range of 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree). The internal consistency value was 0.88 in the original scale development study. Turkish version of the scale has reliability value of 0.82 (Kaya et al., 2024). The internal consistency value for the Positive AI Attitudes scale in this study was 0.88.

An example item is “… flexible; adjusts his/her approach when something doesn’t work.” LA increases as the scale scores increase, ranging from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree). Also, the results showed that the direct effect of LA on PCB is significant (β = 0.652, p < 0.01, 95 % CI [.495, 0.774]). The indirect effects of the LA on the relationship between CSE and PCB (β = 0.243, p < 0.01, 95 % CI [.133, 0.354]) were significant. In other words, LA fully mediates the relationship between CSE and PCB. This means that CSE leads to higher LA, which in turn leads to higher PCB. Thus, H4 was accepted.

As seen in Table 3, the conditional indirect effect of CSE on PCB via LA was moderated by positive AI attitudes (β = 0.254, 95 % CI [.040, 0.447], p < 0.05), supporting H5. This result indicates a moderated mediation mechanism, where the indirect influence of CSE on PCB through LA strengthens with an increase in positive AI attitudes. Additionally, the conditional indirect effect of CSE on PCB through LA was non-significant at low levels of positive AI attitudes (β = 0.042, 95 % CI [–.022,.117], p > 0.01). However, it became significant at medium (β =0.131, 95 % CI [.077,.201], p < 0.01) and high (β =0.220, 95 % CI [.122,.327], p < 0.01) levels. The index of moderated mediation was statistically significant (β =0.043, 95 % CI [.013,.075], p < 0.05), indicating that the strength of the indirect effect differed across levels of the positive AI attitudes. Hence, these results support the hypothesized moderated mediation model, in which positive AI attitudes increase the indirect effect of CSE on LA, thereby improving its influence on PCB.

5. Discussion

This study examines the mediating role of LA and the moderating role of PAIA in the relationship between CSE and PCB. In today’s dynamic business environments, the increasing use of AI applications holds particular significance for young adults preparing to enter the workforce, as it aids in their readiness to adapt to internal organizational settings. Consequently, attitudes toward AI applications have emerged as a critical factor in the connection between self-efficacy and career behaviors. The findings show that CSE is positively related to PAIA, LA, and PCB. Individuals with higher CSE tend to have more favorable attitudes toward AI applications and demonstrate greater LA. Moreover, CSE has a positive influence on PCB. These findings demonstrate, as assumed in the social cognitive model of CSM (Lent et al., 2022), that self-efficacy influences PCB and could impact career behaviors within the domain of creativity.

5.1. Theoretical implications

This study analyzes the determinants of PCB via the lenses of CCT and CSM. The exploration of CSE, in accordance with the concept of proactivity that emphasizes solution-oriented strategies, demonstrated a positive correlation. Recent research highlighted the increasing significance of AI in CSM (Bankins et al., 2024). Similarly, this study shows that LA affects PCB and highlights the importance of individual characteristics in career development processes. This study also found that positive AI attitudes play an important moderating role. Future research could explore different research models integrating CSE or AI attitudes within the framework of the social cognitive model of CSM. Within this framework, the roles of LA and AI attitudes in career decision-making processes could be investigated. Additionally, the effects of AI-induced stress and career choice could be examined using this model.

5.2. Practical implications

The findings of this research provide important suggestions for higher education institutions, employers, and researchers. First, given the increasing prevalence of AI applications driven by technological advancements, it is recommended that higher education institutions integrate AI-related topics into their course curricula. This integration could increase students’ AI awareness and help them develop more positive attitudes toward AI (Wang et al., 2023). Studies have shown that AI has been integrated into various academic disciplines, with particularly high levels of engagement observed in applied sciences (Qu et al., 2024). Additionally, activities that promote CSE and LA in course content, as well as implementing tools like self-assessment methods to periodically measure these traits, could be implemented. Such initiatives would better prepare university students to meet the demands of the current labor markets.

Employers might benefit from selection tools designed to assess levels of creativity, agility, and AI attitudes among potential candidates. Evaluating these factors could provide insights into candidates’ tendencies toward LA and PCB, serving as indicators of their future workplace performance. Employers might also consider collaborating with universities, organizing workshops during career fairs, and fostering partnerships to support these developments. Researchers could shed light on this increasingly important topic by understanding the role of AI and LA in career development. Future studies could investigate the relationships between selfperceptions, career adaptability, perceived employability, and AI attitudes. The role of LA and AI could be explored in the relationship between students’ future work selves and their PCB (Taber & Blankemeyer, 2015). Additionally, some research has focused on evaluating LA in adults. Lee and Song (2022) developed an employee LA scale to evaluate LA in the workplace. This assessment in­ strument is predicated on the idea that humans are agents capable of adjusting to external contextual influences. Additionally, Smith and Watkins (2024) define LA as a multidimensional concept that encompasses people, results, change, and mental agility. Thus, future studies could take other dimensions of LA into account. Furthermore, there are recommendations for examining LA within several contexts (Park et al., 2022). Different contexts may encompass many professional domains and nations. Given LA is a dynamic construct rather than a static concept, longitudinal data might be employed to comprehend its evolution at the individual level. Considering that this study focuses on young adults, it is advisable to see them as future employees and to develop LA measures with established validity and reliability particularly designed for this demographic. In future studies, the status of creativity-related vari­ ables such as creative personality and creative performance may also be measured, specifically in university students.

Regarding AI attitudes, exploring the dark side of AI attitudes could provide valuable results. For instance, studies could examine how negative attitudes toward AI influence young individuals’ work readiness, employment hope, or perceptions of job insecurity. Additionally, the broader impacts of smart technology, AI, robotics, and algorithms (STARA) on career management could also be explored. For instance, STARA has been identified as a moderator in the relationship between employee resilience and career success (Gerçek et al., 2024). Moreover, while the importance of AI literacy for university students is widely recognized, research on the significance of AI for employees is still ongoing (Bankins et al., 2024). In this regard, evaluating the influence of AI attitudes across various career stages might improve the understanding of its impact.
