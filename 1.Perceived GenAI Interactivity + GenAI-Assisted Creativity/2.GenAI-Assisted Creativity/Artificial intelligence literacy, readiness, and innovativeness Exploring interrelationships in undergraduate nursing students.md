Aim/objective: This study aimed to examine the relationships between AI literacy, medical AI readiness, and individual innovativeness among nursing students. Background: Integrating artificial intelligence (AI) into healthcare requires future nurses to develop competencies in literacy, readiness, and innovativeness. Evidence on how these domains interrelate in nursing education re­ mains limited. Design: A cross-sectional, relational study design was adopted. Methods: The study was conducted between April and July 2025 with 440 nursing students from two state universities in Türkiye. Data were collected online using a Descriptive Information Form, the AI Literacy Scale, the Medical AI Readiness Scale, and the Individual Innovativeness Scale. Descriptive statistics, independent samples t-test, one-way ANOVA, Mann–Whitney U test, Kruskal–Wallis test, Pearson correlation, and multiple regression analysis were used. Results: Students' mean age was 21.13 (SD = 2.80), and 70.5% were female. Most participants (91.4%) had not received prior AI training. University-based comparisons showed significant differences in AI literacy (p = .042), medical AI readiness skills (p = .006), and innovativeness (p = .001). Demographic variables such as gender, grade level, AI training, usage frequency, and openness to new technologies were associated with scale scores. Correlation analyses revealed moderate positive associations between AI literacy and both medical AI readiness (r = 0.532, p < .01) and innovativeness (r = 0.423, p < .01). Regression analyses indicated that AI literacy (β = 0.475, p < .001) and individual innovativeness (β = 0.134, p = .003) significantly predicted AI readiness, while university was not a significant predictor, and the model explained 29.8% of the variance. Conclusions: AI literacy, readiness, and innovativeness are interrelated competencies among nursing students. Findings provide evidence for integrating AI-oriented content into curricula and for developing strategies that enhance students' preparedness for technology-driven healthcare.

AI literacy and medical AI readiness are conceptually related but represent distinct constructs within the broader framework of technol­ ogy integration in healthcare. AI literacy primarily reflects individuals' knowledge, awareness, and critical understanding of artificial intelli­ gence systems, including their capabilities, limitations, and ethical im­ plications (Chiu et al., 2024). In contrast, medical AI readiness refers to the extent to which individuals feel cognitively, technically, and ethi­ cally prepared to implement AI applications in clinical practice (Karaca et al., 2021). While AI literacy provides the foundational knowledge necessary to understand AI technologies, readiness reflects the practical preparedness required to apply these technologies in healthcare envi­ ronments. Examining both constructs together therefore enables a more comprehensive understanding of how knowledge-based competencies translate into preparedness for AI-integrated clinical practice.

Innovation is a concept that reflects individuals' openness to change, problem-solving abilities, and creative thinking skills (Roddy and Pol­ fuss, 2020). In the field of healthcare, an innovative mindset enhances both professional development and the quality and efficiency of patient care (Baksi et al., 2020). The International Council of Nurses (ICN) de­ fines innovation as “the process of developing new approaches, tech­ nologies, and ways of working,” and emphasizes the pioneering role of nurses in implementing innovative care practices. The 2009 theme “Nursing and Innovation” highlighted the importance of innovation in delivering quality healthcare services (International Council of Nurses (ICN), 2009).

Today's healthcare systems require nurses who possess leadership skills, can think critically, and are capable of providing safe and highquality care by effectively utilizing technology (Heydari et al., 2023). Therefore, strengthening nursing students' levels of AI literacy, AI readiness, and innovativeness during their education is considered critical for adapting to future AI-driven healthcare environments. Ac­ cording to a recent systematic review by O'Connor et al. (2023), AI implementation in nursing education remains inconsistent, highlighting the need for empirical studies examining readiness and literacy collec­ tively (O'Connor et al., 2023).

2. Background

The rapid integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into healthcare has begun to transform patient care, clinical decision support, di­ agnostics, and health data management (Bajwa et al., 2021; Sheikh et al., 2021). As this transformation unfolds, healthcare professionals are expected to develop competencies that enable them to adopt, evaluate, and effectively use AI-based systems. Nursing, as a discipline that plays a central role in health promotion, disease prevention, and direct patient care, occupies a key position in this technological shift (O'Connor et al., 2023). Accordingly, AI literacy—defined as the ability to understand, assess, and appropriately use AI applications—has emerged as a fundamental skill for nursing students and practitioners (Çelebi et al., 2023; Chiu et al., 2024). Parallel to this, the concept of readiness for medical AI reflects the cognitive, technical, ethical, and anticipatory capacities required for nurses to engage with AI-driven practices in clinical environments (Karaca et al., 2021).

Accordingly, the present study was designed to examine the levels of AI literacy, medical AI readiness, and individual innovativeness among nursing students, to explore their differences across demographic vari­ ables, and to analyze the interrelationships among these constructs. Beyond these aims, a predictive model was also tested in which medical AI readiness was conceptualized as the outcome variable. This decision rests on the view that readiness reflects students' preparedness to inte­ grate AI into nursing practice, whereas AI literacy (knowledge and skills) and innovativeness (a personal trait indicating openness to new ideas) are antecedent factors that are expected to influence readiness.

Research questions

• What are the levels of nursing students' artificial intelligence literacy, medical artificial intelligence readiness, and individual innovativeness? • Do nursing students' levels of artificial intelligence literacy, medical artificial intelligence readiness, and individual innovativeness differ according to their demographic characteristics? • Are there significant relationships among artificial intelligence lit­ eracy, medical artificial intelligence readiness, and individual innovativeness? • To what extent do artificial intelligence literacy and individual innovativeness predict nursing students' medical artificial intelli­ gence readiness?

3. Methods

3.1. Design

This study was conducted as a cross-sectional and correlational research. The research was reported in accordance with the Strength­ ening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement (Equator, 2023).

3.2. Participants and setting

The population of the study consisted of 1100 undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the nursing departments of two public universities in Türkiye between April and July 2025. The required sample size was initially estimated using the Yamane formula for known populations with a 95% confidence level, p = .8, and a ± 5% margin of error, resulting in a minimum required sample of 285 participants. However, a total of 440 nursing students who met the inclusion criteria and voluntarily agreed to participate were included in the final sample.

To further evaluate the adequacy of the sample size for the regression analysis, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1. Assuming two predictors, an alpha level of 0.05, and a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15), the achieved statistical power for the final sample size (n = 440) was calculated as 0.99. University A is located in Türkiye's third-largest city, which hosts five public and two foundation universities. Students at this university complete their clinical practices in multiple healthcare institutions, allowing them to gain broad experience with diverse patient pop­ ulations. University B, on the other hand, is located in a smaller pro­ vincial city with more limited clinical practice opportunities. The city has only one public hospital and no large-scale healthcare institutions with education and research status. Therefore, students' exposure to diverse cases and clinical experiences may be more restricted. The in­ clusion criteria for the study were being over 18 years of age, having access to data collection forms via e-mail or social media tools, and volunteering to participate in the research.

3.3. Data collection tools

3.3.1. Student descriptive information form The descriptive information form was developed by the researchers based on the relevant literature (Akca Sumengen et al., 2025; Erol et al., 2018; Yalcinkaya et al., 2024). The form included basic information such as students' age, gender, marital status, university, year of study, whether they had received education on artificial intelligence, fre­ quency of using AI applications, and openness to new technologies.

3.3.2. Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale (AILS) The Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale, originally developed by Wang et al. (2023), was adapted into Turkish and validated for reli­ ability by Çelebi et al. (2023) (Çelebi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). The scale consists of 12 items and four subdimensions—Evaluation, Ethics, Awareness, and Usage—parallel to the original form. It is scored on a 7-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging between 12 and 84. In the reliability analysis of the original study, Cronbach's alpha coefficient was 0.85. In the present study, the Cronbach's alpha internal consistency coefficient for the entire scale was calculated as 0.802.

3.3.3. Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness (MAIR) Scale The Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale, developed by Karaca et al. (2021), is used to evaluate nursing students' readiness for medical AI (Karaca et al., 2021). The scale consists of 22 items across four subdimensions, rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Total scores range from 22 to 110, with higher scores indicating higher levels of readiness. The reliability analysis of the original study reported Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.877. In this study, Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the entire scale was calculated as 0.948.

3.3.4. Individual Innovativeness Scale (IIS) The scale, originally developed by Hurt et al. (1977), was adapted into Turkish with validity and reliability established by Sarıo˘glu and Altuntas¸ (Hurt et al., 1977; Sarıo˘glu Kemer and Altuntas¸, 2017). It consists of 18 items and three subdimensions. Scores range between 18 and 90, with classifications as follows: individuals scoring ≥80 are considered innovators, 69–80 early adopters, 57–68 interrogators, 46–56 skeptics, and <46 traditionalists. In the original study, Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficient was 0.89, and in the present study it was calculated as 0.802.

Data collection commenced following the approval of the Ethics Committee for the conduct of this study. The data were collected be­ tween April and July 2025 through an online survey administered via the Google Forms platform. Since the study aimed to reach a broader group rather than being limited to a single data collection site, data were obtained electronically by the researchers using a digital survey tool. The survey link was distributed to eligible nursing students through social media platforms. Prior to the main data collection, the question­ naire was pilot-tested with a small group of nursing students to evaluate clarity and estimate completion time.

At the beginning of the four-section survey, an informed consent form was included on the first page, outlining the purpose, content, researchers, confidentiality of data, and voluntary nature of participa­ tion. Only participants who read and consented to this form were allowed to proceed to the subsequent questions. Based on the pilot test results, the average completion time was approximately 15 min. Therefore, responses completed in less than 15 min were considered potentially inattentive and were excluded from the analysis. A total of 9 responses were excluded based on this criterion. Submission of the survey was considered confirmation of participants' consent to take part in the study. Common challenges associated with online surveys include item–response difficulties, low response rates, and representativeness issues (Polit and Beck, 2017). To mitigate these, several strategies were employed during the data collection process (Berdida and Grande, 2023). The survey was administered in the participants' native language, and verbal explanations were added for each Likert-scale item to ensure clarity. Additionally, the instruments were pilot-tested prior to full administration to identify and resolve potential issues. To maintain data integrity, responses completed in less than 15 min were excluded from the final analysis. Given the participants' Turkish language proficiency, the instruments used had undergone Turkish validity and reliability testing.

3.5. Data analysis

The data obtained from the study were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 software. Descriptive statistics included mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values for continuous variables, and frequency (f) and percentage (%) values for categorical variables, presented in tabular form. Prior to analysis, normality tests were con­ ducted to examine the distribution characteristics of the variables. For continuous variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test, skewness and kurtosis values, and Q-Q plots were utilized. Accordingly, appropriate para­ metric and non-parametric tests (independent samples t-test, one-way ANOVA, Mann–Whitney U, and Kruskal–Wallis H) were used for group comparisons, and Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine relationships between variables. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the extent to which AI literacy and individual innovativeness predict AI readiness, with university included as a control variable. In addition, university was included as a control variable in the regression analysis. Bootstrap resampling (1000 samples, 95% CI) was performed to assess the robustness of the regression esti­ mates. The internal consistency of the scales was assessed using Cron­ bach's alpha reliability coefficient. A significance level of p < .05 was considered for all statistical analysis.

3.6. Ethical considerations

Before the data collection process was initiated, ethics committee approval (IRB: 30.04.2025/2025/3-9) of the XX University Noninvasive Clinical Research Ethics Committee was obtained. Partici­ pants were permitted to withdraw their responses both during and after the data collection period. The collected data was securely stored in a password-protected Google Drive, accessible only to the research team.

4. Results

The majority of the students were female (70.5%), while 29.5% were male. The mean age of participants was 21.13 years (SD = 2.80). Most of the students were single (97.7%). A total of 62.7% were enrolled at University A, while 37.3% were from University B. Regarding year of study, the highest proportion was observed among second-year students (33.0%). The majority of participants (91.4%) reported not having received prior AI training. In terms of AI application usage frequency, the most common response was “occasionally” (46.6%). More than half of the students (50.9%) self-rated their AI knowledge level as “moder­ ate.” Finally, the vast majority of participants (88.4%) reported being open to new technologies (Table 1).

In comparing the AI literacy, medical AI readiness, and individual innovativeness levels of nursing students by university, statistically significant differences were found in the AI Literacy Scale total score and in the subdimensions of Usage, Evaluation, and Ethics; in the Skills subdimension of the Medical AI Readiness Scale; and in the Individual Innovativeness Scale total score as well as the subdimensions of Opinion Leadership and Risk Taking (p < .05) (Table 2).

The mean total score of the AI Literacy Scale for students at Uni­ versity A (M = 61.36 ± 9.69) was significantly higher than that of stu­ dents at University B (M = 59.39 ± 9.96) (U = 19,724.500, p = .024). At the subdimension level, University A students scored significantly higher in Usage (U = 20,061.500, p = .045), Evaluation (U = 19,889.500, p = .032), and Ethics (U = 19,524.000, p = .015) compared to their peers at University B.

Although the total score of the Medical AI Readiness Scale did not differ significantly between universities (p = .115), students at Univer­ sity A (M = 29.43 ± 6.31) had significantly higher scores in the Skills subdimension compared to those at University B (M = 27.84 ± 5.61) (U = 19,002.000, p = .005).

Individual Innovativeness was also found to be a significant and positive predictor of AI readiness (B = 0.229, β = 0.134, p = .003). However, university was not a significant predictor of AI readiness (B = −0.001, β = 0.000, p = .999).

5. Discussion

In this study, nursing students' levels of artificial intelligence (AI) literacy, medical AI readiness, and individual innovativeness were examined together, and positive relationships among these variables were identified. The findings demonstrated that students' medical AI readiness levels were significantly predicted by both AI literacy and individual innovativeness. This result highlights that AI in nursing ed­ ucation should not be considered merely a technical competence but also a component of innovative thinking and professional adaptation.

Differences observed according to gender, year of study, and socio­ demographic variables were consistent with the diversity reported in the literature. Although gender did not have a significant effect on AI lit­ eracy in this study, male students were found to have higher levels of readiness. This result is in line with findings from some studies reporting that male students demonstrate higher levels of technological literacy (Kaya et al., 2025; Luki´c et al., 2023). However, other research, such as that of Hamedani et al. (2023) and Yalcinkaya et al. (2024), suggests that gender is not a determining factor. Such differences may be asso­ ciated with variations in curricular content, cultural context, and stu­ dents' exposure to technology (Hamedani et al., 2023; Yalcinkaya et al., 2024). The finding that the majority of students had not previously received AI training yet rated their knowledge at a moderate level is consistent with similar findings in both national and international literature.

In comparisons between universities, students at University A scored higher in AI literacy and the skills dimension, which is noteworthy. This result parallels the findings of Akca Sumengen et al. (2025) and ¨Ozdemir Aydın et al. (2025), who highlighted that institutional differences and clinical practice experiences shape students' awareness of and ability to use AI. These findings suggest that the content of education, techno­ logical resources, and teaching approaches in different institutions can influence students' knowledge and attitudes toward technology (Chiu et al., 2024). However, when university was included as a control var­ iable in the regression analysis, it was not found to be a significant predictor of AI readiness. This suggests that individual-level factors, rather than institutional differences, play a more central role in explaining AI readiness among nursing students.

In particular, the finding that students scored higher in AI use and skills during periods of intensive clinical experience is consistent with previous studies (Abou Hashish and Alnajjar, 2024; Aydınlar et al., 2024). The diversity of educational opportunities may be a determining factor in students' ability to adopt technology and develop innovative thinking.

The individual innovativeness scores of the participating students were found to be higher in the upper years of study. This supports studies emphasizing that as the educational process advances, students' problem-solving skills and innovative attitudes improve (Roddy and Polfuss, 2020). Furthermore, the finding that students who had received AI training demonstrated significantly higher levels of individual inno­ vativeness suggests that education is an important factor in fostering innovative attitudes. Students who used AI applications more frequently, rated their knowledge as “good,” and reported openness to new technologies had higher scores in both AI Literacy Scale (AILS) and Medical AI Readiness (MAIR), underscoring the critical role of techno­ logical experience and openness in the learning process (Heydari et al., 2023). Correlation analyses revealed that AI Literacy was moderately and positively associated with both Medical AI Readiness and Individual Innovativeness. A low but significant positive relationship was also identified between Medical AI Readiness and Individual Innovativeness. These results indicate that individuals with higher AI literacy are more likely to display readiness for technology and maintain innovative at­ titudes. Similar findings in the literature emphasize that individuals with higher levels of technology literacy tend to adopt innovative ways of thinking and adapt more easily to technological applications (O'Connor et al., 2023; Roddy and Polfuss, 2020). 5.1. Strengths and limitations

The most important contribution of this study is that it is among the first to examine artificial intelligence (AI) literacy, medical AI readiness, and individual innovativeness together in nursing students. In this respect, the study provides a new and valuable perspective to literature. Moreover, the fact that the sample size exceeded the calculated mini­ mum requirement (n = 440) allowed for stronger statistical evaluation of the findings. Another strength is that the scales used in this study had previously been validated for Turkish, and high internal consistency coefficients were also obtained here, supporting the reliability of the data.

However, several limitations should be taken into account. Since the study was limited to two universities, the generalization of the results to all nursing students should be made with caution. The use of self-report forms to collect data carries the risk of responses being influenced by social desirability bias. Given the cross-sectional design, the relation­ ships among variables cannot be interpreted within a causal framework. Furthermore, as AI-related educational content and inter-institutional differences were not analyzed in detail, the sources of variation be­ tween universities could not be fully explained.

5.2. Implications for nursing practice and policy

The findings of this study present important implications for nursing education and health policies. First, the strong association of nursing students' artificial intelligence (AI) literacy with readiness and innova­ tiveness highlights the necessity of including AI-related courses and practices in nursing curricula. AI-focused content should not be limited to theoretical knowledge but should also be reinforced with clinical scenarios and simulation-based applications.

The differences observed between the universities in this study particularly underscore the importance of clinical practice diversity. Students who have the opportunity to complete clinical experiences in multiple institutions encounter a broader variety of cases and healthcare delivery models, thereby enriching their learning processes. Conversely, when clinical practice opportunities are limited, students are exposed to fewer cases and experiences, which may indirectly influence both the competencies gained through education and their readiness for inno­ vative technologies such as AI.

At the policy level, preparing nurses for AI technologies is crucial to ensuring the sustainability of digital transformation in healthcare sys­ tems. National nursing education policies should include standards aimed at fostering students' technology literacy and innovative thinking skills. Moreover, in determining the locations where nursing schools are established, not only student enrollment capacity but also the adequacy of case diversity and clinical practice opportunities should be consid­ ered. In this regard, the Ministry of Health and accreditation bodies for nursing education should take AI-focused core competencies and clinical practice diversity into account when updating curricula and planning educational institutions.

6. Conclusion

This study is among the first to examine artificial intelligence (AI) literacy, medical AI readiness, and individual innovativeness simulta­ neously in nursing students. The findings demonstrated that AI literacy levels were associated with both readiness for medical AI and innova­ tiveness, and that these three domains are interconnected dimensions shaping students' preparedness for the digital transformation of healthcare. Significant differences across universities and demographic variables highlighted the influence of educational context and prior exposure to AI on students' competencies. Regression analyses further indicated that AI literacy and individual innovativeness significantly predicted medical AI readiness, together explaining approximately onethird of its variance.

Overall, this study provides empirical evidence that nursing students' AI-related knowledge, readiness, and innovativeness can be evaluated in an integrated framework. These findings underscore the necessity of strengthening AI-focused content within nursing curricula and offer a basis for future research aiming to support the integration of emerging technologies into nursing education and practice.
