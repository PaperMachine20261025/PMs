Critical thinking and AI-Assisted creativity in engineering education: differences between Undergraduate, Master’s, and doctoral students

Huifen Guo1 · Zhen Zhou2 · Fengqi Ma1 · Zhijun Ning1

Received: 28 April 2025 / Accepted: 12 November 2025 © The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2025

Abstract As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes integral to engineering education, under­ standing how students develop AI-assisted creativity, the ability to generate original and valuable outcomes in collaboration with AI tools, is increasingly important. This study explores how critical thinking, creative self-efficacy, and perceived organiza­ tional support influence AI-assisted creativity among 729 engineering students (252 undergraduates, 203 master’s, and 274 doctoral). Results show that critical thinking directly enhances AI-assisted creativity and indirectly strengthens it through cre­ ative self-efficacy, which serves as a key mediator. While perceived organizational support does not directly affect creativity, it amplifies the impact of critical thinking. These relationships differ by educational level: critical thinking has a stronger effect among doctoral students, and organizational support varies in influence between master’s and doctoral cohorts, whereas creative self-efficacy remains stable across groups. This research advances theoretical understanding by identifying the key mechanisms through which critical thinking, creative self-efficacy, and perceived organizational support shape AI-assisted creativity.

Keywords  AI-assisted creativity · Critical thinking · Engineering education · Creative self-efficacy · Perceived organizational support

1  Introduction

As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly embedded in education (Hutson & Cotroneo, 2023), engineering programs face new challenges in developing students’ critical thinking and creativity. These skills are central to creativity and

problem-solving (Habib et al., 2024). Their development varies across academic levels. Undergraduate education builds inquiry skills and structured problemsolving. Master’s programs promote analytical reasoning and knowledge integra­ tion. Doctoral study focuses on independent critique and original contribution (Gajda, 2016). Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence (GAI) have created new opportunities to support these skills. Tools such as ChatGPT pro­ vide prompts, feedback, and alternative perspectives that improve analytical and creative thinking (Qawqzeh, 2024). When used effectively, GAI can help undergraduates explore diverse solutions, support master’s students in synthe­ sizing complex ideas, and assist doctoral researchers in refining questions and frameworks.

In education, creativity means producing ideas that are both novel and valu­ able (Corazza, 2016; Runco & Jaeger, 2012). It involves originality, or the cre­ ation of unique ideas based on disciplinary knowledge, and appropriateness, which ensures that ideas are purposeful and relevant (Sternberg & Lubart, 1995; Beghetto & Kaufman, 2014). AI-assisted creativity builds on this concept by viewing AI as a partner in the creative process (Mok et al., 2025). Human and AI collaboration allows outcomes that neither could achieve alone. AI can support stages such as idea generation and resource gathering, while students maintain control over authentic­ ity and final direction (Vinchon et al., 2023; Runco, 2025). Research has shown that AI can enhance creativity in brainstorming, storytelling, and visual design when students use it thoughtfully (Manditereza & Chamboko-Mpotaringa, 2024; Rong et al., 2022; Doshi & Hauser, 2024). Critical thinking has roots in philosophy, psy­ chology, and education. It includes both reflective dispositions and cognitive skills. From a philosophical view, it involves curiosity, open-mindedness, and fair rea­ soning (Facione, 2000; Ennis, 1985). From a cognitive view, it includes analyzing information, evaluating evidence, and solving problems (Glaser, 1941; Halpern, 1998; Sternberg, 2020). These skills help learners question assumptions, integrate knowledge, and make reasoned judgments (Paul & Elder, 2019). Although many studies link critical thinking and creativity, little is known about how they inter­ act in AI-supported learning in engineering education. The roles of creative selfefficacy, meaning students’ confidence in their creativity (Naderi et al., 2009), and perceived organizational support, meaning institutional encouragement of innova­ tion (Akgunduz et al., 2018), also remain underexplored, especially across different academic levels. This study examines how critical thinking, creative self-efficacy, and perceived organizational support influence AI-assisted creativity among under­ graduate, master’s, and doctoral engineering students. It tests the direct, mediating, and moderating relationships among these factors and compares their effects across academic levels using multi-group analysis. The study combines cognitive, moti­ vational, and contextual perspectives in one framework. It also provides evidence for stage-specific educational strategies that can enhance AI-assisted creativity in engineering education.

2  Hypothesis development

2.1  The relationship between critical thinking and AI-Assisted creativity

Extensive research over the past few decades has examined the dynamic relation­ ship between creativity and critical thinking (Akpur, 2020). Pioneering studies of creativity and critical thinking have revealed a moderate association between these intellectual processes (Gibson et al., 1968). Recent findings have indicated that AI tools can enhance students’ capacity for analysis and introspection, indirectly foster­ ing their development of creativity (Gadzella & Penland, 1995). Education research has underscored a strong synergy among AI, critical thinking, and creativity (Daju & Moldovan, 2024). This suggests that AI’s effectiveness in cultivating creativity via critical thinking may hinge on the disciplinary context (Saritepeci & Yildiz Durak, 2024). Recent literature highlights critical thinking as a catalyst for creativity (Dwyer et al., 2025), but concerns persist about the risks of overreliance on AI technologies. Suppose students delegate too much cognitive load to AI, such as in problem resolu­ tion or idea generation. In that case, they may diminish their independent reasoning, potentially undermining both critical thinking and creativity, which are essential in engineering education (George et al., 2024). Furthermore, research to date has pro­ vided limited clarity on how these dynamics unfold across various educational stages (Runco, 2025). There is a notable scarcity of empirical investigations into whether the interplay of critical thinking and AI-enhanced creativity differs among undergrad­ uate, master’s, and doctoral students in engineering. This knowledge deficit ham­ pers the development of tailored pedagogical approaches that effectively integrate AI while preserving students’ cognitive independence. Addressing this gap is essential for formulating evidence-based instructional strategies that optimally nurture both analytical and creative competencies throughout the engineering education contin­ uum. Based on the above literature review, we developed the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1  Critical thinking exerts a significant positive influence on AI-assisted creativity.

2.2  The relationship between critical thinking and creative self-efficacy

Self-efficacy, conceptualized as confidence in one’s ability to achieve desired out­ comes in specific scenarios, profoundly influences an individual’s behavioral choices (Bandura, 1997). This conviction governs the aspirations individuals set, the strate­ gies they deploy, and the resilience they exhibit in pursuit of their goals. Notably, it is not the possession of skills but belief in one’s capacity that shapes performance (Lemons, 2010). Given its motivational potency, self-efficacy is indispensable for cul­ tivating creative distinction (Karwowski et al., 2012). Creative self-efficacy, under­ stood as confidence in one’s ability to produce original and valuable work, extends Bandura’s (1997) theoretical framework to the domain of innovation. Research has since affirmed the critical role of creative self-efficacy in driving inventiveness across individual, group, and organizational contexts (Carmeli & Schaubroeck, 2007). Fur­

thermore, creative self-efficacy fosters a robust sense of creative identity and elevates creativity (Karwowski, 2016).

Although creative potential is near-ubiquitous (Spuzic et al., 2016), many indi­ viduals withdraw from intellectually demanding tasks due to inadequate confidence in their creativity (Rogaten & Moneta, 2016). Scholarship has consistently linked creative self-efficacy to exceptional achievements (Puente-Diaz & Cavazos Arroyo, 2016). Individuals who excel creatively display intellectual inquisitiveness, embrace rigorous challenges, utilize versatile cognitive approaches, and persist despite obsta­ cles—qualities inherently tied to creative production (Beghetto, 2006). Longitudinal research has robustly established creative self-efficacy as a predictor of innovative success, substantiating a causal connection between creative self-efficacy and cre­ ative outcomes (Chen, 2016). Additionally, Beghetto et al. (2011) found that stu­ dents with heightened creative self-efficacy exhibited superior ingenuity in scientific pursuits. Contemporary analyses have revealed that creative self-efficacy serves as a pivotal mediator between analytical reasoning and AI-enhanced creativity: cuttingedge AI technologies in higher education significantly enhance students’ confidence in their creativity, thereby fostering inventive thought and enhancing academic per­ formance in technology-driven contexts (Wang et al., 2023). Based on the above literature review, we developed the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2  Critical thinking exerts a significant positive influence on creative self-efficacy.

2.3  The mediating role of creative self-efficacy

Research examining the connection between creativity and creative self-efficacy has grown substantially. Most studies have indicated that creative output is positively associated with individuals’ confidence in their general abilities and their specific creative skills, although findings differ regarding the extent of this connection. Some research has reported a strong link, with correlations as high as 0.85 (Chuang et al., 2010), while other studies have found a weak or no connection, with correla­ tions ranging from − 0.04 to 0.15 (Richter et al., 2012; Simmons et al., 2014). These variations point to factors that may influence the relationship between creativity and creative self-efficacy. Creative self-efficacy has also been shown to act as a bridge between critical thinking and creativity. For example, studies have reported that critical thinking supports creativity, with creative self-efficacy channeling this effect (Yang et al., 2020). One study found that a tendency for critical thinking predicted both a sense of creative identity and scientific creativity, with creative identity fully accounting for the link between critical thinking and creative outcomes (Qiang et al., 2020). Another study reported that creative self-efficacy partially explained the con­ nection between students’ critical thinking and their creative performance (Suherman & Vidákovich, 2024).

In contexts where AI supports creative work, this mediating role becomes even more salient. Learners with stronger creative self-efficacy are more likely to engage critically with AI outputs, adapt them for novel purposes, and integrate them into original problem-solving processes. Conversely, students with low creative self-effi­

cacy may rely passively on AI-generated content, reducing opportunities to refine their own critical thinking and creative skills. Thus, the interplay between creative self-efficacy and AI use may determine whether AI functions as a tool that ampli­ fies individuals’ creativity or as a crutch that diminishes independent intellectual effort. Understanding this mechanism is therefore essential for developing pedagogi­ cal strategies that foster not only confidence in creativity but also the effective and responsible use of AI technologies in engineering education. Based on the above literature review, we developed the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3  Creative self-efficacy exerts a significant positive influence on AI- assisted creativity.

Hypothesis 4  Creative self-efficacy plays a mediating role between critical thinking and AI-assisted creativity.

2.4  The moderating role of perceived organizational support

Perceived organizational support refers to employees’ belief that their workplace will help them, when needed, to excel in their roles and manage challenging circum­ stances (Rhoades & Eisenberger, 2002). According to organizational support theory (Eisenberger et al., 1986), this perception stems from employees’ attributing humanlike qualities to the organization, which they assume reflects their values. Such support addresses emotional needs and mitigates the effects of stress or exhaustion (Kurtessis et al., 2017). Within Csikszentmihalyi’s (1988) systems model, perceived organizational support operates as a cultural framework, motivating employees to contribute to the organization’s innovative efforts. This framework fosters a socio­ cultural environment that nurtures creativity by facilitating knowledge exchange and collaboration among individuals (Csikszentmihalyi, 2014). Research has shown that supportive social contexts, including leadership and workplace resources, encour­ age employees to perceive organizational backing, thereby enhancing their focus on creative contributions (Thao & Kang, 2018). Perceived organizational support often serves as a moderating factor, with strong such support cultivating trust, a sense of belonging, and commitment to long-term organizational goals (Rhoades & Eisen­ berger, 2002). Conversely, weak perceived organizational support can trigger nega­ tive behaviors, such as disengagement and tension (Kurtessis et al., 2017).

(Al-Taie, and Khattak, 2024) proposed that when individuals perceive organi­ zational support, they infer that the organization values their contributions. These perceptions impact how learners interpret the opportunities and risks associated with AI. Perceiving organizational support for experimentation, such as permission to explore new tools or trial environments, helps learners treat AI as an actionable instrument for iterative prototyping and empirical testing (Babu & Kushwaha, 2024). This support fosters AI literacy, enabling students to critically interrogate, adapt, and integrate AI outputs rather than accepting them uncritically (Shen & Cui, 2024). Per­ ceiving recognition for originality, transparent attribution of AI use, and processbased improvement aligns incentives so that students treat AI outputs as stimuli for further refinement rather than as finished products (Chiu, 2025). Conversely, when

organizational signals are absent, inconsistent, or ambiguous, learners may perceive a lack of support, which can lead to superficial, expedient, or non-reflective AI use, undermining creative ownership and deeper learning. Crucially, because perceived organizational support is a subjective appraisal, identical organizational provisions can yield different outcomes depending on communication, leadership framing, and assessment practices—what the organization signals matters as much as what it pro­ vides. These perceptions not only influence learners’ engagement with AI tools but also shape their confidence in their creativity (Aldabbas et al., 2025). In particular, learners who perceive supportive organizational environments are more likely to develop higher creative self-efficacy, which in turn may impact AI-assisted creativity (Fig. 1). Therefore, we developed the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5  Perceived organizational support exerts a significant positive influence on AI-assisted creativity.

Hypothesis 6  Perceived organizational support plays a moderating role between cre­ ative self-efficacy and AI-assisted creativity.

3  Method

3.1  Research procedure

We investigated the influence of critical thinking on AI-assisted creativity among engineering students at different academic levels, with creative self-efficacy as a medi­ ating role and perceived organizational support as a moderating role. We conducted the research using a partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) framework. A total of 729 participants were recruited, comprising 252 undergraduate students, 203 master’s students, and 274 doctoral students, ensuring diverse represen­ tation across academic levels. We collected data through an online survey distributed to engineering students at eight universities, targeting those actively engaged in AI- assisted creativity. The survey included validated scales to measure critical thinking,

AI-assisted creativity, creative self-efficacy, and perceived organizational support. We informed the participants of the study’s purpose and obtained their consent before data collection. After collecting the data, we screened the responses for complete­ ness, resulting in a final sample of 729 valid responses. The data were then analyzed using SmartPLS (v. 4.1) software to assess the measurement model, structural model, and additional robustness checks (finite mixture PLS segmentation [FIMIX-PLS], measurement invariance of composite models [MICOM] analysis, a non-linearity check, and an endogeneity test) were performed to ensure the reliability of the find­ ings across the three groups. In addition, the research hypotheses were tested, and a multi-group analysis was conducted to compare differences among the bachelor’s, master’s, and doctoral cohorts.

3.2  Sample and data collection

A total of 777 survey responses were collected. After screening for completeness, 729 valid responses were retained for analysis (Guo et al., 2025), yielding a valid response rate of 93.8%. The sample included 612 males (84%) and 117 females (16%), distributed across academic levels as follows: 252 undergraduates (35%), 203 master’s students (28%), and 274 doctoral students (38%). To ensure a representa­ tive sample, a stratified random sampling method was employed, with participants first grouped by academic level (undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral) based on enrolment records from multiple universities and then randomly selected within each stratum to reflect the proportion of students at each level. Data were collected through an online survey targeting engineering students, with validated scales designed to measure critical thinking, AI-assisted creativity, creative self-efficacy, and perceived organizational support. The survey was distributed via email invitations, and the par­ ticipants were provided with informed consent forms outlining the study’s purpose and their rights. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of the School of Education, Guangzhou University (GZHUSE2024001).

3.3  Survey questionnaire

At the outset of the online questionnaire, respondents were screened with the follow­ ing item: “Have you used one or more GAI tools or platforms (for example, ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot, Beautiful.ai, DALL·E, or similar) to assist with coursework, proj­ ects, or creative/technical tasks?” (Yes/No). Only participants who answered “Yes” were permitted to proceed to the full survey. We intentionally adopted a tool-agnostic approach: participants were not restricted to any single GAI system because different platforms offer distinct affordances (e.g., ChatGPT is optimized for natural-language generation, GitHub Copilot for code completion and programming assistance, and Beautiful.ai for automatic slide layout and design). The principal interest of this study was participants’ direct experience using GAI tools and the reflective and cognitive processes they reported during use (prompt generation, revision, evaluation, and inte­ gration), rather than the effects of a single proprietary system.

The survey questionnaire consisted of two sections. The first was designed to col­ lect demographic information, including the participants’ gender and educational

level. The second section comprised a self-report measure assessing four central constructs derived from the study’s conceptual framework: AI-assisted creativity, critical thinking, creative self-efficacy, and perceived organizational support. The participants responded to each item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Detailed descriptions of the scales, including item composition and theoretical grounding, are presented in Appendix A.

To assess AI-assisted creativity, which exhibited a composite reliability of 0.922, a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.919, and an average variance extracted of 0.673, we employed the AI-Assisted Creativity Scale developed by Mok et al. (2025). This rigorously validated instrument consists of seven items designed to measure creativity in AI- supported contexts. The scale was adapted to the current study to reflect the specific learning and project-based contexts encountered by undergraduate engineering stu­ dents. Revisions were undertaken to ensure contextual relevance, with sample items such as “I have utilized AI tools, such as chatbots, to generate innovative ideas or novel approaches for my engineering projects or assignments.”

To evaluate the students’ critical thinking, which exhibited a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.948, a composite reliability of 0.948, and an average variance extracted of 0.865, we utilized a six-item scale adapted from Hwang et al. (2023). This carefully vali­ dated instrument was designed to measure critical thinking skills in educational con­ texts. The scale captures key dimensions of evaluative and reflective reasoning. An illustrative item is “I do not accept AI-generated outputs as inherently accurate.”

To measure creative self-efficacy, which exhibited a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.883, a composite reliability of 0.887, and an average variance extracted of 0.740, we employed a four-item scale adapted from Lamb et al. (2025). This meticulously validated instrument is designed to assess students’ confidence in applying creative thinking to problem-solving in the field of engineering. For instance, a representative item reads, “I am confident in my capacity to devise innovative solutions to complex engineering challenges.”

Perceived organizational support, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.886, a compos­ ite reliability of 0.905, and an average variance extracted of 0.743, was evaluated using a four-item measure. This instrument, grounded in the conceptual framework established by Eisenberger et al. (1986), was developed to assess the extent to which individuals perceive support from their organization. The scale reflects the extent to which students feel recognized and supported by their academic institution. A sample item is “I perceive that my engineering department values my academic and profes­ sional aspirations.”

3.4  Conceptual framework of Student–AI contribution in creativity

To delineate the scope of this study and clarify the role of student versus machine contributions, a conceptual framework was developed to illustrate the dynamic pro­ cess of student–AI collaboration in creativity (see Fig. 2). The framework empha­ sizes that AI-assisted creativity is not equivalent to machine-generated creativity. Rather, it represents the synergistic outcome of student–AI interaction, in which stu­ dents’ critical thinking and creative self-efficacy play decisive roles. Specifically, stu­ dents engage in prompt creation by formulating problems, defining tasks, and setting

Students subsequently undertake evaluation and integration, refining and selecting outputs to ensure originality, use­ fulness, and applicability. The final creative outcome thus reflects a co-constructive process, where student cognitive and motivational resources are augmented, but not supplanted, by the computational capacity of AI. This framework was introduced in response to the broader concern that studies on AI in education might inadvertently overemphasize the role of technology, thereby conflating machine output with stu­ dent creativity. By situating creativity within this student-centered framework, the present study underscores that what is measured is students’ creativity, with AI serv­ ing as a supportive tool rather than an autonomous creative agent.

4.2  Structural model assessment

To evaluate the predictive relevance of the structural model, the cross-validated redundancy approach was employed via the blindfolding procedure in SmartPLS (v. 4.1). The resulting Q² values (AI-assisted creativity: Q² = 0.404; creative selfefficacy: Q² = 0.387; and critical thinking: Q² = 0.500)—exceeded the recommended threshold of 0 (Urbach & Ahlemann, 2010), indicating that the model showed strong predictive relevance for all endogenous latent constructs. In addition, the overall model fit was assessed using the goodness-of-fit (GOF) index, which was calculated at 0.576. According to the cut-off criterion proposed by Tenenhaus et al. (2005), this value surpasses the threshold for a large model fit (GOF > 0.25), suggesting that the model exhibited substantial explanatory power and overall adequacy in capturing the variance in the data.

4.3  Robustness check

To ensure the robustness of the results of SEM, Sarstedt et al. (2020) proposed a com­ prehensive approach within the PLS-SEM framework, addressing nonlinear effects, endogeneity, and unobserved heterogeneity. This robustness check encompasses three key analytical steps. First, FIMIX-PLS analysis is employed to detect bias due

to unobserved heterogeneity by identifying latent subgroups within the sample and examining differences in PLS-SEM parameters across these subgroups. Second, a non-linearity check is conducted to assess whether the relationships between the vari­ ables deviate from the assumption of linearity in PLS-SEM. If passed, this test con­ firms linearity; if failed, and significant nonlinearity is detected, the test highlights the need to account for nonlinear effects. Third, an endogeneity test is conducted to evaluate whether endogeneity issues exist by checking for correlations between the independent variables and the error term, ensuring that statistical assumptions are not violated and that the parameter estimates remain reliable. Our FIMIX-PLS results (see Table 2) identified three distinct segments within the sample, confirming the presence of heterogeneity. Additionally, the relationships between variables were found to be linear, and no endogeneity issues were detected, collectively validating the robustness of the structural model.

The non-linearity check and endogeneity test results are presented in Table 3. The nonlinearity test showed nonsignificant results (p > 0.05), indicating a linear rela­ tionship between the constructs. The endogeneity analysis revealed that all Gauss­ ian copula estimates were nonsignificant (p > 0.05), suggesting that no endogeneity issues were present.

Our MICOM permutation analysis (Shafaei et al., 2019) confirmed full measure­ ment invariance for all of the constructs (AI-assisted creativity, creative self-efficacy, critical thinking, and perceived organizational support) across groups, indicating that the measurement model was consistent in terms of structure, composite scores, means, and variances. This established a reliable foundation for conducting multi­ group comparisons in the PLS-SEM framework, ensuring that any observed group differences were due to theoretical variations rather than measurement inconsisten­ cies. 4.4  Hypothesis testing

As shown in Table 5, all of the hypotheses except Hypothesis 5 were supported. Specifically, both critical thinking and creative self-efficacy had significant effects on AI-assisted creativity. Critical thinking also had a significant impact on creative selfefficacy. However, perceived organizational support did not have a significant effect on AI-assisted creativity. Creative self-efficacy served as a mediator between critical thinking and AI-assisted creativity, and perceived organizational support played a moderating role in this relationship.

5  Discussion and conclusion

5.1  Discussion

5.1.1  Critical thinking, creative self-efficacy, and AI-assisted creativity

The current study demonstrates that both critical thinking and creative self-efficacy positively influence AI-assisted creativity among engineering students. This supports earlier research suggesting that cognitive skills help students evaluate and effectively incorporate AI, while confidence in their creativity encourages active use of AI tools (Lin & Chen, 2024). However, other research shows more mixed results. Some stud­ ies warn that overreliance on AI might reduce originality or promote conformity to algorithmic patterns, resulting in less diverse solutions compared to those produced independently by students (Hirpara et al., 2025; Klingbeil et al., 2024). These find­ ings emphasize that whether AI enhances creativity depends on how well students critically examine, modify, and build upon its outputs. Therefore, critical thinking and creative self-efficacy are crucial for AI to effectively foster creativity. Addition­ ally, in real classroom settings, we need to consider AI’s potential downsides, such as encouraging dependence, hindering independent thought, or leading students to accept flawed assumptions (Shahzad et al., 2025a). To maximize its benefits, AI should be integrated in ways that promote creative exploration while encouraging thoughtful reflection, balancing its advantages with its limitations.

The comparative analysis across academic levels adds nuance to this picture. The effect of critical thinking on AI-assisted creativity was significantly stronger for doctoral students compared to undergraduates, whereas no such differences were observed between undergraduates and master’s students, or between master’s and doctoral students. This pattern partially reflects earlier observations that advanced students may apply critical thinking in more specialized ways, shaped by deeper disciplinary knowledge and research experience (Rana et al., 2025; Gimenez et al., 2024). By contrast, undergraduate and master’s students appear to converge in their early development of cognitive dispositions and technological fluency, which may explain the absence of significant differences between these groups. These findings suggest that while critical thinking and creative self-efficacy are broadly foundational for AI-assisted creativity, the developmental trajectories of students influence how these factors manifest across different stages of higher education. For educational practice, this implies the importance of embedding opportunities for critical think­ ing and confidence-building in curricula, while tailoring instructional approaches to the needs of students at varying levels of expertise. This study’s findings, based on a sample of engineering students, offer valuable insights into AI’s role in foster­ ing creativity. While these results hold promise for broader applications across other disciplines, additional research would help confirm their generalizability to diverse educational contexts.

5.1.2  The mediating role of creative self-efficacy

The results show that creative self-efficacy mediates the link between critical think­ ing and AI-assisted creativity, supporting theories that see self-efficacy as a psycho­ logical bridge between cognitive skills and creative output (Shahzad et al., 2025b). In this model, students’ ability to analyze and evaluate problems does not directly lead to creativity; instead, it boosts confidence to experiment, adapt, and develop new solutions, which then encourages more effective engagement with creative tasks. This finding builds on previous ideas that highlight the motivational and emotional aspects of creativity, demonstrating that cognitive skills and psychological traits interact dynamically in AI-supported learning environments (Moșoi et al., 2025). While our results agree with studies that identify self-efficacy as a strong predictor of creativity, other research has shown weaker or inconsistent effects. Some studies sug­ gest that high self-efficacy can lead to complacency or overconfidence, decreasing the willingness to revise or critically assess AI-generated outputs (Lin & Wang, 2025; Zhang & Xu, 2025). In such cases, students might accept algorithmic suggestions too easily, limiting rather than expanding creative exploration. These contrasting findings underscore the dual nature of creative self-efficacy: while it can empower learners to use AI tools creatively, it also poses risks if not paired with reflective and critical thinking.

Comparative analysis across undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral students revealed no significant differences in the mediating effect of self-efficacy, suggest­ ing that its role is relatively stable across stages of higher education. This stabil­ ity contrasts with expectations that self-efficacy might develop incrementally with academic seniority (Miao et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2024). One interpretation is that students’ confidence in their creativity is established early in their educational tra­ jectory and remains influential regardless of disciplinary advancement. The impor­ tance of integrating opportunities to build creative self-efficacy alongside instruction in analytical skills. Strategies for cultivating self-efficacy should be balanced with maintaining critical thinking of AI-generated material (Liu et al., 2025). Given that our findings derive from engineering students, further research is needed to explore whether similar mediating dynamics apply in other disciplinary contexts, where the interplay between cognitive skills, self-belief, and AI engagement may take different forms (Jansen et al., 2025).

5.1.3  The moderating role of perceived organizational support

Perceived organizational support significantly and positively moderated the relation­ ship between creative self-efficacy and AI-assisted creativity. This suggests that stu­ dents with high levels of creative self-efficacy are more capable of transforming their confidence into creativity when they perceive their institution as supportive. Prior research has highlighted that organizational support strengthens the link between per­ sonal resources and creative outcomes. For example, Aldabbas et al. (2025) empha­ sized that employees who feel valued and supported by their organization are more motivated to take risks and apply their skills in creative ways. Similarly, Yang and Zhou (2022) demonstrated that perceived organizational support enhances the effect

of self-efficacy on creative behavior in learning settings. Supportive environments thus act less as direct generators of creativity and more as amplifiers of motivation, aligning with O’Mahony et al. (2024), who argued that contextual resources magnify individual cognitive strengths. However, the role of perceived organizational support is not unconditionally positive. Supportive structures can provide valuable resources, such as technical training, access to AI tools, and mentorship, that lower barriers to experimentation. But if these supports become overly prescriptive, they risk limiting students’ willingness to take intellectual risks or pursue unconventional approaches. Research indicates that while resource provision enhances confidence and compe­ tence, autonomy is equally critical for sustaining creativity (Hunter-Doniger, 2024). In the context of AI-assisted learning, institutions that empower students to explore AI applications freely, while also offering guidance and safeguards, are more likely to cultivate genuine creative engagement.

The comparative analysis further indicated that perceived organizational support exerted stronger moderating effects among doctoral students than among master’s or undergraduate students. This difference may be attributed to the higher levels of independence and uncertainty inherent in doctoral research, which make institutional resources and supervisory guidance more salient. Prior studies support this interpre­ tation: Shang et al. (2024) found that doctoral students’ research productivity and cre­ ativity are highly contingent on the quality of supervisory and institutional support. This is also consistent with studies in professional contexts showing that workers in less structured roles are more sensitive to organizational support compared with employees in routine or highly standardized jobs (Yiming et al., 2024). Thus, the sig­ nificance of perceived organizational support as a moderator is not uniform but varies with students’ academic stage and the degree of autonomy in their learning tasks.

5.2  Conclusion

Hypothesis testing revealed that critical thinking exerted a significant positive influ­ ence on both AI-assisted creativity and creative self-efficacy, and creative self-efficacy also significantly enhanced AI-assisted creativity. Creative self-efficacy mediated the relationship between critical thinking and AI-assisted creativity, and perceived orga­ nizational support positively moderated this association. However, perceived orga­ nizational support did not exhibit a direct significant effect on AI-assisted creativity. Comparative analysis across the three academic levels highlighted nuanced differ­ ences: the impact of critical thinking on AI-assisted creativity differed significantly between undergraduate and doctoral students, but not between undergraduate and master’s students or master’s and doctoral students. Similarly, the influence of per­ ceived organizational support on AI-assisted creativity differed significantly between master’s and doctoral students but not across other pairwise comparisons.

This study advances the field by integrating critical thinking, creative self-effi­ cacy, and perceived organizational support into a unified framework, whereas prior research has typically examined these constructs in isolation. In doing so, it extends existing theoretical models of AI-assisted creativity by capturing their combined influence across academic stages. Methodologically, the multilevel design provides novel comparative insights among undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral engineering

students. Pedagogically, the findings offer an evidence base for stage-sensitive inter­ ventions aimed at enhancing engineering students’ creativity with AI support. While our findings highlight the potential of AI to support creativity, caution is warranted due to concerns about its potential negative effects on students’ independent thinking and intrinsic motivation.

6  Implications

6.1  Theoretical implications

Our findings enhance theoretical frameworks by clarifying the roles of critical think­ ing, creative self-efficacy, and perceived organizational support in AI-assisted cre­ ativity within engineering education. The strong positive effects of critical thinking and creative self-efficacy on AI-assisted creativity expand self-efficacy theories (Acosta-Enriquez et al., 2025) to include AI-driven innovation. The mediating role of creative self-efficacy between critical thinking and AI-assisted creativity improves creativity models (Li & Qi, 2025), emphasizing a key psychological mechanism in technology-based creative processes. Notably, while perceived organizational sup­ port did not have a direct impact on AI-assisted creativity, our finding about its posi­ tive moderating role in the relationship between creative self-efficacy and AI-assisted creativity enriches organizational support theory (Aldabbas et al., 2025), indicating that institutional support boosts the influence of self-efficacy in academic settings. Additionally, differences across academic levels, especially between undergraduate and doctoral students, reflect distinct organizational needs, with doctoral students needing more funding support for research and publication than undergraduates and master’s students. These insights offer a comprehensive theoretical perspective for understanding AI-assisted creativity across different academic stages.

6.2  Practical implications

This study offers actionable insights for engineering education stakeholders aim­ ing to enhance AI-assisted creativity. The significant influence of critical thinking on AI-assisted creativity suggests that educators should prioritize integrating criti­ cal thinking training into curricula across all academic levels, particularly through exercises that leverage AI tools for problem-solving. The pivotal role of creative self-efficacy, as both a direct contributor to and a mediator between critical thinking and AI-assisted creativity, underscores the need to foster students’ confidence in their creativity, potentially through workshops or mentorship programs that emphasize AI-supported innovation. Although perceived organizational support lacked a direct impact on AI-assisted creativity, its positive moderating effect on the creative selfefficacy–AI-assisted creativity relationship indicates that institutions should provide targeted support, such as access to or encouragement to use AI resources, to amplify students’ creative potential. Moreover, the differing organizational needs across aca­ demic levels highlight the importance of tailored strategies. Doctoral students, who rely more heavily on funding for research and publication than do undergraduate and

master’s students, would benefit from enhanced financial and infrastructural support to maximize their AI-assisted creative output. Such resources may be less critical for undergraduates and master’s students, who may gain more from foundational skill-building and exposure to AI tools. These findings advocate for a differentiated approach to resource allocation and pedagogical design, ensuring that interventions align with the specific demands and developmental contexts of each academic stage in engineering education.

7  Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although AI was found to exert a signifi­ cant positive influence on creativity, its application in authentic educational settings remains complex. The pedagogical, ethical, and contextual challenges of integrating AI into real classrooms call for cautious interpretation. Future studies should exam­ ine how these tools function in diverse instructional environments and under varying institutional policies. Second, the sample was restricted to students in engineeringrelated programs. As creativity and critical thinking may manifest differently across disciplines, future research should broaden the scope to include students from the humanities, social sciences, and the arts. While the present sample size met accepted methodological standards, expanding both the scale and disciplinary diversity would enhance the external validity of the findings. Third, the cross-sectional design lim­ its our ability to assess causality or developmental dynamics. Longitudinal research would provide richer insights into how critical thinking, creative self-efficacy, and AI-assisted creativity evolve and interact over time. Such designs could also reveal whether the mediating and moderating mechanisms identified here remain stable or shift as learners advance through their academic and professional trajectories.
