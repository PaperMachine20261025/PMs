1. Introduction

The rapid integration of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) into higher education has reshaped the way students engage in creative learning. With its powerful capabilities in learning, prediction, and adaptation, GAI enables students to efficiently complete tasks such as information retrieval, question answering, and academic writing improvement, thereby freeing time and cognitive resources for creative exploration (Bandi et al., 2023; Fui-Hoon Nah et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025; Labadze et al., 2023). These affordances have made GAI a central subject in innovation and creativity research, with studies demon­ strating that its use enhances confidence and supports creative perfor­ mance (Habib et al., 2024; Wang & Wang, 2025; Zhu et al., 2024). With the rapid integration of generative artificial intelligence (AI) into higher education, its influence on stu­ dents’ creative development has drawn growing attention. However, little research has systematically examined how AI literacy (AIL) shapes university students’ creative personal identity (CPI). To address this gap, this study developed a moderated mediation model grounded in identity theory and social cognitive theory, positioning AIL as the predictor, creative self-efficacy (CSE) as the mediator, and creative mindset as the moderator.

Using a cross-sectional design, the study employed structural equation modeling and multi-group analysis. The results show that AIL significantly and positively predicts CPI, with CSE partially mediating this relationship. Moreover, fixed and growth mindsets exerted distinct moderating effects across the model’s pathways. Multigroup analysis further revealed that among students with more than two years of exposure to generative AI, the impact of AIL on CPI was stronger, whereas the influence of CSE on CPI was weaker.

From both cognitive and psychological perspectives, this study clarifies the mechanisms through which AIL shapes students’ creative identity and highlights how differences in AI usage experience alter these pathways. The findings provide a theoretical foundation for understanding creative identity construction in the digital era and offer actionable insights for designing curricula and teaching practices that foster creativity in university education.

Literacy (AIL)—the knowledge, skills, and attitudes necessary to use AI critically, effectively, and ethically (Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021). A related psychological construct is Creative Personal Identity (CPI), defined as an individual’s internal recognition of creativity as central to self-concept (Beghetto & Karwowski, 2017; Jaussi et al., 2007). CPI differs from Creative Role Identity, which reflects external expectations; CPI emphasizes how individuals value and internalize creativity as part of who they are. Previous studies confirm that higher CPI levels are associated with stronger persistence in challenges, greater willingness to pursue creative opportunities, and superior creative performance (Koseoglu et al., 2017; Uddin et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2014). However, systematic research on what factors shape CPI remains limited, espe­ cially in university contexts where identity formation is particularly salient (Deng et al., 2022; Karwowski, 2015a; Lee, 2022).

To address this gap, the present study integrates Identity Theory (Stryker, 1980) and Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura, 1986) to develop a moderated mediation model. Specifically, we examine.

1. whether AI literacy positively predicts creative personal identity; 2. whether creative self-efficacy mediates the effect of AI literacy on creative personal identity; and 3. whether fixed and growth creative mindsets moderate these relationships.

By systematically analyzing these pathways, this study makes three contributions. First, it introduces AI literacy as a novel antecedent of creative identity in higher education. Second, it clarifies the mediating role of creative self-efficacy, thereby linking technological skills to psychological identity formation. Third, it reveals the moderating in­ fluence of creative mindsets, offering insights into individual differences in technology-supported creativity. Collectively, these contributions provide theoretical support for understanding how emerging technolo­ gies shape creative identity and offer practical implications for designing curricula and teaching strategies that cultivate creativity in the age of generative AI.

2. Conceptual background and hypothesis development

2.1. Theoretical background

Identity theory (Stryker, 1980) emphasizes that an individual’s identity develops and solidifies through the roles they adopt in society. Identity is not merely the acknowledgment of social roles but also an integral part of self-concept (Farmer & Kung-Mcintyre, 2003). Through adaptive strategies, individuals internalize role expectations and align their behaviors with societal demands (Wang & Cheng, 2010). Research suggests that adolescence and young adulthood are critical stages for identity formation, during which positive group interactions and recognition significantly contribute to identity development (Erikson, 1968; Hogg & Abrams, 1988; Marcia, 1966). In the higher education context, when students receive affirmation or recognition from gener­ ating creative outputs (e.g., texts, images, or designs) with generative AI, they may begin to internalize the role of “creator.” Over time, this role-based identity evolves into a deeper creative personal identity (CPI) (Creely & Blannin, 2025). Thus, identity theory provides a useful lens for understanding how students integrate technological practices into their evolving creative self-concept.

Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) (Bandura, 1986) further highlights the interplay between cognition, environment, and behavior in shaping human action. A central construct within SCT is self-efficacy, or the belief in one’s ability to accomplish specific tasks (Bandura, 2001). Applied to creativity, creative self-efficacy (CSE) reflects individuals’ confidence in successfully engaging in creative tasks (Karwowski et al., 2013). Generative AI can enhance CSE by offering immediate feedback, tangible outcomes, and opportunities for iterative practice, thereby increasing users’ sense of control and achievement (Ji et al., 2025). Moreover, by providing simulations, prompts, and exemplars, AI tools facilitate observational learning and vicarious experiences, both of which are key sources of self-efficacy in SCT. 2.2. Literature review and research hypotheses

2.2.1. AI literacy and creative personal identity Extending the traditional notion of literacy, AI literacy (AIL) refers to the capacity to critically evaluate, collaborate with, and effectively apply AI tools across contexts (Long & Magerko, 2020). Scholars generally agree that AIL comprises four dimensions: cognitive aware­ ness, proficiency in application, evaluative and creative use of AI tools, and ethical considerations (Ng et al., 2021). Yet, not all students benefit equally from generative AI. Factors such as digital readiness, socioeco­ nomic background, and critical awareness of issues like accuracy and ethics can introduce disparities in students’ ability to engage produc­ tively with AI (Hornberger et al., 2023; Valdivieso & Gonz´alez, 2025).

According to identity theory (Stryker, 1980), individuals construct and validate their self-identity through social interactions, which are influenced by situational affordances and internal beliefs. Students with higher AIL are better able to select appropriate generative AI tools, critically engage with outputs, and integrate them meaningfully into creative tasks (Wang & Wang, 2025). Such competence not only facili­ tates smoother participation in creative processes but also fosters recognition and affirmation that consolidate CPI. Empirical evidence supports this reasoning: Lee (2022) found that limited tool-use profi­ ciency and perceived difficulty undermine students’ CPI, whereas greater competence strengthens it.

Therefore, it can be inferred that students with higher AIL are more likely to sustain creative engagement and reinforce their identity as creators. Based on this reasoning, the following hypothesis is proposed.

H1. AI literacy is significantly positively correlated with university students’ creative personal identity (CPI).

2.2.2. The mediating role of creative self-efficacy Rooted in Bandura’s (1994) concept of self-efficacy, creative self-efficacy (CSE) refers to individuals’ belief in their ability to generate novel and useful ideas, overcome challenges requiring innovation, and successfully engage in creative roles (Karwowski, 2012). As a key indi­ cator of perceived creative capacity, CSE is widely recognized as a strong predictor of creative performance and innovative behavior (Royston & Reiter-Palmon, 2019). Prior research consistently shows that individuals with higher CSE are more likely to persist in creative problem-solving, display innovative thinking, and achieve creative outcomes (Capron Puozzo & Audrin, 2021; Tierney & Farmer, 2011).

From the perspective of Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) (Bandura, 1986), self-efficacy is strengthened when individuals perceive adequate external support and experience mastery in task performance. In AI-enhanced learning environments, generative AI provides abundant external resources, simplifies complex tasks, and enables interdisci­ plinary exploration (Pellas, 2023). Through real-time feedback, encouragement, and performance optimization, AI tools enhance stu­ dents’ sense of control and mastery, which in turn fosters higher CSE (Sethi & Jain, 2024).

Ji et al. (2025) found that students with higher AIL actively explored and solved problems with generative AI, thereby improving their con­ fidence in innovative activities. Similarly, Kong and Yang (2025) demonstrated experimentally that AI literacy courses deepened stu­ dents’ understanding of AI, increased their empowerment, and further boosted their CSE.

Based on this reasoning, the following hypothesis is proposed.

H2. AI literacy is significantly positively correlated with university students’ creative self-efficacy (CSE).

H3. Creative self-efficacy (CSE) is significantly positively correlated with creative personal identity (CPI).

H4. Creative self-efficacy (CSE) mediates the relationship between AI literacy (AIL) and creative personal identity (CPI).

2.2.3. The moderating role of creative mindsets Creative mindsets (CM) refer to individuals’ implicit beliefs about the nature of creativity, particularly whether creative abilities are fixed or malleable (Karwowski, 2014). A fixed creative mindset reflects the belief that creativity is innate and unchangeable, whereas a growth creative mindset emphasizes that creativity can be developed and improved with practice and effort (Royston & Reiter-Palmon, 2019).

Students with a fixed mindset are more likely to undervalue AI’s role in creativity, focus on risks, and avoid experimentation, which weakens the positive effect of AIL on CSE. In contrast, students with a growth mindset are more open to new experiences, treat AI as a resource rather than a threat, and thus amplify the positive impacts of AIL on CSE and CPI. However, growth mindset unexpectedly weakens the CSE → CPI link, as these students rely more on ongoing exploration and external feedback rather than self-efficacy alone.

Based on this reasoning, the following hypotheses are proposed.

• H5a: Fixed creative mindset moderates the relationship between AIL and CSE. • H5b: Fixed creative mindset moderates the relationship between AIL and CPI. • H5c: Fixed creative mindset moderates the relationship between CSE and CPI. • H6a: Growth creative mindset moderates the relationship between AIL and CSE.

2.3. The current study

Drawing on identity theory and Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), this study develops a moderated mediation model to examine how AI liter­ acy (AIL) influences university students’ creative personal identity (CPI). Specifically, the model proposes that AIL enhances CPI both directly and indirectly through the mediating role of creative selfefficacy (CSE). Additionally, the model incorporates creative mindsets (CM)—both fixed and growth—as moderators that shape the strength and direction of these relationships.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the conceptual framework tests four core pathways.

(1) The direct effects of AIL on CSE and CPI, and of CSE on CPI. (2) The mediating role of CSE in the AIL–CPI relationship. (3) The moderating effects of fixed and growth creative mindsets on

the pathways linking AIL, CSE, and CPI. (4) The influence of demographic factors (gender, age, and grade),

which are included as control variables to ensure robustness.

Through this framework, the study aims to clarify the psychological processes through which AIL shapes students’ creative identity, while also identifying how individual differences in mindset and experience influence this pathway in the context of generative AI.

3.1. Samples and data collection

This study adopted a cross-sectional questionnaire survey design. Data were collected from students at three universities between February and March 2025. A stratified random sampling strategy was applied based on student ID, academic year, and major. Before distrib­ uting the questionnaires, the research team coordinated with university departments to obtain student contact information. The selected stu­ dents were invited via email, text message, and social media.

Of the 1200 distributed questionnaires, 1072 were returned (response rate: 89.33 %). After excluding incomplete responses (n = 23) and those reporting no prior use of generative AI (n = 63), the final dataset included 986 valid responses. Demographic information and correlations with study variables are presented in Table 2. Participants were predominantly female (60.8 %), aged 18–22 (79.5 %), and evenly distributed across the first three academic years. 3.2. Measures

The survey consisted of two sections: (1) demographic information (gender, age, major, and prior AI experience) and (2) validated scales measuring AI Literacy (AIL), Creative Self-Efficacy (CSE), Creative Per­ sonal Identity (CPI), and Creative Mindsets (CM). All items were trans­ lated and back-translated to ensure linguistic and cultural equivalence (Brislin, 1980). Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).

• AI Literacy (AIL): Measured with a 12-item scale developed by Wang et al. (2022), comprising four dimensions: awareness, usage, evalu­ ation, and ethics (3 items each). Example items include: o “I am aware of the functions and applications of generative AI

and accurate” (evaluation), o “I consider ethical issues such as bias and fairness when using AI tools”

The overall Cronbach’s α was 0.949, with strong internal con­ sistency and validity (KMO = 0.959, Bartlett’s p < 0.001). • Creative Self-Efficacy (CSE) and Creative Personal Identity (CPI): Assessed with the Short Scale of Creative Self (SSCS) (Karwowski et al., 2018). The scale includes 5 items for CSE (e.g., “I trust my ability to solve problems creatively”) and 6 items for CPI (e.g., “Being a creative person is an important part of who I am”). Both subscales showed high reliability (CSE α = 0.927; CPI α = 0.913) and acceptable CFA fit indices. • Creative Mindsets (CM): Measured with the Creative Mindset Scale (CMS) (Karwowski, 2014), including 5 items for fixed mindset (e.g., “Creativity is something you either have or you don’t”) and 5 items for growth mindset (e.g., “Everyone can improve their creativity with practice”). Both subscales demonstrated high reliability (fixed α = 0.933; growth α = 0.902).

4.2. Confirmatory Factor Analysis

To examine the validity of the measurement model, a Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using the observed dimensions of the four constructs (AI Literacy, Creative Self-Efficacy, Creative Personal Identity, and Creative Mindsets). The overall model demonstrated satisfactory fit: χ2/df = 4.006, CFI = 0.950, TLI = 0.943, RMSEA = 0.055, SRMR = 0.038, NFI = 0.935, and GFI = 0.885. All indices fall within or close to the recommended thresholds (Marsh & Hocevar, 1985; Hu & Bentler, 1999), indicating that the proposed measurement structure adequately represents the data (see Fig. 2).

4.3. Measurement model

This study applied a two-step method for structural equation modeling analysis to validate the relationships within the research model and ensure the reliability and validity of the predictive model. In the first step, internal reliability was examined by calculating composite reliability (CR) and Cronbach’s alpha for each construct. In the second step, convergent validity was assessed using item loadings, CR, and average variance extracted (AVE), while discriminant validity was evaluated through the Fornell–Larcker criterion and the Hetero­ trait–Monotrait (HTMT) ratio.

From a methodological standpoint, these results provide strong ev­ idence that the scales employed in this study capture distinct but related constructs. Specifically, the four dimensions of AI literacy—awareness, usage, evaluation, and ethics—are empirically distinguishable, and the separation between CSE and CPI is statistically supported. Similarly, fixed and growth creative mindsets emerged as distinct latent constructs.

As shown in Table 5, all CR and Cronbach’s alpha values exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70, indicating satisfactory internal consistency across constructs. The AVE values for all latent variables were above 0.50, suggesting that the items within each construct converge adequately to measure the same underlying concept (Hair et al., 2012).

4.8. Multigroup Analysis (MGA)

This study employed a multi-group analysis (MGA) to examine whether participants’ exposure time, usage frequency, and usage dura­ tion of generative AI influenced the structural relationships in the model. MGA compares path coefficients across groups to test whether the hypothesized effects are consistent or vary under different condi­ tions (Hair et al., 2017).

Before conducting MGA, a Measurement Invariance of Composite Models (MICOM) procedure was performed to confirm that any observed group differences reflected genuine structural variations rather than differences in construct interpretation (Henseler et al., 2016). Because the three grouping variables were measured on multi-category scales, participants were reclassified into two groups for each factor: (a) exposure time (≤2 years vs. > 2 years), (b) usage frequency (≤2 times per week vs. ≥3 times per week), and (c) usage duration (<30 min vs. ≥ 30 min per session). The MICOM results are presented in Table 12. Composite invariance was established only for the exposure time grouping, indicating that MGA results are interpretable for this factor. In contrast, measurement invariance was not achieved for usage frequency and usage duration, making MGA inappropriate for these dimensions.

This study addressed this gap by employing PLS-SEM to examine the effects of AI Literacy (AIL) on CPI, the mediating role of Creative Self-Efficacy (CSE), the moderating role of creative mindsets, and potential group differ­ ences based on exposure time to generative AI. 5.2. The mediating effect of creative self-efficacy

The results confirm that CSE partially mediates the relationship be­ tween AIL and CPI (VAF = 42 %), supporting H4.

• Expected results: The mediation aligns with SCT, showing that selfefficacy functions as a psychological mechanism through which external skills and support (AIL) enhance internal identity processes (Capron Puozzo & Audrin, 2021; Wingstr¨om et al., 2024). • Novel contribution: Unlike prior research that emphasized selfefficacy as the dominant predictor of CPI, our findings reveal that AIL exerts both direct and indirect effects on CPI. This indicates that in the generative AI context, self-efficacy is necessary but not suffi­ cient—digital literacy itself plays an equally crucial role in shaping identity. • Educational implication: Educators should design AI-integrated creative tasks that simultaneously strengthen students’ self-efficacy (e.g., through scaffolded creative problem-solving) and enhance AI literacy (e.g., by training students to critically evaluate AI-generated outputs). This dual focus ensures both technological competence and psychological empowerment.

5.3. The moderating effect of creative mindsets

The study found that fixed and growth creative mindsets signifi­ cantly moderated pathways in the model, supporting H5a, H6a, H6b, and H6c.

• Expected results: Growth creative mindset amplified the positive effects of AIL on both CSE and CPI, consistent with Dweck’s (2006) theory that growth-oriented individuals perceive creativity as malleable and thus maximize the benefits of new technologies (Amabile, 1996). Fixed creative mindset weakened the AIL → CSE path, reinforcing the idea that perceiving creativity as innate limits the activation of psychological resources. • Novel contribution: A surprising finding is that growth creative mindset negatively moderated the CSE → CPI path. This suggests that for students with strong growth beliefs, creative identity formation relies less on immediate self-efficacy and more on ongoing explora­ tion, environmental feedback, and human–AI collaboration. This challenges the assumption that growth mindset always uniformly strengthens positive relationships, introducing a more nuanced view of mindset dynamics in AI-supported creativity.

5.4. Impact differences of exposure time to generative AI (MGA results)

The MGA revealed significant group differences based on exposure time to generative AI: the AIL → CPI path strengthened, while the CSE → CPI path weakened with longer exposure.

• Expected results: Longer exposure enabled students to more effec­ tively internalize AI literacy as part of their creative self-concept, consistent with identity construction theory (Oyserman, 2009). • Novel contribution: The weakening of the CSE → CPI link with longer exposure is unexpected. It suggests that as students gain more experience, their identity becomes less dependent on self-efficacy and more reliant on accumulated literacy and critical engagement with AI. This highlights a developmental shift in the psychological foundations of creative identity across different stages of AI experience. • Educational implication: For novice users, interventions should emphasize building self-efficacy to establish creative confidence. For experienced users, instructional design should shift toward advanced AI literacy—especially evaluation and ethical awareness—that directly reinforces creative identity beyond reliance on self-efficacy.

6. Implications

Drawing upon identity theory and Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), this study investigated how generative artificial intelligence influences university students’ Creative Personal Identity (CPI). By analyzing data from a large-scale questionnaire survey, most hypotheses were sup­ ported, offering both theoretical advances and practical applications.

6.1. Theoretical implications

This study contributes to theory in three main ways: First, integrating SCT and identity theory, this study explicates the mechanism by which AI Literacy (AIL) influences CPI. The findings show that AIL enhances CPI both directly and indirectly through Creative Self- Efficacy (CSE). While prior studies linked tool use to creative identity (Lee, 2022) and documented AI’s benefits in creative tasks (Khoso et al., 2025), few explored how generative AI competencies shape identity formation. By situating CSE as a mediator, this study clarifies the psy­ chological pathway through which technological literacy translates into identity, thereby extending identity theory into the digital era.

Second, this study advances research on creative mindsets (CM) by examining their moderating roles. Fixed mindset weakened the AIL → CSE path, whereas growth mindset amplified the AIL → CSE and AIL → CPI paths but unexpectedly weakened the CSE → CPI relationship. This nuanced pattern enriches existing mixed findings on creative mindset effects (Hass et al., 2016; Royston & Reiter-Palmon, 2019). The results provide a novel theoretical perspective: in AI-supported contexts, growth-oriented students may rely less on self-efficacy alone and more on continuous exploration, feedback, and human–AI collaboration when constructing creative identity.

Third, by applying Multigroup Analysis (MGA), this study reveals how generative AI exposure time shapes structural relationships. Stu­ dents with longer exposure exhibited stronger AIL → CPI effects but weaker CSE → CPI effects. This demonstrates that technological usage experience not only strengthens the psychological role of AI literacy but also reshapes how self-efficacy contributes to identity. Theoretically, this suggests that identity construction is a developmental process,

Together, these contributions highlight the value of integrating digital literacies, psychological beliefs, and usage experience into identity research, providing a more holistic understanding of how generative AI reshapes creativity-related identities.

6.2. Practical implications

The findings also offer several practical recommendations for higher education:

First, embed AI literacy into curricula as a foundation for creative development. As AIL is shown to directly and indirectly shape CPI, ed­ ucators should move beyond viewing AI literacy as a technical skill. Instead, structured AI literacy training—covering awareness, usage, evaluation, and ethics—should be systematically integrated into courses and project-based learning. Such training not only improves students’ technical competence but also empowers them to see themselves as creative individuals.

Second, design learning experiences that actively build creative selfefficacy. Since CSE mediates the AIL–CPI link, instructors should pro­ vide scaffolded creative tasks, emphasize mastery experiences, and deliver constructive feedback. These strategies help students internalize success, boosting both confidence and creative identity. Co-curricular activities, such as innovation competitions and exhibitions, can further strengthen recognition and reinforce students’ sense of belonging as creative contributors.

Third, tailor teaching strategies to students’ creative mindsets. Growth-minded students benefit from open-ended exploration and AI- supported experimentation, while fixed-minded students require addi­ tional encouragement and structured support to overcome fear of fail­ ure. Importantly, educators can help shift cognitive styles through reflective training and discourse on creativity (Dweck, 2006), enabling more students to adopt adaptive orientations toward AI-supported creativity.

Finally, adapt instruction based on students’ AI experience levels. For beginners, building self-efficacy is crucial, as confidence drives initial identity formation. For more experienced users, however, in­ struction should emphasize advanced AI literacy skills such as critical evaluation, ethical awareness, and creative application, ensuring that extended AI engagement continues to strengthen identity rather than foster overreliance.

7. Limitations and future research

Despite its valuable contributions, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged, along with corresponding directions for future research.

First, the study employed a cross-sectional survey design. While this approach clarifies relationships and mediation mechanisms among variables, it limits causal inference. Future studies could employ longi­ tudinal designs or experimental methods to strengthen causal claims. For example, Karwowski (2016) conducted two longitudinal surveys over six and twenty months to explore developmental changes in CSE and CPI, offering a model for examining how AI literacy and identity evolve over time.

Second, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires may introduce common method bias. Although this study applied procedural remedies, self-reported data are inherently limited. Future research could trian­ gulate data sources by incorporating teacher evaluations, behavioral observations, or qualitative interviews to validate findings and increase robustness. Prior studies (e.g., Wang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024) demonstrated the value of multi-source approaches in uncovering biases in CPI measurement and their implications for creative behavior.

Third, this study focused on students’ general use of generative AI without distinguishing between tool types or creative domains.

In summary, addressing these limitations in future work—through longitudinal and experimental designs, multi-source data collection, and domain-specific analysis—will deepen insights into the mechanisms through which generative AI shapes creative self-concepts and identities.

8. Conclusion

This study examined how AI Literacy (AIL) shapes university stu­ dents’ Creative Personal Identity (CPI), with Creative Self-Efficacy (CSE) as a mediator, creative mindsets as moderators, and exposure time to generative AI as a contextual factor. By integrating identity theory and Social Cognitive Theory, the study provides both theoretical and prac­ tical insights into the mechanisms through which generative AI in­ fluences students’ creative development.

The results confirm that students with higher AIL not only use AI tools more effectively but also gain greater confidence in their creative abilities, which in turn strengthens their CPI. Importantly, CSE emerged as a partial mediator, demonstrating that technological competence supports identity formation both directly and indirectly through psy­ chological self-beliefs. Moreover, mindset differences significantly sha­ ped these relationships: while fixed mindset constrained the benefits of AIL, growth mindset amplified its positive impact but unexpectedly weakened the influence of CSE on CPI. Finally, multi-group analysis revealed that students with longer AI exposure internalize literacy more deeply as part of their creative identity, while relying less on selfefficacy alone.

Together, these findings extend current understanding of CPI by positioning digital literacies, psychological beliefs, and experiential factors as interconnected drivers of identity development in the age of generative AI. The study underscores that AI literacy is not only a technical competency but also a psychological and educational resource that shapes how students see themselves as creative individuals.

For educators and institutions, the implications are clear: fostering students’ AI literacy, building their creative self-efficacy, and cultivating adaptive mindsets are essential strategies for enhancing creative iden­ tity. Instruction should also be sensitive to students’ levels of AI expe­ rience, offering confidence-building opportunities for novices and advanced literacy training for experienced users.

Although this study provides valuable contributions, it also has limitations, such as reliance on self-reported data and a cross-sectional design. Future research should adopt longitudinal or experimental ap­ proaches and diversify data sources to validate and extend these findings.

In conclusion, this study highlights the transformative role of generative AI in shaping creative identity. By bridging technology, psychology, and education, it offers a framework for understanding and supporting students’ creative growth in an era where AI is becoming an integral part of learning and innovation.
