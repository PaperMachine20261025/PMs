The serial mediating roles of AI self-efficacy and academic procrastination in the association between AI literacy and AI dependence among Chinese undergraduates

Houyu Wu a,b, Haiyang Ni c, Jiaqi Heb and Eng Hoe Wee b

aSchool of Artiﬁcial Intelligence, Neijiang Normal University, Neijiang, China; bFaculty of Education, Languages, Psychology and Music, SEGi University, Petaling Jaya, Malaysia; cGraduate School of Business, Nilai University, Nilai, Malaysia

ABSTRACT Objective: Generative artificial intelligence is now embedded in university learning, but the psychological pathways linking AI literacy with AI dependence remain unclear. This study examined whether AI self-efficacy and academic procrastination mediate the association between AI literacy and AI dependence among Chinese undergraduates. Method: A total of 417 Chinese undergraduate students aged 18–24 years completed measures of AI literacy, AI self-efficacy, academic procrastination and AI dependence. This study tested common method bias, estimated descriptive statistics and Pearson correlations, examined multicollinearity, conducted robustness checks and estimated a serial mediation model with PROCESS Model 6. Results: AI literacy was negatively associated with AI dependence (r = –.283). Bootstrapped indirect effects indicated three significant pathways: through AI selfefficacy, through academic procrastination and through the sequential pathway from AI self-efficacy to academic procrastination. The total standardised indirect effect was −.229, accounting for 55.84% of the total effect. Conclusions: AI self-efficacy and academic procrastination partially and sequentially mediated the association between AI literacy and AI dependence. The findings suggest that AI literacy may reduce maladaptive AI dependence by strengthening efficacy beliefs and reducing procrastination-related reliance on AI tools. Given the cross-sectional design, longitudinal research is needed to clarify temporal ordering.

Introduction

Generative AI and AI dependence in higher education

Generative artificial intelligence (AI), a subset of AI that produces new content in response to prompts, has rapidly become part of higher education. Students use large language models and related tools to search for information, generate ideas, translate materials, receive feedback, draft text and solve academic problems. In this context, AI dependence has become an important outcome for educational psychology and educational technology research. AI dependence differs from frequent or effective AI use because frequency alone does not indicate impaired control. It refers to a maladaptive pattern in which students rely excessively on AI tools for cognitive, functional, emotional or self-regulatory support when independent effort and judgement would be more appropriate (Wu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2024). This distinction matters because universities should not discourage all AI use. The central issue is whether students can use AI as a learning support without turning it into a substitute for planning, reasoning, problem solving and self-monitoring. Earlier studies of problematic internet use and social media addiction showed that technology-related problems often emerge when cognitive beliefs, emotional needs and self-regulatory difficulties interact (R. A. Davis, 2001; Kuss & Griffiths, 2017). Recent work on AI dependency similarly suggests that over-reliance on AI dialogue systems may affect students’ cognitive abilities and critical thinking when users accept AI outputs without sufficient evaluation (Tian & Zhang, 2025; Zhai et al., 2024). Other evidence connects problematic AI use with academic self-efficacy, stress and performance expectations (Zhang et al., 2024). Identifying how AI literacy, AI self-efficacy and academic procrastination may shape maladaptive AI dependence is therefore timely.

AI literacy, AI self-efficacy and academic procrastination

AI literacy is one such protective factor. AI literacy refers to the knowledge and competence needed to understand, use, evaluate and ethically engage with AI systems (Ng et al., 2021; B. Wang et al., 2023). The Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale (AILS) represents these capacities through self-reported awareness, usage, evaluation and ethics. It therefore captures perceived competence rather than directly observed responsible behaviour or the related construct of AI fluency. At the conceptual level, recent reviews emphasise that AI literacy should support critical understanding, responsible use and informed decision making, not only technical capability (Mustafa et al., 2024; Park, 2025). This view is consistent with technology adoption research, which shows that perceived usefulness, ease of use, facilitating conditions and behavioural control shape technology-related behaviour (F. D. Davis, 1989; Scherer et al., 2019; Venkatesh et al., 2003). However, acceptance models mainly explain why learners adopt technologies. The present study instead asks a different question: how AI literacy may reduce maladaptive dependence after AI tools have entered students’ learning routines? The theory of planned behaviour is relevant because AI-related behaviour is likely to be influenced by attitudes, perceived norms and perceived control (Ajzen, 1991; Ivanov et al., 2024; C. Wang et al., 2025). A more specific mechanism is still needed to explain how literacy becomes healthier AI engagement.

AI self-efficacy provides a cognitive mechanism for this process. Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) states that self-efficacy shapes how individuals approach challenging tasks, how much effort they invest and how persistently they cope with difficulty (Bandura, 1997; Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2020). In academic settings, selfefficacy is closely linked to learning persistence, academic achievement and self-regulated learning (Honicke & Broadbent, 2016; Richardson et al., 2012). AI self-efficacy extends this logic to students’ perceived capability to use AI tools effectively and appropriately (Y. Y. Wang & Chuang, 2024). The AISES adopts a broader operational definition that includes perceived assistance, anthropomorphic interaction, comfort with AI and technological skills, although technological skills most directly reflect perceived capability. Students with higher AI literacy may better understand AI capabilities, recognise limitations, construct appropriate prompts, evaluate AI-generated outputs and correct errors. These experiences can strengthen confidence in managing AI-supported academic tasks, consistent with recent evidence linking AI literacy to self-efficacy

Academic procrastination offers a behavioural mechanism linking efficacy beliefs to AI dependence. Procrastination is commonly defined as the voluntary delay of intended action despite expected negative consequences (Steel, 2007). The APS-S captures this construct as a concise, unidimensional tendency to delay academic tasks (Yockey, 2016). In academic contexts, procrastination is associated with poorer performance, lower study discipline, weaker learning strategies and greater stress (Credé & Kuncel, 2008; Kim & Seo, 2015; Steel & Klingsieck, 2016; Tice & Baumeister, 1997). It also has emotional and motivational dimensions. Students may delay academic tasks to avoid unpleasant feelings, uncertainty or effortful self-control (Klingsieck, 2013; Sirois & Pychyl, 2013). Self-regulated learning theories suggest that successful learners plan, monitor, adjust strategies and reflect on their progress (Broadbent & Poon, 2015; Panadero, 2017; Pintrich, 2000; Zimmerman, 2002). When students lack confidence in AI-supported tasks, they may be more likely to delay work. The reverse relationship is also plausible because procrastination may reduce mastery opportunities and weaken confidence. Delayed work may then increase reliance on AI as a quick solution under time pressure. Recent evidence also links academic self-efficacy and emotion-regulation difficulties to academic procrastination (Rad et al., 2025). In this sense, procrastination may translate low confidence and poor planning into dependence on AI tools. The proposed order is therefore a theoretically guided hypothesis rather than a confirmed causal process.

Theoretical integration, research gap and hypotheses

The theoretical logic of the proposed serial model linking AI literacy, AI self-efficacy, academic procrastination and AI dependence is grounded in SCT and the Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I- PACE) model. SCT explains the first part of the pathway because AI literacy may strengthen AI self-efficacy through knowledge, perceived control and mastery experiences (Bandura, 1997; Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2020). The I-PACE model explains the second part because maladaptive technology-related behaviours can emerge from interactions among personal characteristics, affective and cognitive responses and weakened executive control (Brand et al., 2019). In the present study, AI literacy is treated as a competence-based personal resource, AI self-efficacy as a cognitive belief, academic procrastination as a behavioural marker of self-regulatory difficulty and AI dependence as a maladaptive outcome represented by emotional, functional and cognitive dependence and loss of control (Wu et al., 2026). This integration suggests that students with stronger AI literacy may develop higher AI self-efficacy, show less academic procrastination and report lower AI dependence. It also frames AI dependence as a problem of academic self-regulation, not only as a technology-use problem.

Despite this theoretical foundation, two gaps remain. First, many studies of AI in higher education have focused on acceptance, perceived usefulness or general attitudes. Fewer have examined maladaptive dependence as a distinct outcome (Abbas et al., 2024; Lim et al., 2023; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019). Second, research on students’ AI-related competence has often treated literacy as an endpoint. Recent work links AI literacy with self-efficacy and output quality, while studies of problematic AI use examine efficacy, stress, performance or cognition in separate models (Bećirović et al., 2025; Tian & Zhang, 2025; Zhai et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Together, these streams establish relevant pairwise associations but leave their integration unresolved. Less attention has been paid to whether literacy initiates a psychological process that shapes confidence, self-regulation and dependence. It remains unclear how AI literacy becomes protective in students’ everyday academic behaviour. A serial mediation model can address this gap by testing whether the association between AI literacy and AI dependence is transmitted first through a cognitive mechanism and then through a behavioural mechanism. Such a model is suitable because AI dependence may develop gradually through repeated academic decisions rather than through a single technology-use episode.

Collectively, the present study therefore tested a theoretically guided serial mediation model among Chinese university students using SPSS PROCESS Model 6. Building on the theoretical reasoning above, we formulated the model shown in Figure 1 and proposed four hypotheses:

Methods

Recruitment

A cross-sectional survey design was used. Participants were recruited from a normal university in Southwest China. The university had 20,726 undergraduates. Its 2025 to 2026 Office of Academic Affairs enrolment register formed the sampling frame. Approximately 70% were female, consistent with the institution’s teacher-education orientation. As gender composition and major category were important structural characteristics of the institution, stratified random sampling was conducted by gender and major category. The major categories were natural sciences, social sciences, arts and education. The number selected from each stratum was proportional to its enrolment size. Within each stratum, students were selected using computergenerated random numbers and invited to participate. This strategy was used to improve alignment between the sample and the demographic structure of the university. Using Krejcie and Morgan’s (1970) finite-population sampling recommendation, a sample larger than 377 was considered sufficient for the planned analyses. In total, 450 online survey invitations were sent to the selected students. Nine declined to participate. Of the 441 returned questionnaires, 24 were excluded. Eleven were incomplete, eight showed repeated or patterned responses and five failed attention or consistency checks. After data screening, 417 valid responses were retained, yielding an effective response rate of 92.67%. The final sample closely matched the university population by gender and major category.

The survey was administered online in simplified Chinese. Two bilingual researchers independently translated the AILS, AISES and APS-S and reconciled the wording. A third bilingual researcher completed a blind back-translation. An expert panel checked semantic and cultural equivalence before a 30-student pilot outside the analytic sample. The AIDep-22 was administered in its original Chinese form. The first page described the study purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality protections and the right to withdraw without penalty. Participants were informed that the questionnaire focused on learning experiences and AI-related perceptions. No personally identifiable information was collected. The survey system was configured to prevent repeated submissions from the same account. Eligibility criteria required participants to be enrolled undergraduates aged 18–24 years, able to understand the questionnaire and willing to provide informed consent. Measures

AI literacy AI literacy was measured using the Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale (AILS) developed by B. Wang et al. (2023). This 12-item scale was designed to assess users’ self-reported competence in understanding and using AI technology across four dimensions: awareness, usage, evaluation, and ethics. Accordingly, the present study operationalised AI literacy as a multidimensional capacity that includes recognising AI technologies, using AI applications effectively, critically evaluating AI-generated information and outputs, and understanding the ethical responsibilities associated with AI use. Sample items include “I can identify the AI technology employed in the applications and products I use” (awareness), “I can use AI applications or products to improve my work efficiency” (usage), “I can evaluate the capabilities and limitations of an AI application or product after using it for a while” (evaluation), and “I always comply with ethical principles when using AI applications or products” (ethics). Items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating higher levels of AI literacy. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was .88.

AI self-eﬃcacy AI self-efficacy was assessed using the Artificial Intelligence Self-Efficacy Scale (AISES) developed by Y. Y. Wang and Chuang (2024). This 22-item scale captures individuals’ confidence in using and interacting with AI technologies or products across four dimensions: assistance, anthropomorphic interaction, comfort with AI, and technological skills. The scale was selected because it measures self-efficacy in relation to AI- specific characteristics, including perceived assistance, human-like interaction, emotional comfort during AI use, and confidence in handling AI technologies, rather than general computer or technology self-efficacy. In the original validation, technological skills most directly reflected capability, whereas the other dimensions represented broader AI efficacy-related appraisals. The total score is therefore interpreted as broad AI- specific self-efficacy rather than narrow task-specific confidence. Sample items include “AI technologies/ products are good aids to learning” (assistance), “I think the interactive process of AI technologies/products is very vivid, just like chatting with a real person” (anthropomorphic interaction), “When interacting with AI technologies/products, I feel very relaxed” (comfort with AI), and “When using AI technologies/products, I am not worried that I might press the wrong button and cause risks” (technological skills). Items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating stronger perceived capability to use AI effectively. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was .92.

Academic procrastination Academic procrastination was measured using the Academic Procrastination Scale-Short Form (APS-S) validated by Yockey (2016). This five-item scale was developed as a concise unidimensional measure of students’ tendency to delay academic tasks. It was selected for the present study because it directly captures procrastinatory behaviour in academic contexts while reducing respondent burden. This measure was appropriate because the present study treated academic procrastination as a behavioural indicator of impaired academic self-regulation. Sample items include “I put off projects until the last minute”, “I know I should work on schoolwork, but I just don’t do it”, and “I frequently find myself putting important deadlines off”. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating greater academic procrastination. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was .84.

AI dependence AI dependence was measured using the AI Dependence Scale for Chinese Undergraduates (AIDep-22) developed and validated with Chinese undergraduates by Wu et al. (2026). This 22-item scale was designed to assess maladaptive reliance on AI in academic contexts across four dimensions: emotional dependence, functional dependence, cognitive dependence, and loss of control. Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in SPSS 27.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participants’ demographic characteristics. Pearson correlations were calculated to examine associations among AI literacy, AI self-efficacy, academic procrastination and AI dependence. Scale scores were computed by averaging item responses so that all focal variables retained the original response metric. Following the source scoring procedures, global scores were used because the hypotheses concerned relations among the four overall constructs. For the mediation analysis, variables were standardised before regression paths were estimated. The PROCESS macro for SPSS, Model 6, was used to test the hypothesised serial mediation among observed composite variables. Although this approach provides a parsimonious regression-based bootstrapping method for estimating indirect effects, it does not account for measurement error (Hayes, 2022). Indirect effects were estimated using 5,000 bootstrap samples, and a 95% bootstrap Confidence Interval (CI) that did not include zero was interpreted as statistically significant. Gender, grade, major category and weekly AI use frequency were controlled in the models. A p value below .05 was considered statistically significant.

Several diagnostic and supplementary analyses were conducted. Common Method Bias (CMB) was addressed procedurally through anonymous responses and statistically through Harman’s single-factor test and an Unmeasured Latent Common Method (ULCM) factor model (Podsakoff et al., 2003, 2012). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to evaluate the measurement model and compare the hypothesised four-factor structure with alternative models. The CFA used 17 indicators comprising four AILS dimension means, four AISES dimension means, five APS-S items and four AIDep-22 dimension means. Using dimension means for the multidimensional measures preserved each validated domain, while retaining all five APS-S items preserved its unidimensional structure and kept the measurement model parsimonious for N = 417. Multicollinearity was examined using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values, with acceptable values interpreted according to conventional criteria (Hair et al., 2019). Given the crosssectional design, a fully reversed serial mediation model and two alternative serial mediation models were also examined as sensitivity checks with the same covariates and 5,000 bootstrap samples.

The mediation model was interpreted using both path coefficients and bootstrapped indirect effects. The regression table reports standardised coefficients to facilitate comparison across paths. The indirect-effect table reports completely standardised indirect effects and relative mediation effects. This approach follows the logic of serial mediation reporting, in which the direct path, the two single-mediator paths and the sequential path are examined together. The significance of the total and direct effects was also reviewed to determine whether the mediation was partial or complete.

Correlations

Pearson correlations were calculated to examine associations among the study variables. As shown in Table 4, AI literacy was positively correlated with AI self-efficacy (r = .542, p < .001) and negatively correlated with academic procrastination (r = −.309, p < .001) and AI dependence (r = −.283, p < .001). AI self-efficacy was negatively correlated with academic procrastination (r = −.420, p < .001) and AI dependence (r = −.361, p < .001). Academic procrastination was positively correlated with AI dependence (r = .475, p < .001).

The correlation pattern was consistent with the proposed model. The positive correlation between AI literacy and AI self-efficacy indicated that students with higher AI literacy tended to report stronger confidence in using AI. The negative correlations of AI literacy with academic procrastination and AI dependence suggested that literacy was related to healthier learning behaviour and lower maladaptive reliance. The negative correlation between AI self-efficacy and academic procrastination supported the expected cognitive-to-behavioural link. The positive correlation between academic procrastination and AI dependence indicated that students who delayed academic tasks were more likely to report AI dependence.

Testing the mediation effect

The significant correlations among the focal variables provided preliminary support for testing the hypothesised mediation model. After controlling for gender, grade, major category and weekly AI use frequency, PROCESS Model 6 was used to analyse the serial mediating effects of AI self-efficacy and academic procrastination in the association between AI literacy and AI dependence.

The regression results are shown in Table 5. AI literacy was significantly and positively associated with AI self-efficacy (β = .510, p < .001). AI literacy (β = −.217, p < .001) and AI self-efficacy (β = −.391, p < .001) were both significantly and negatively associated with academic procrastination. In the final regression equation, AI literacy (β = −.181, p < .001) and AI self-efficacy (β = −.230, p < .001) were negatively associated with AI dependence, whereas academic procrastination was positively associated with AI dependence (β = .270, p < .001). The path model is shown in Figure 2.

The regression equations showed moderate explanatory power. The first equation explained 30.4% of the variance in AI self-efficacy. The second equation explained 29.1% of the variance in academic procrastination. The final equation explained 33.5% of the variance in AI dependence. These R2 values suggest that the focal predictors and covariates accounted for meaningful variance in the proposed mediators and outcome. The directions and effect sizes of all focal paths were consistent with the hypothesised theoretical sequence.

The pattern of direct and indirect effects therefore supported a partial serial mediation model. AI literacy retained a significant direct association with AI dependence after AI self-efficacy and academic procrastination were included. The two mediators explained a substantial part of the association while leaving room for additional mechanisms, such as critical thinking, ethical awareness and metacognitive monitoring.

To further examine sensitivity to model ordering, reversed and alternative serial mediation models were tested with the same covariates and 5,000 bootstrap samples. All three competing models also produced significant indirect effects. The procrastination-first model, from AI literacy to AI dependence through academic procrastination and AI self-efficacy, yielded an effect of −.0319, Boot SE = .0092, 95% CI [−.0520, −.0159]. The fully reversed model, from AI dependence to AI literacy through academic procrastination and AI self-efficacy, yielded an effect of −.0536, Boot SE = .0125, 95% CI [−.0815, −.0324]. The reversed-endpoint model, from AI dependence to AI literacy through AI self-efficacy and academic procrastination, yielded an effect of −.0227, Boot SE = .0086, 95% CI [−.0405, −.0070]. These findings show that the cross-sectional data are compatible with more than one ordering. The hypothesised sequence retained theoretical priority because it followed SCT and the I-PACE model, but the significant competing models preclude a unique temporal interpretation.

This study examined psychological mechanisms linking AI literacy to AI dependence among Chinese undergraduates. AI literacy was negatively associated with AI dependence, and this association was explained by three significant indirect pathways. These pathways involved AI self-efficacy, academic procrastination and the sequential pathway from AI self-efficacy to academic procrastination. By integrating cognitive and behavioural mechanisms into one framework, the study provides a more detailed account of how AI literacy may protect students from maladaptive dependence on AI tools. The results also indicate that AI dependence is not simply frequent technology use. It is better understood as a pattern of learning behaviour that reflects how students understand AI, how confident they feel in managing it and how effectively they regulate academic tasks.

The negative association between AI literacy and AI dependence suggests that AI literacy may function as a protective academic resource. Students with higher AI literacy may be better able to understand the affordances and limitations of AI systems, evaluate AI-generated information and maintain appropriate boundaries between assistance and substitution. This interpretation is consistent with research arguing that AI literacy should include critical, ethical and evaluative capabilities, rather than only technical skill (Ng et al., 2021; Park, 2025; B. Wang et al., 2023). It also extends debates on generative AI in higher education, which increasingly emphasise that students need to work with AI without surrendering judgement to it (Chan & Hu, 2023; Kasneci et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024). Recent evidence also links AI literacy with generative AI use intentions (C. Wang et al., 2025). In practical terms, the goal of AI literacy education should not be to reduce all AI use. It should help students use AI reflectively, responsibly and in ways that preserve autonomous learning.

The persistence of the direct effect after AI self-efficacy and academic procrastination were included suggests that AI literacy may also operate through other mechanisms. For example, AI literacy includes ethical awareness and evaluation of AI outputs, both of which may directly reduce uncritical dependence. Students who understand that AI systems can produce plausible but inaccurate responses may be more likely to verify information and less likely to accept outputs passively. Students who recognise the ethical boundaries of AI use may also be more likely to distinguish support from academic substitution. These processes are not fully captured by AI self-efficacy or procrastination. This interpretation is consistent with work emphasising evaluative and ethical dimensions of AIL and the need for critical engagement with generative AI outputs (Ng et al., 2021; B. Wang et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024).

The mediating role of AI self-efficacy indicates that students with higher AI literacy may develop stronger confidence in managing AI-supported academic tasks, which is associated with lower AI dependence. This result aligns with SCT, which holds that self-efficacy shapes effort, persistence and adaptive coping in challenging tasks (Bandura, 1997; Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2020). It also extends research showing that academic self-efficacy is associated with achievement and self-regulated learning (Honicke & Broadbent, 2016; Richardson et al., 2012). Recent AI-specific evidence also connects AI literacy with self-efficacy and efficacy beliefs with academic AI use (Bećirović et al., 2025; Schutte & Li, 2025). The present finding extends this evidence by linking broad AI self-efficacy to lower maladaptive dependence within an indirect pathway. In the AI context, efficacy beliefs are important because students must decide when to use AI, how to formulate prompts, how to interpret outputs and how to correct errors. Students with low AI self-efficacy may depend on AI passively because they feel uncertain about how to evaluate or control the tool. Students with higher AI self-efficacy may use AI more actively and critically. Broad AI self-efficacy therefore appears to be a central cognitive bridge between literacy and healthier AI engagement.

Academic procrastination also mediated the association between AI literacy and AI dependence. This finding suggests that AI dependence is partly related to academic self-regulation. Procrastination has long been understood as a self-regulatory failure involving delay, avoidance and short-term mood repair (Sirois & Pychyl, 2013; Steel, 2007). In the present model, students with lower AI literacy may be less able to plan and regulate AI use, which may contribute to procrastination and task avoidance. As deadlines approach, these students may turn to AI as a rapid compensatory tool for delayed academic work. This pattern can gradually normalise reliance on AI as a substitute for earlier planning and independent effort. The finding is consistent with evidence that procrastination is associated with weaker academic performance and study habits (Credé & Kuncel, 2008; Kim & Seo, 2015). The significant sequential mediation pathway was consistent with the hypothesised ordering of further indicates that cognitive and behavioural mechanisms operate in an ordered process. Higher AI literacy was associated with stronger AI self-efficacy, which was associated with lower academic procrastination. Lower procrastination was then associated with lower AI dependence. This pathway is theoretically meaningful because it links competence, belief, self-regulation and dependence in one process. SCT helps explain how literacy can shape efficacy beliefs. The I-PACE model helps explain how cognitive beliefs and executivecontrol difficulties may contribute to maladaptive technology-related outcomes (Brand et al., 2019). In this framework, procrastination functions as a behavioural indicator of weakened academic control, and AI dependence reflects a downstream pattern of maladaptive reliance. The chain mechanism suggests that students may become dependent on AI not only because AI is convenient. Dependence may also reflect limited confidence and self-regulatory habits needed to use AI as a controlled learning support. The significant competing models point to plausible reciprocal processes. Repeated delay may limit mastery experiences and weaken confidence, while AI dependence may also reduce perceived control. Although the proposed sequence remains theoretically grounded in SCT and the I-PACE model, the cross-sectional data cannot establish a unique temporal order. Longitudinal studies should therefore compare the proposed and reciprocal paths.

Another implication concerns AI use frequency. Weekly AI use frequency was controlled in the mediation model because frequent use and dependence are not equivalent. A student may use AI often for planning, feedback or revision while maintaining strong judgement and self-regulation (Schutte & Li, 2025). Another student may use AI less frequently but rely on it heavily when facing pressure. By controlling use frequency, the present model focused on the psychological pattern of dependence rather than the quantity of use. This distinction is consistent with research on problematic technology-related behaviour, which distinguishes frequent engagement from maladaptive or poorly regulated use (Kuss & Griffiths, 2017; Zhang et al., 2024).

Implications

Theoretically, this study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, it extends AI literacy from a competence-oriented construct to a potential protective resource against maladaptive AI dependence. Previous research has largely emphasised AI literacy as a capacity needed for participation in AI-rich learning environments (Mustafa et al., 2024; S. Wang et al., 2024). The present findings suggest that AI literacy may also be protective when AI tools create risks of over-reliance. Second, the study identifies AI self-efficacy as a cognitive mechanism through which AI literacy may translate into healthier AI engagement. This extends SCT to the emerging domain of generative AI use in higher education. Third, the study incorporates academic procrastination into the explanation of AI dependence. This contribution is important because it frames AI dependence as an academic self-regulation issue, rather than only a technology-use issue.

The findings also have practical implications. Universities should design AI literacy education that goes beyond tool operation. Training should address how AI systems generate responses, where errors may occur, how to evaluate outputs, how to cite or acknowledge AI assistance and how to maintain boundaries between support and substitution. Curriculum designers may embed AI literacy into academic writing, information literacy, research methods and general learning-skills education, rather than treating it as an isolated technical topic. Students can be asked to compare AI-generated explanations with scholarly sources, identify errors, revise prompts and explain why they accepted or rejected particular outputs. Such tasks can strengthen both AI literacy and reflective judgement.

Students with low AI self-efficacy may also need guided practice. Educators can provide scaffolded activities in which students learn to create prompts, check sources, compare outputs and revise their own work. These activities may reduce passive reliance by helping students experience AI as a controllable tool, rather than as an authority to follow. Because academic procrastination was positively associated with AI dependence, AI guidance should also be integrated with time-management and self-regulation support.

These findings may also inform student support services. Academic advisers, writing centres and learning development units can integrate AI-use guidance into existing support for study planning and academic integrity. For example, students can be encouraged to document how they used AI at different stages of a task, identify which parts of the work required their own judgement and reflect on whether AI supported or replaced their learning. Such practices can make AI use visible and discussable. They may also help students recognise when procrastination is pushing them towards unreflective dependence.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal and temporal inferences. Although the hypothesised model was theoretically grounded and reverse and alternative models were tested, longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to clarify the temporal dynamics among AI literacy, AI self-efficacy, academic procrastination and AI dependence. Second, all variables were measured using self-report questionnaires, which may introduce recall bias and shared method variance. Harman’s single-factor test and the ULCM comparison suggested that CMB was unlikely to dominate the findings. Future research should nevertheless include behavioural indicators of AI use, learning analytics or performance-based tasks and use latent-variable SEM to account for measurement error in the observed composites. Because the alternative models also yielded significant indirect effects, the proposed sequence should be interpreted as theoretically preferred rather than as definitive evidence of temporal ordering.

Future research could use longitudinal designs to test whether AI literacy predicts later changes in AI selfefficacy, procrastination and dependence. Experience sampling methods may also be useful because AI use often occurs in specific task contexts, such as writing, programming, translation or revision. Such designs could clarify whether dependence increases during periods of deadline pressure and whether self-efficacy protects students during those periods.

Third, the sample was drawn from one normal university in Southwest China and was approximately 70% female, reflecting its teacher-education orientation. Stratified sampling was used to reflect the institution’s gender and major-category structure, but future studies should examine whether the findings generalise to other types of universities, more gender-balanced populations, disciplines and cultural contexts. Because the AIDep-22 was developed for Chinese undergraduates, cross-cultural use requires adaptation and measurement-invariance testing. Fourth, the present study examined global construct-level relationships. Future research could examine dimension-specific mechanisms, compare the broad AISES total score with taskspecific capability measures, and test whether AI ethics, evaluation, technological skills, emotional dependence, cognitive dependence and loss of control play different roles. Finally, although the robustness check showed that dummy coding of major category did not change the focal findings, future research with larger samples could test demographic differences and moderated mediation more directly.

Conclusion

This study indicates that AI self-efficacy and academic procrastination sequentially mediate the association between AI literacy and AI dependence among Chinese undergraduates. AI literacy was associated with lower AI dependence directly and indirectly through stronger AI self-efficacy and lower academic procrastination. These findings suggest that reducing maladaptive AI dependence requires more than limiting AI use. Universities should help students develop AI literacy, strengthen students’ confidence in managing AI- supported learning and improve academic self-regulation so that AI functions as a learning support rather than a substitute for autonomous academic engagement.
