Creative self-efficacy has increasingly gained traction in the field of creativity research and has been described as a key component of creative outcomes. However, most studies have used short scales or author-developed instruments consisting of three items to measure the construct of creative self-efficacy. The purpose of this study was to respond to the call for additional research in this area and the development of a longer, reliable scale to measure creative self-efficacy. The authors of this study present findings that support the validity and reliability of the creative selfefficacy scale developed and examined in this study. Exploratory and confirmatory analyses were conducted and supported a single factor structure of creative self-efficacy. A set of 20 items that all had pattern coefficients greater than 0.61 with the single factor were retained to create the final version of the creative self-efficacy scale. Implications and future areas of research are also discussed.

Creativity is one of the top five skills—only second to analytical thinking—that organizations are prioritizing in both hiring and training initiatives (World Economic Forum, 2023), and the same is true for students. Creativity has been increasingly emphasized as an essential 21st century skill that moves students beyond basic literacy and mathematics (Programme for International Student Assessment [PISA], 2021). Globally, employment in creative sectors has increased by at least 20 million jobs within a five-year period (Florida, 2006, 2011), and that number has since grown. In the United States (2011 – 2013), the creative economy provided over 14 million jobs, almost 10 % of all employment in the country (National Endowment for Science, Technology & the Arts [Nesta], 2016). Moreover, data from 2002 – 2015 presented the strength of the creative economy, even during periods of economic recession, showing the creative economy doubling its initial trade value of $208 billion in the global market (United Nations Conference on Trade & Development [UNCTAD], 2018). Creativity is defined as, “the interaction among aptitude, process, and environment by which an in­ dividual or group produces a perceptible product that is both novel and useful as defined within a social context” (Plucker et al., 2004, p. 90). In response to global demands for creative thinkers, leaders in business, education, and policy have worked to realign classroom practice to real world necessity—critical thinking, creativity, collaboration, and communication skills (Partnership for 21st Century Learning (P21), 2019). Creative self-efficacy (CSE) is defined as an individual’s belief that they can produce creative outcomes (Tierney & Farmer, 2002). CSE is a type of creative self-belief—an individual’s convictions related to their creative abilities—which reveals why, how, and what an individual creates; these beliefs also reveal why an individual chooses not to create. Simply stated, CSE describes an individual’s belief about their creative ability (Haase et al., 2018). CSE along with other creative self-beliefs (e.g., creative metacognition, creative self-concept) mold an individual’s creative identity (Beghetto & Karwowski, 2017). These beliefs play a pivotal role in how individuals engage creatively and assess their own ability to be creative.

Interest in the construct of CSE has rapidly increased (Beghetto, 2020), and most studies have supported positive relationships between CSE and creative performance/production (Haase et al., 2018; Tierney & Farmer, 2011). For instance, CSE has been found to be closely associated with creative task engagement (Karwowski, 2012; Tierney & Farmer, 2002) and labeled by some scholars as a “key determinant of creative outcomes” (Hass et al., 2016, p. 438) in both educational and workplace settings. Due to its connection to desired creative outcomes, CSE is a construct worthy of attention.

1.1. Creative self-efficacy: the why and the will to create

According to Bandura (1977, 2006), people are guided by their self-efficacy beliefs, or their perceived capabilities to successfully perform given tasks or “execute the behavior required to produce the outcome” (Bandura, 1977, p. 193). Exerting this type of assessment of one’s beliefs and control leads the individual to develop agency which Bandura described as the intentional action an individual pursues. Therefore, an individual’s perceived self-efficacy beliefs are critical determinants of agency. CSE can contribute to the understanding of why individuals pursue, or avoid, some creative tasks over others. Indeed, multiple studies have supported the key role CSE plays in the creative process across multiple settings (Beghetto, 2006; Karwowski, 2011, 2012; Karwowski & Lebuda, 2016; Tierney & Farmer, 2002). CSE can change based on an individual’s environment and experiences (Bandura, 2006). Results from Tierney and Farmer (2002) indicated that although elevated levels of CSE predicted employees’ likelihood to be creative in their work, this process was unique to the setting. They found that feedback from supervisors operated as a mediator in the relationship between CSE and creative perfor­ mance. These findings were later generalized to student populations (Beghetto, 2006), showing that the nature in which feedback was perceived, supportive or critical, affected individuals’ CSE, and thus, creative outcomes. Other scholars have found similar results: supportive work and learning environments can bolster levels of CSE (Chong & Ma, 2010; Farmer & Tierney, 2017; Jaussi et al., 2007; Karwowski, 2011).

CSE not only predicts an individual’s willingness to engage creatively, but it also sustains an individual, even when challenges and setbacks are experienced during creative tasks (Farmer & Tierney, 2017; Ivcevic & Nusbaum, 2017). CSE beliefs enhance an in­ dividual’s persistence and facilitate the necessary coping skills when faced with challenges (Tierney & Farmer, 2002). Essentially, an individual’s CSE can be used to predict both engagement and follow-through with creative performance or production.

However, findings from a meta-analysis suggested that the relationship between CSE and creative outcomes is more nuanced. Although Haase et al. (2018) found a medium effect size (r = 0.39) between creativity and CSE, they also found inconsistencies be­ tween studies included in their meta-analysis. Haase and colleagues did not negate the relationship between CSE and creative out­ comes, but their study demonstrated variance between CSE and different types of creativity measurements. For instance, CSE demonstrated stronger relationships with other self-reported creativity constructs than with creative performance measures such as divergent thinking tasks. An additional recommendation from the Haase et al. (2018) meta-analysis was to move toward consistent use of one or a few scales in order to accurately compare results across samples and contexts. However, researchers have also expressed the need for an extended measure of CSE (Beghetto, 2006; Karwowski, 2011).

The overarching aim of the present study was to build upon prior research and develop a psychometrically improved instrument to measure individual CSE. Given the role CSE plays in determining creative outcomes, the importance of measuring the construct accurately is three-fold: (a) further our understanding of the creative process, (b) expand our understanding of creative task engagement and avoidance, and (c) identify those who may benefit from targeted interventions to increase their CSE.

1.2. Limitations of current creative self-efficacy assessments

As it stands, measuring CSE has limitations. Current measures either lack alignment with the definition of CSE or are limited by the prevalent use of only three items to measure CSE. The first is a limitation of content validity, such that the instrument may not capture the construct of CSE. The second is a limitation in the ability to assess the construct validity of the instrument with use of confirmatory factor analysis (CFA; DiStefano & Hess, 2005).

According to Karwowski (2011), an important distinction of CSE is an individual’s belief that the person can be creative rather than that the person is creative. Though a person’s creativity and CSE are related constructs, they are not identical. In the three-item scale used by Karwowski to measure CSE, the items are (a) “I think I am creative,” (b) “I would describe myself as a talented person,” and (c) “I am gifted enough to manage problems” (p. 157). Interestingly, the first item is a self-evaluation of an individual’s belief that they are creative and does not align with the distinction Karwowski made, whereas the second item is related to an individual’s self-perceived talent. Later, Karwowski et al. (2018) developed the SSCS (Short Scale for Creative Self) in which CSE is one component of the creative self and measured using six out of 11 total items: (a) “I know I can efficiently solve even complicated problems;” (b) “I trust my creative abilities;” (c) “Compared to my friends, I am distinguished by my imagination and ingenuity;” (d) “Many times I have proven that I can cope with difficult situations;” (e) “I am sure I can deal with problems requiring creative thinking;” and (f) “I am good at proposing original solutions to problems” (Karwowski, 2012, p. 550). Although the six-item CSE scale has demonstrated validity support (Karwowski et al., 2018; Shaw et al., 2021), some concerns related to content validity and the relatively low number of items used to measure the construct remain. For instance, one might argue that the fourth item (i.e., “Many times I have proven that I can cope with difficult situations”) may be closer to the construct of grit. In the same study, Karwowski (2012) also noted limitations associated with the use of short scales. Almost ten years later, Shaw et al. (2021) examined Karwowski’s CSE scale using item response theory analysis and demonstrated validity of the CSE scale; however, Shaw et al. also noted the small sample size used in the study as a limitation that “makes all interpretations of the results subject to suspicion” (p. 5).

Other studies have used three items to measure CSE (e.g., Beghetto, 2006; Karwowski, 2011; Tierney & Farmer, 2002). The latent factor structure of an instrument, that is the number of underlying traits that are being identified and quantified by the scores resulting from measurement, cannot be assessed using CFA, because a measurement model with only three items is just-identified (Kline, 2016). Typical fit indices (e.g., root-mean square error of approximation [RMSEA]) used to evaluate a model in CFA cannot be calculated when a measurement model is just-identified, limiting an investigator’s ability to verify that all three items are indeed indicators of the same latent construct. Further, use of an instrument in a full structural equation model (SEM) is typically predicated with the assessment of the measurement model for each instrument, requiring use of CFA (Kline, 2016). Without the ability to conduct a CFA, a three-item instrument has limited utility in identifying a latent construct in a SEM unless other constructs are evaluated as well. In­ struments developed by Tierney and Farmer (2002) and Beghetto (2006) each contain only three items. Tierney and Farmer (2002) measured CSE with the following three questions: (a) I have confidence in my ability to solve problems creatively, (b) I have a knack for further developing the ideas of others, and (c) I feel that I am good at generating novel ideas. This particular instrument was developed with adult employees in manufacturing and business operations fields. Beghetto’s (2006) instrument was used with students and consisted of the following three items: “(a) I am good at coming up with new ideas, (b) I have a lot of good ideas, and (c) I have a good imagination” (p. 450). Both studies used a Likert-type scale.

1.3. Key considerations in the development of creative self-efficacy assessments

Bandura provided important considerations and directions for self-efficacy instrument construction that can be applied in any domain. To develop items for the extended CSE scale in this study, we followed the guidelines established by Bandura (2006, 2012) and existing research in creative self-beliefs (Beghetto & Karwowski, 2017). According to Bandura (2006, 2012), self-efficacy items should be relevant to the domain, or phenomenon of interest—in this case, creativity—and should consider operational areas such as situ­ ational contexts, related tasks, or behaviors. Moreover, self-efficacy instruments must “assess the multifaceted ways in which efficacy beliefs operate” within the relevant domain (Bandura, 2006, p. 310). In other words, the instrument should capture various activities and conditions relevant to the type of self-efficacy being measured.

Items should also be written in a way that “elicit a person’s confidence in performing specific features of a task” (Beghetto & Karwowski, 2017, p. 10). In the case of creativity, CSE items should be connected to related activities or tasks (e.g., creative ideation, problem-solving, idea presentation, creative performance). In this scenario, participants’ responses to CSE items may vary based on their perceived ability to generate ideas versus their perceived ability to perform creatively. Moreover, self-efficacy beliefs can change based on an individual’s environmental context and prior experiences (Bandura, 2006; Beghetto, 2020); therefore, items should be developed in a way that not only captures activities associated with the domain, but items should represent varying degrees of dif­ ficulty or obstacles that may interfere with successful performance (Bandura, 2006). Situational contexts may indicate past experiences of failure or criticism which may also be conveyed by indicating different social contexts within the items. For example, individuals may consider themselves to have high CSE when evaluating their CSE based on their ability to generate ideas or independently work creatively but may have decreased CSE in situations where the individual is asked to share their creative ideas with others. In another scenario, an individual may have high CSE related to creative problem-solving but low CSE when asked to present those innovative solutions to a supervisor or an expert in the field, especially if the individual has experienced negative feedback in the past (Beghetto, 2006; Tierney & Famer, 2002). Items must be written in a way in that impose constraints and perceived impediments for participants. CSE will vary for individuals according to contextual factors such as environment, domain, task, and audience. Whether creativity is a domain-specific or domain-general construct has been debated (Baer, 1998, 2016; Baer & Kaufman, 2005; Plucker & Beghetto, 2004), and self-report measures that consider perceptions of creativity across different domains, such as the Kaufman Domains of Creativity Scale (K-DOCS; Kaufman, 2012), have been developed. Evidence supporting creativity as domain-general, domain-specific, or both exists (An & Runco, 2016; Qian et al., 2019; Silvia et al., 2009). In terms of CSE, CSE has often been measured as a general construct (Karwowski et al., 2018; Lebuda et al., 2021), and evidence has supported the validity of domain-general creative self-beliefs, including CSE (Karwowski & Lebuda, 2016). In fact, one might argue that CSE represents what Bandura (2006) called a “domain of functioning” (p. 307).

Self-efficacy assessments should also be a measure of what a person can do rather than what a person will do. “I will” statements can consequently inflate individuals’ perceived self-efficacy (Bandura, 2006). In the example provided by Bandura, self-efficacy in­ struments should include explicit directions that instruct participants to respond to items based on how confident they are in per­ forming a task now, not as they wish to be.

Given these limitations, researchers have called for clarification in how CSE is conceptualized and measured (Beghetto & Kar­ wowski, 2017) and the development of an extended measure of CSE (Beghetto, 2006; Karwowski, 2011). To accomplish this task, two studies were conducted. In the first study, a potential item pool was created, and face validity assessed. In the second study, the item pool was reduced, and the factor structure established and validated.

2.1. Study 1: item pool development

The purpose of the first study was to develop a set of items that measure domain general CSE. As previously stated, we followed Bandura’s guidelines (2006) and existing research in creative self-beliefs (Beghetto & Karwowski, 2017) to develop CSE items. This was done in three steps: (a) items were created according to guidelines for self-efficacy assessment and operational areas relative to creativity as supported in empirical research only (Bandura, 2012), (b) preliminary items were evaluated by scholars with relevant expertise in measurement and creativity, and (c) revised items were piloted to post-secondary students for initial validation and feedback. In this way, a refined pool of items was established from which a final instrument could be constructed.

2.1.1. Method

2.1.1.1. Participants. The initial set of items (26 items) were developed and then reviewed by scholars with expertise in measurement and creativity. Feedback was incorporated and the revised set of items was then peer reviewed a second time by additional academics and practitioners with expertise in educational psychology, creativity, and measurement.

2.1.1.2. Procedure for item development. Items were created based on previous research (e.g., Beghetto, 2006; Karwowski, 2011; Tierney & Farmer, 2002) and Bandura’s guidelines (2006). Bandura’s guidelines were followed based on his notable contributions in the areas of social cognitive theory and self-efficacy. In measuring self-efficacy beliefs, Bandura (2006) cautioned that there is “no all-purpose measure of perceived self-efficacy” but that a self-efficacy scale should be “tailored to the particular domain of functioning that is the object of interest” (pp. 307–308). In developing CSE items, the following guidelines for measuring self-efficacy were fol­ lowed: (a) items should be adapted to the particular construct of interest; (b) items should be phrased in terms of what the individual can do as opposed to will do; and (c) items should include varied levels of difficulty; in this respect, some CSE items were added that went beyond individual ideation or problem-solving and included some type of peer evaluation or performance. Because this is an instrument development study, items were created to target multiple tasks associated with creativity such as ideation, problem-solving, and task-engagement.

2.2. Study 2: instrument refinement and factorial validity

After item development and review, two steps were followed in reducing the total number of items and verifying the instrument’s factor structure. The first step was to iteratively conduct a series of exploratory factor analyses (EFA) to refine the instrument to fewer items with the greatest psychometric alignment to a single construct. In the second step, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was utilized to verify the results. A random split of a single sample of responses was made with one set (n = 865) used for the EFA and the other set (n = 866) used for the CFA. Splitting the sample in this way allowed for a two-step verification of the instrument’s psy­ chometric properties. Further, items from previous measures of CSE (Tierney & Farmer, 2002) and personality (Big Five) were collected to evaluate the proposed instrument for convergent validity. All analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2019) using the psych package (Revelle, 2019) and the lavaan (Rosseel, 2012) package.

2.2.1. Method

2.2.1.3. Participants. Participants were students at a large public university in the southwestern region of the United States. There were 1731 participants (66 % females and 34 % males), aged from 18 to 49 years (M = 25 years, SD = 0.18). Participants in the sample identified along racial/ethnic lines as 57.2 % White, 17.2 % Latinx, 9.1 % Asian, 9 % two or more races, 6.6 % Black, < 1 % American Indian or Alaskan Native, and < 1 % Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander. A wide range of majors were represented as well as a mix of both undergraduate and graduate students. Participants represented multiple academic units at the university, including the College of Business (9.5 %), College of Education (13.4 %), College of Engineering (8.8 %), School of Journalism (2.4 %), College of Health and Public Services (8 %), College of Information (6.2 %), College of Liberal Arts and Social Sciences (27.5 %), College of Merchandising, Hospitality, and Tourism (2 %), College of Music (4.3 %), College of Science (12.4 %), and College of Visual Arts and Design (5.3 %). Participant consent and responses to the 26 author-developed items in addition to items from previously used measures of CSE were collected through Qualtrics, an online survey tool.

2.2.1.4. EFA data analysis. A parallel analysis was conducted to determine the number of factors that may be represented in the data (Horn, 1965). This procedure compares eigenvalues extracted from the sample data to eigenvalues extracted from simulated data for which no underlying factor is present. Therefore, eigenvalues extracted from the real data that exceed the eigenvalues extracted from the simulated data indicate the possible existence of a true factor. Additionally, the eigenvalue-greater-than-one criterion (Kaiser, 1960) has been commonly utilized in applications of factor analysis for determining the number of reliable factors to retain (Henson & Roberts, 2006).

Following the parallel analysis, a series of EFAs were conducted using principal axis factoring. For testing multiple-factor solutions, Promax rotation was used allowing factors to be correlated with each other. Given item responses were Likert-type-scaled, factors were based on the polychoric correlations between items rather than Pearson’s r correlations (Sellbom & Tellegen, 2019). Item pattern coefficients were evaluated based on Comrey and Lee’s (1992) recommendation that 0.32 is poor, 0.45 is fair, 0.55 is good, 0.63 is very good, and 0.70 is excellent. Further, the variance in the items explained was considered when determining a final solution. Coefficient alpha and omega (McNeish, 2018) were calculated to evaluate reliability, where values around 0.9 are considered “excellent”, 0.8 considered “very good”, and 0.7 considered “adequate” (Kline, 2016).

2.2.1.5. CFA data analysis. A CFA model was fit based on the results of the EFA with no error correlations. Given the Likert-type-scale of the items, the diagonally weighted least squares estimator was used (DiStefano & Morgan, 2014). Model fit indices were evaluated according to Hu and Bentler’s (1999) recommendations, where comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) values greater than 0.95 are considered to demonstrate good incremental model fit and root mean square error of <0.08 and standardized root mean-square residual (SRMR) of <0.05 indicate good absolute model fit. Coefficient alpha and omega were utilized to assess the reliability of scores for the instrument.

The validity of the CSE scale was further assessed by evaluating the correlations between the instrument’s latent scores and another instrument measuring CSE. Specifically, we correlated in a CFA the latent CSE factor based on the proposed instrument and CSE scores derived from Tierney and Farmer’s (2002) three item CSE instrument (referred to hereafter as TFCSE). Scores for the TFCSE were found by taking the average of an individual’s responses to the three items. The Big Five personality factors (i.e., neuroticism, extraversion, openness to experience, agreeableness, and conscientiousness) were also considered as they have been correlated with CSE. Though the parallel analysis indicated that up to six factors had eigenvalues greater than the average of the simulated eigenvalues, only two were greater than 1. EFAs were conducted iteratively. Initially, a two-factor solution was fit and evaluated. Item relationships with factors were assessed based on the standardized pattern coefficient matrix. The second factor had few items that could be considered uniquely associated with it whereas all remaining items either strongly indicated the first factor or were split between the two factors. The correlation between the two factors was 0.73 indicating a strong similarity between the two.

A single factor solution was evaluated next and all, but five items had a pattern coefficient greater than 0.63 (considered “very good” using Comrey and Lee’s characterization). Four of the five items were the reverse coded items; therefore, these were removed because they yielded higher correlations with one another likely due to the reversed nature of the items rather than the construct. The single factor solution was then re-evaluated. All items on the reduced set of items had pattern coefficients greater than 0.62 with the single factor. The single factor solution with 20 items accounted for 51 % of the variability in the items.

4. Discussion

This study was designed and conducted in response to the growing demand for creative behaviors and researchers’ call (e.g., Beghetto, 2006; Karwowski, 2011) for additional studies on CSE and the development of an extended CSE instrument with improved measurement validity. To guide this endeavor, there were two primary goals. The first goal was to develop an instrument that appropriately captured the construct of CSE, or an individual’s belief that they can produce creative outcomes (Tierney & Farmer, 2002) and aligned with a widely accepted definition of creativity (Plucker et al., 2004). The second was to improve the psychometric properties of a new instrument beyond what is possible with a three-item measure (Kline, 2016).

Overall, the results of this study were promising and an important step forward in the development of a CSE scale that can also be used with statistical analyses, such as SEM and CFA. Validity and reliability were also supported for the CSE instrument developed in this study. The CSE scale developed in this study strongly supported convergent validity when correlated with the three CSE items used by Tierney and Farmer (2002). Additionally, the CSE scale correlated to personality traits of openness to experience, agreeableness, conscientiousness, and extraversion which aligned with other studies on CSE and personality (Fino & Sun, 2022; Tan et al., 2013).

Reliability estimates are based on scores obtained from a measure and generally vary from sample to sample (Henson, 2001; Vacha-Haase et al., 2002). In other words, tests themselves are not reliable, but rather the scores obtained from the samples using those tests yield reliability estimates. Previous research consistently indicates that the number of items on an instrument is correlated positively with reliability estimates (Cortina, 1993; Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). In a reliability generalization study, Hellman et al. (2006) found the correlation between item numbers and reliability estimates to be r = 0.60, p < .001. Inductively, we might presume that the ability to measure CSE with 20 items rather than three items should generally improve the reliability of those scores.

Item development closely followed Bandura’s (2006, 2012) theory and guidelines as well as prior research on CSE (Beghetto & Karwowski, 2017). For instance, some retained CSE items in this scale included key aspects of CSE such as varied levels of difficulty or potential evaluation. Evaluation expectation can impact creativity (Amabile et al., 2009) in a positive or negative way based on factors such as style of feedback (Darfler & Kalantari, 2022) which can also affect CSE (Beghetto, 2006; Hu et al., 2018; Tierney & Farmer, 2002). Using a scale of 20 items that presents varied levels of difficulty and evaluation is a promising step forward in capturing a more nuanced view of CSE under diverse conditions and contexts and as CSE likely operates in real-life.

The significance of this study was to improve the measurement capacity of subsequent research relative to the theories of creative self-beliefs and creative cognition and production. Previous work using shortand three-item measurement scales have yielded sig­ nificant and interesting correlates of CSE. The research team conducting this study sought to build upon prior work in creativity and CSE and improve the psychometric properties of continued research in this area. Although the results of this study were positive, there are limitations and important next steps to consider in the continued validation and refinement of this extended CSE scale.

In the future, a broader response range (0 – 100) should accompany CSE items (Bandura, 2006) which can provide predictions that are more sensitive, reliable, and accurate (Beghetto & Karwowski, 2017).

This study also did not include a measure of creative outcomes or performance. CSE is an important correlate of creative outcomes (Haase et al., 2018; Hass et al., 2016). According to Tierney and Farmer (2011), understanding how CSE is developed is vital to creative performance and (perhaps, more importantly) changes in creative performance. For instance, Tierney and Farmer found that as employees’ CSE increased so did their creative performance. An important next step is to investigate the relationship between the extended CSE scale and a measure of creative product or performance, such as the CAT (Amabile, 1982). The CAT is a flexible measure in which a group of judges (i.e., domain experts) evaluates a creative product and has demonstrated high inter-rater reliability (Baer & Kaufman, 2018) across a variety of tasks and domains (e.g., math [α = 0.87] and poetry [α = 0.88]; Hennessey, 1994). Including a reliable outcome measure like the CAT is a necessary step in establishing predictive validity for the extended CSE scale.

Another avenue for future construct validity studies includes divergent thinking. Theoretically, we would expect a valid measure of CSE to be positively related to divergent thinking. Previous studies have examined the relationship between CSE and divergent thinking (Kharkhurin, 2017; Puente-Díaz & Cavazos-Arroyo, 2017). Kharkhurin (2017) estimated the relationship between CSE and divergent thinking to be r = 0.30 with a population of undergraduate students in the United States. However, those findings were contradicted in the Puente-Díaz and Cavazos-Arroyo (2017) study. They found that CSE did not have a positive effect (r = −0.02) on divergent thinking in a population of elementary students in Mexico. When trying to interpret these contradictory findings, we suggest measurement imprecision of CSE as one potential explanation. The Puente-Díaz and Cavazos-Arroyo study used a 3-item CSE scale and reported a somewhat low internal consistency (α = 0.65). The Kharkhurin study used the Something About Myself (SAM) scale (Khatena & Torrance, 1998) which consisted of 50 statements to which respondents simply checked yes/no answering whether it applied to them. No internal consistency measure was reported. Perhaps a CSE measure with more items and improved internal consistency would help future researchers better understand the relationship between CSE and divergent thinking.

Creativity manifests in domain specific ways (Baer, 1998, 2016; Baer & Kaufman, 2005) and using a creative outcome measure is not always feasible. According to Forgeard and Kaufman (2016), about 40 % of empirical articles use a self-report measure for creativity, and we suspect that number has only grown since. Future research should evaluate the relationship between the extended CSE scale and self-perceived creativity across domains. The K-DOCS (Kaufman, 2012) is one avenue in which this could be carried out. The K-DOCS is a valid and reliable self-report measure of individuals’ perceptions of their creativity across five different domains: (a) everyday, (b) scholarly, (c) performance, (d) scientific, and (e) artistic (Kapoor et al., 2021; Kaufman, 2012; McKay et al., 2017), and scholars have identified CSE as a significant predictor of creativity in the everyday, scholarly, and science domains (McKay et al., 2017). Deepening our understanding of creative self-beliefs across domains and micro-domains is a critical area of future research. This also aligns with Haase et al. (2018) call for research that investigates the level of CSE across different domains, and even micro-domains (McKay et al., 2017). Examining potential relationships between the extended CSE scale and self-perceived creativity across domains is another important step in further establishing validity.

Future research in this area should also consider using prominent theoretical frameworks or models to deepen our understanding of the role of CSE, or other creative self-beliefs, with regard to behavior and behavioral change. For example, one viable theoretical framework to study creative behavior is the integrated behavior model (IBM; Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975; Monta˜no & Kasprzyk, 2015). The IBM is a theoretical merging of the theory of reasoned action (TRA; Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980) and the theory of planned behavior (TPB; Ajzen, 1985) and has been widely used to investigate health behaviors (Hill et al., 2022), and more recently, to study a broader range of behaviors, such as social media use (Roth et al., 2021) and the likelihood of voting (Holbert et al., 2020). The IBM has been able to explain large proportions of variance in behaviors and has also been used as a framework for intervention study designs (Holbert et al., 2020; Monta˜no & Kasprzyk, 2015). The three major variable categories of the IBM are attitudes, perceived norms, and personal agency (self-efficacy). CSE, according to this model, is hypothesized to influence intent to engage in creative behaviors. Humans are reasonable actors who make decisions and engage in behaviors based on underlying factors including self-efficacy. Moreover, CSE can be intentionally developed through training (Byrge & Tang, 2015; Mathisen & Bronnick, 2009). Next steps should also include using the extended CSE scale with the application of behavioral change interventions (Steinmetz et al., 2016) that are designed to enhance CSE and consequently, creative performance (Hass et al., 2016; Tierney & Farmer, 2011). Applying behavioral change interventions under the IBM framework is also an important direction for the field in building a reservoir of evidence-based practices, especially with regard to interventions that can develop or enhance CSE and creativity in real-life settings.

Research on CSE has rapidly increased in the past decade and has emerged both theoretically and empirically as an important variable in this area of scholarship; however, there is still much to explore and understand in this area, particularly the relationship between CSE (and other creative self-beliefs) and creativity across domains. The CSE scale developed in this study demonstrated strong evidence of validity and reliability. Although promising, additional studies are necessary to continue validating and refining this extended CSE scale.

Understanding creative behaviors and the factors that predict them is valuable scientific work in fields ranging from education to business and the health sciences. Seeking to blend theories of creativity with established theories of behavioral analyses requires the development of valid and reliable scales that avoid issues related to just-identified models and accurately capture the constructs of the theoretical model. We believe this extended scale has the potential to improve future descriptive models and potential intervention designs that improve and sustain individuals and their creative behaviors across multiple settings. With increasing emphasis on creative behaviors in today’s educational and workplace settings, the study of creativity and CSE has never been more important.
