ABSTRACT Considering the pivotal role of creativity across various eras and the rapid integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in both creative processes and education, this study introduces and provides validity evidence for the AI-­assisted Creativity Questionnaire (AICQ). This new 16-­item instrument aims to quantify human creative potential in AI-­assisted endeavors. Initially, a diverse cohort of 322 university students in Taiwan completed the AICQ from November to December 2023. Through exploratory factor analysis (EFA) of responses from this sample, three distinct factors emerged: (1) AI-­assisted functional creativity (AIFC), (2) AI-­assisted visual artistic creativity (AIVAC), and (3) AI-­assisted ideational creativity (AIIC). One item was removed due to cross-­loading. Subsequently, in September 2024, 330 university students in Taiwan engaged with both the AICQ and the 28-­item Creative Behavior Inventory (CBI). Based on responses from this sample, construct validity evidence for the AICQ was examined using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), which affirmed that a three-­factor model provided a good fit to the data. Gender differences in AICQ scores were found in these subsequent data. Overall, data from participants provided evidence for the reliability as well as convergent, concurrent, and discriminant validity of the scores obtained from the AICQ.

The integration of AI in creative processes can be classified into four key functions: generators, which aid in brainstorming ideas; blenders, which mix existing concepts for inspiration; transformers, which digitize designs for implementation; and editors, which refine content in the later stages of the creative process (Hwang 2022). These functions span across five application areas: content creation, information analysis, content enhancement, information extraction, and data compression. In addition, GenAI is becoming a valuable tool for boosting students' creativity in education. Hutson and Cotroneo (2023) investigated the integration of GenAI in art education. They found that students' conceptualization became more refined with multiple iterations of prompts for the generated artwork by increasing their understanding of what AI can do, and students appreciated how AI enhanced their creativity and ideation process. Despite the increasing interest in how AI impacts human creativity, there is a lack of tools to measure individual creativity in AI contexts. Given the importance of measuring creativity for research and practical applications (Amabile 1983), there has been a demand for the development of assessment tools that keep pace with the technological advancements in creative processes. The current study was inspired by Hoffmann et  al.'s  (2016) Creative Behavior Questionnaire: Digital (CBQD), and its goal of assessing the construct of creative behavior. However, we realized that the CBQD, developed before the rapid development of AI, no longer fully captures the influence of today's technological progress. To bridge this gap, we aimed to design and provide validity evidence for a new instrument tailored to assess creative potential in the AI domain. As suggested by Ivcevic (2009), such an inventory of creative behavior can serve as a preliminary screening tool that can be used prior to conducting laboratory experiments or evaluating an individual's actual creative performance or behavior.

2   |   Theoretical Framework

2.1   |   Creativity

Creativity, identified as a key 21st-­century skill (Trilling and Fadel 2009), involves generating innovative and practical solutions within given constraints (Sternberg 1999). According to Runco and Jaeger's  (2012) Standard Definition of Creativity, creativity requires two essential factors: originality and

Corazza (2016) expanded this definition by emphasizing “potential,” defining creativity as potential originality and effectiveness and describing it as a context-­embedded and dynamic phenomenon. This suggests that creative inconclusiveness is a crucial phase in the creative process that individuals must navigate and show they can manage effectively. While the creative process does not always culminate in a singular clear solution but instead embraces inconclusive outcomes, individuals with high creativity utilize the frustration they encounter due to inconclusive results constructively, whereas those less creative may drop out.

Creativity consists of both creative potential and creative behavior, and these must be clearly distinguished in research (Runco 2007; Ivcevic 2009). This study focuses on creative potential, which refers to cognitive traits and abilities that contribute to creativity, such as personality, divergent thinking, and self-­assessed creativity (Ivcevic 2009). On the other hand, creative behavior is the actual performance or product that occurs as the concrete result of creative potential (Ivcevic 2009). Runco's (2007) hierarchical framework differentiates these two concepts by revising the “4P” model of creativity (Rhodes 1961) and integrating Simonton's  (2012) additional “P”—persuasion. The framework asserts that creative potential consists of components that exist without observable creative results (i.e., “person,” “process,” and “press”) as well as components that directly lead to actual creative outcomes (i.e., “product” and “persuasion”). Additionally, Runco emphasizes the interactions that can occur between these components.

Corazza et al. (2022) suggested that creative potential can be interpreted in up to 15 different ways based on three creativity-­ relevant frameworks. One of these frameworks—particularly relevant to the current study—is the “Four C Model of Creativity” (Kaufman and Beghetto  2009), particularly relevant to this study, includes “mini-­c” (potential for personal exploration), “little-­c” (potential for non-­professional creative performance), “Pro-­C” (potential for professional-­level creativity), and “Big-­C” (potential for extraordinary contributions). Additionally, “embedded individual potential” refers to the creative potential shaped by an individual's cultural environment.

2.2   |   Creativity in Different Contexts and Time Periods

True to its name, the definition and framework of creativity continually evolves, shaped by various cultures (Boden 1998) and historical periods (Sawyer 2011). Sternberg and Karami (2021), for example, recently expanded the theoretical framework of creativity, evolving it from the 6P model to an 8P model. This updated framework retains the six original components of purpose, press, person, problem, process, and product, while emphasizing the increasing significance of propulsion and public recognition in today's context. The rapid evolution of technology has led researchers to explore creativity enabled by technological advancements across history. Boden's  (1998) framework categorizes computational creativity into three components: combinational creativity (combining unrelated ideas), exploratory creativity (generating new concepts within established frameworks), and transformational creativity (redefining existing conceptual dimensions). Wiggins  (2006) subsequently defined computational creativity as the exploration and support, including simulations, of behaviors exhibited by natural and artificial systems through computational techniques that would be considered creative if performed by humans. Further, at the beginning of 21st century, creativity was transformed into a new form known as digital creativity (Lee 2012), encompassing all forms of creative activity facilitated by digital tools (Lee and Chen 2015).

Recent technological advancements have refined the conceptualization of creativity, indicating a new phase in its evolution within the AI era. Because GenAI continues to gain widespread adoption and has revolutionized digital processes in many personal and professional activities with the assistance of sophisticated deep-­learning models such as the Generative Pretrained Transformer, the approach of human-­centered AI and hybrid intelligence has been introduced for developing, refining, and executing ideas in diverse creative disciplines (Rafner, Beaty et al. 2023).

In addition, Runco (2023) updated the Standard Definition of Creativity by adding two components—authenticity and intentionality—to the original components. The objective behind this update was to distinguish human creativity from artificial creativity. Authenticity emphasizes genuine expression reflecting an individual's true desires, while intentionality underscores humans' innate ability to creatively address problems, contrasting with AI's reliance on programmed recognition. Similarly, Wingström et al. (2022) revised the concept of creativity by integrating perspectives from computer scientists and new media artists, outlining 10 distinct definitions of creativity in the era of AI. Two noteworthy updates—interacting with peers or the environment and challenging conventional reality norms—distinguished creativity with AI assistance from conventional forms of creativity, reflecting AI's transformative impact on creativity. Based on these findings, the current study focuses on a new phase of creativity, marking a notable advancement within the era of AI, known as AI-­assisted creativity.

2.3   |   AI-­Assisted Creativity

AI-­assisted creativity occurs when AI serves as either a partner collaborating with individuals to generate creative works, or as an assistant helping individuals in enhance their creativity in various says (Bron 2023). This aligns with the concept of “Co-­ Cre-­AI-­tion” proposed by Vinchon et al. (2023) which emphasizes genuine cooperation, whereby both humans and GenAI contribute to integrated outcomes surpassing individual capabilities, thereby fostering augmented creativity.

In the realm of education, GenAI has the potential to boost creativity by automating processes like brainstorming, idea generation, art and design, music composition, storytelling, and writing. This provides students with diverse starting points for creative projects while also requiring careful management of potential risks (Manditereza and Chamboko-­Mpotaringa 2024). This has been demonstrated in empirical research. Rong et al. (2022), for example, conducted an experiment with middle school students, where the group receiving AI and VR fine arts training showed enhanced creativity, concentration, and enthusiasm compared to the control group undergoing traditional training.

3   |   Factors Related to AI-­Assisted Creativity

Prior research findings have indicated a positive correlation between students' familiarity with GenAI and their frequency of using it (Chan and Hu 2023). GenAI has emerged as a prominent tool for producing high-­quality artistic media across diverse domains including visual arts, music, video, and animation (Epstein et al. 2023). Given the fundamental role of these media in the art field, it is reasonable to presume that students in art-­ related disciplines possess foundational skills or proficiency to effectively utilize AI tools in creating artworks. Therefore, we hypothesize that students in art fields will show greater AI-­ assisted creativity compared to students in non-­art fields.

Gender is considered another potential influential factor, due to its correlation with attitudes toward AI and its effect on self-­ rated creativity. Research has indicated that males tend to exhibit more favorable attitudes toward AI compared to females (Grassini 2023; Sindermann et al. 2021) and to display higher confidence levels and greater engagement with new technologies (Yau and Cheng 2012).

Research on gender differences in creativity has yielded mixed results. Baer and Kaufman (2008) reviewed approximately 74 studies and found no consistent gender differences in creativity. They suggested that environmental factors, such as opportunities and societal expectations, may explain the inconsistent findings. Nakano et al. (2021) analyzed 133 studies and reported mixed findings, with some showing women performing better, others showing men performing better, and the remainder

Given that this study utilizes a self-­rated questionnaire, we propose that males' self-­assessed AI-­assisted creativity will be higher than that of females.

4   |   Purpose of this Study

Considering that measuring creativity is crucial for academic and practical research (Amabile 1983), and there is lack of instrumentation tailored to evaluate creativity within the realm of AI assistance, this study developed and validated the AI-­ assisted Creativity Questionnaire (AICQ) to evaluate creative potential in AI-­assisted environments, while also exploring group differences in AI-­assisted creative potential.

5   |   Research Hypothesis

Based on the aforementioned factors related to AI-­assisted creativity, the following hypotheses are posited:

H1.  Students in art-­related majors will show greater AI-­ assisted creative potential than those in other fields.

H2.  Male students will show greater AI-­assisted creative potential than female students.

6   |   Questionnaire Development

The AICQ was developed following three principles. First, McKim's  (2023) guidelines were employed to incorporate existing literature into the scale construction, including literature selection and table creation. Second, we followed the guidelines in Scale Development Theory and Application (DeVellis and Thorpe 2022) to define measurement objectives, generate an item pool, and conduct expert reviews and cognitive interviews. Finally, the validation process adhered to best practices outlined by Worthington and Whittaker (2006), considering sample size, reporting standards, and utilizing techniques such as factor analysis and structural equation modeling (SEM).

6.2   |   Content Validity

Upon drafting the preliminary questionnaire, a focus group— consisting of an instructional technology expert, a creativity specialist, and five Ph.D. students with diverse international backgrounds—was formed to assess evidence for content validity. Subsequently, a cognitive interview was conducted with nine students from a research-­oriented university in Taiwan to evaluate their interpretation and understanding of the items, aiming to ensure clarity in the content (DeVellis and Thorpe 2022). Following feedback, amendments were made by removing specific names for software applications to address any reported confusion.

The questionnaire, originally crafted in English, was translated into Chinese and back-­translated into English by three Chinese-­speaking Ph.D. students to ensure accuracy. An external interpreter and a Chinese-­speaking professor reviewed the bilingual version for final approval.

7   |   The Present Study

Two studies were conducted to develop and provide validity evidence for the AICQ. Study 1 employed exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to identify underlying factors, evaluate item characteristics, and explore aspects of creative behavior in the AI domain. Study 2 employed confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess the fit of the data to the hypothesized model from Study 1, and provide evidence for convergent, concurrent, and discriminant validity. Both studies also independently tested the research hypotheses (H1 and H2).

8.2   |   Data Analysis

8.2.1   |   Exploratory Factor Analysis

A sample size of 200 is considered adequate for factor analyses involving up to 40 items (Comrey 1988). EFA was conducted with a sufficient sample size (N = 322) using IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0. The EFA applied to the 17 items employed principal axis factoring with varimax rotation to better illustrate interrelationships among factors (Brown 2015). Prior to analysis, each item's univariate distribution was assessed for normality using Kolmogorov– Smirnov tests, and Mardia's tests for multivariate kurtosis and skewness were conducted (Cain et al. 2017). 8.2.2   |   Comparing Arts and Non-­Arts Students and Gender Differences

The difference between students was examined using the factors and corresponding questionnaire items that were established by EFA. For the first research hypothesis (H1), participants were divided into two groups for evaluating: (i) the arts group, including students in art-­related fields such as fashion design, product design, music, and theater, and (ii) the non-­arts group consists of students in majors such as engineering, humanities, management, mathematics, medicine, sciences, social sciences, and technology. For the second research hypothesis (H2), participants were categorized into (i) male and (ii) female based on self-­reported demographics. Welch's t-­test (Welch 1938) was used to compare mean AICQ scores between the groups. This method was chosen because it controls Type 1 error rates and is robust compared to Student's t-­test when homogeneity of variance is not met (Delacre et al. 2017).

No missing values were found among the respondents' responses. Outlying responses, accounting for less than 2% of the dataset and observed within items 8 to 17, were retained for analysis. This decision was based on the belief that these outliers likely represented genuine data points from a distinct subgroup rather than errors in data collection.

9.2   |   Data Analysis

9.2.1   |   Confirmatory Factor Analysis

After extracting factors through EFA in Study 1, a CFA was performed using data from the second, independently-­ collected sample (N = 330) using the R statistical application and lavaan (Rosseel 2012) package to assess construct validity for the AICQ scores. Model fit was evaluated using four indices: root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-­Lewis Index (TLI), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). As fit criteria, an RMSEA value over 0.10 indicates poor fit, 0.08–0.10 suggests mediocre fit, and below 0.08 indicates good fit (MacCallum et al. 1996). For a good fit, CFI and TLI values should exceed 0.95, and SRMR should be less than 0.08 (Hu and Bentler 1999).

9.2.2   |   Composite Reliability, Construct Validity, and Concurrent Validity

To further examine the construct validity evidence for scores obtained from the AICQ, composite reliability was computed, and evidence for both convergent and discriminant validity was evaluated. Cronbach's alpha and composite reliability were used to assess internal consistency of the scale, where the value should be at least 0.70 for scores from the instrument to be considered internally consistent (Cheung et  al.  2023; Cronbach  1951). Convergent validity was assessed by examining the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) value (Cheung et  al.  2023), ensuring it exceeds the 0.5 threshold (Fornell and Larcker  1981). Discriminant validity was examined by comparing the factor correlations with the square root of the AVE value, specifically by assessing whether the correlations

between factors were smaller than the square root of the AVE for each respective factor (Rönkkö and Cho 2022).

Concurrent validity for the AICQ was assessed by computing the Pearson correlation coefficient between the AICQ scores and the overall scores of CBI. For this concurrent validity assessment, it is suggested that the measures to be correlated should be obtained concurrently from the same participants (Kimberlin and Winterstein 2008).

9.2.3   |   Comparing Arts and Non-­Arts Students and Gender differences

The differences between students were examined using the factors and corresponding questionnaire items derived from the CFA. The groupings and data analysis methods from Study 1 were applied.

9.3   |   Results

9.3.1   |   Data Screening and Management

No missing values were found in the data. Outliers, comprising less than 2% of the data, were identified across different CBI and AICQ items. In line with the rationale from Study 1, these outliers were retained for analysis.

9.3.2   |   Confirmatory Factor Analysis

CFA was carried out on the resulting responses to the AICQ, positing a three-­factor model based on the results of the EFA in Study 1. Weighted least squares mean and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimation was used, which is recommended for datasets containing variables with either two or three categories (Beauducel and Herzberg  2006) or fewer than five categories (Rhemtulla et al. 2012). The decision to utilize WLSMV was strengthened by the sample size surpassing 200 participants, rendering it an appropriate choice (Liang and Yang 2014). Consequently, the results from the CFA indicated good model fit based on all four fit indices (CFI = 0.978, TLI = 0.974, RMSEA = 0.063, and SRMR = 0.068). Individual items showed good saturation, with all standardized loadings statistically significant and exceeding 0.40. It was concluded that scores from the 16-­item instrument adhere to a three-­ factor structure. The factor structure of the AICQ is depicted in Figure 2.

9.3.5   |   Differences Between Arts and Non-­Arts Students

The results from Welch's t-­tests showed no significant differences between arts students (n = 28) and non-­arts students (n = 302) on any of the factors (each p > 0.05). Thus, H1 was not supported.

9.3.6   |   Gender Differences

The results from Welch's t-­tests showed significant differences between males (n = 120) and females (n = 210) for all factors of the AICQ, supporting H2. Male students (M = 19.89, SD = 8.84) reported higher mean levels of AIFC compared to females (M = 15.70, SD = 7.14), t(207.85) = 4.43, p < 0.001, with a medium effect size (d = 0.537; Cohen 1988). Similarly, male students (M = 10.32, SD = 3.84) also reported higher mean levels of AIVAC compared to females (M = 9.20, SD = 3.57), t(233.30) = 2.61, p = 0.005, with a small-­to-­medium effect size (d = 0.304; Cohen 1988). Additionally, male students (M = 8.48, SD = 3.07) reported higher mean levels of AIIC than females (M = 7.40, SD = 2.71), t(223.21) = 3.21, p = 0.002, with a small-­ to-­medium effect size (d = 0.380; Cohen 1988).

10   |   Discussion and Conclusion

This study developed a new instrument, the AICQ, comprising 16 items that show evidence of a three-­factor structure consisting of: AIFC, AIVAC, and AIIC.

Functional creativity refers to the ability to create practical products to address real-­life problems or make solid contributions (Cropley and Cropley 2010; e.g. “Generated unique voice tracks or videos”). Visual artistic creativity refers to the ability to create tangible arts such as painting, drawing, and photography (Heilman and Acosta 2013; e.g. “Produced a digital art or creative drawing”). Ideational creativity emphasizes an individual's ability to generate a wide variety of ideas in terms of fluency, flexibility, and originality (Runco and Mraz 1992; Paulus et  al.  2010; e.g., “Using AI tools like chatbots to explore new ideas or generate fresh insights”).
