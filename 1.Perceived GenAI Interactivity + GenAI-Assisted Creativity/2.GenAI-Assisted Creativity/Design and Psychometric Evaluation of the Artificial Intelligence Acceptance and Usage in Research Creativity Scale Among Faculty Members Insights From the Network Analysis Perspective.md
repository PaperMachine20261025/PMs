Keywords: AI adoption in academia | artificial intelligence | faculty members | psychometrics | research creativity | technology acceptance

ABSTRACT The acceptance of artificial intelligence (AI) in academic settings, particularly in the context of research creativity, is a growing area of interest. This study aimed to design and validate the AI Acceptance and Research Creativity Scale (AIA&RCS) among faculty members. This exploratory mixed-­method was conducted among 720 faculty members. A literature review and participant interviews were conducted in the qualitative phase to generate and develop items. In the quantitative phase, face validity, content validity, construct validity, convergent validity and reliability (internal consistency and stability) were used. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) indicated a 4-­factor model of the scale with ‘perceived usefulness and effectiveness of AI in research creativity’, ‘ethical issues in research’, ‘trusted in AI capabilities’ and ‘willingness to use AI’ accounting for 51.6% of the variance. This arrangement was verified by confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), with fit indices that were at suitable levels. Then, the network analysis took into account the four-­factor structure of AIA&RCS further. Similarly, the exploratory graph analysis (EGA) indicated the four-­factor configuration of the AIA&RCS. The 25-­item scale is well-­suited for measuring AI acceptance and research innovation among faculty because of its psychometrics.

1   |   Introduction

Increasingly sophisticated AI systems (e.g., deep learning algorithms, natural language processing, generative systems) have increased the productivity to generate content, process data and generate novel ideas to a whole new level (Wu et al. 2021). By offering services such as generating simulated data, quick analysis of big datasets and decision support, this technology has allowed scientists to think creatively about problems with a more innovative approach (Qin et al. 2024).

One of the undeniable and extraordinary technological advancements in the present century is artificial intelligence (AI) (Sneesl et  al.  2023). AI profoundly impacts almost all aspects of human life, such as health services, the economy, entertainment, education and politics (Benefo et  al.  2022). The advancements in AI technologies over the last few decades have increased productivity across all sectors and provided untold possibilities to augment human innovation (Ohno 2024).

Research findings suggest that psychological readiness and the perceived usefulness of the technology are among the determining factors in its acceptance (Alhwaiti 2023; Ali 2023; Falebita and Kok 2024; Kohen-­Vacs et al. 2024). While some studies indicate that faculty members in academic environments who properly recognise the added value of AI in enhancing creativity and research innovation show greater acceptance of the technology (Rana et al. 2024), AI adoption may still face challenges due to ethical concerns, limitations in technological infrastructure, complexities related to learning and usage, and cultural resistance to technological changes. 1.2   |   AI Acceptance Scale: A New Perspective

There are many scales to evaluate the acceptability of AI in different societies. Such a scale is the Generative Artificial Intelligence Acceptance Scale (GAIAS) (Yilmaz, Yilmaz, and Ceylan 2023), which measures students' tolerance for generative AI use in education. That is a key difference because faculty (though they, too, use AI tools) might have different expectations, motivations, and engagement levels than students. Thus, the scale was tailor-­made for students, an audience whose AI use in schools was distinctly different. This scale, therefore, is not a good one to administer to faculty because it was not designed to evaluate how faculty perceive or act towards generative AI.

A second scale is the Teachers' Acceptance of Artificial Intelligence in Education (TAAI) scale, which assesses preservice teachers' acceptance of AI in the classroom (Guo, Shi, and Zhai  2024). The scale has five factors—perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, behavioural intention, self-­efficacy and anxiety. The research validating this scale involved preservice teachers in many different subjects, and the instrument is reliable and robust, with 27 items on these dimensions. However, this scale is more focussed on teachers and their experiences in the field of AI in the classroom. Thus, it cannot be used to measure faculty's acquiescence to AI because faculty regularly use AI in other, more general, nonteaching uses: research, administration and decision-­making. In general, tools are in place that enable researchers to test a variety of factors impacting AI acceptance: behavioural motivation, mindset, perceived value, institutional infrastructure, psychological readiness and moral issues. Such dimensions can give helpful information on how users think about AI in a particular environment, such as schools or workplace technology. However, for all the promise that AI might offer in terms of creativity, productivity and workflow improvement, there is still no valid, standardised measure to measure AI acceptance and deployment in the academy. Current scales, whether the Student GAIAS for students or the Preservice TAAI for teachers, cannot accurately reflect faculty's particular functions and rich experiences with AI systems. It would also help establish successful plans for AI adoption, faculty preparation and advancing AI as widely as possible in the academic and research process. For this reason, the current research was carried out to develop and validate the ‘AI Acceptance and Research Creativity Scale (AIA&RCS)’ for faculty members. This research uses psychometric properties to develop a robust measure of AI adoption in academia.

1.3   |   Network Analysis: A New Perspective in Psychometric Studies

Psychometric research has generally used classic techniques such as CFA and EFA. These techniques are grounded in latent variable models, which treat constructs as unobservable things whose symptoms or indications can be measured (Davoudi, Pourshahbaz, et al. 2023; Tosi et al. 2024). For instance, latent models could characterise COVID-­19 pathology as a condition that manifests through fever, cough and fatigue. Similarly, in psychopathology, diseases such as posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are perceived as unrealised things that emerge as re-­ experiences of trauma, sleep disturbance and increased arousal.

However, new dimensional tools, like network analysis, provide a new way of thinking about psychological constructions. While latent models think of constructs as secret causes of symptoms,

Network analysis has gained much traction in psychometrics studies due to significant developments in its use. These are developments such as the capacity to find central symptoms with the highest impact on a network and the degree of the interconnection between symptoms or clusters or communities within a network (Borsboom and Cramer 2013; Davoudi et al. 2024; Azarmehr et  al.  2024). Such knowledge can guide targeted treatments and rework theories. One promising extension of network analysis in psychometrics uses exploratory graph analysis (EGA). EGA is a network approach to finding the dimensionality of a dataset by grouping variables into communities with a connection to each other (Abdelrahman et  al.  2024; Davoudi, Pourshahbaz, et al. 2023; Golino et al. 2020; Golino and Epskamp  2017). Because EGA is free of preconceptions about the underlying structure, in contrast to other techniques, it is pretty flexible and revealing in developing and validating scales.

Hence, EGA has made it into psychometric research, and an increasing number of studies use EGA to investigate the shape and characteristics of scales. For example, this method is used in different scales such as socioemotional skills instrument for teachers (Sáez-­Delgado, Mella-­Norambuena, and López-­ Angulo  2024), life satisfaction scale (Dominguez-­Vergara et al. 2024), Brief Inventory of Perceived Stress (BIPS-­C) (Meng et al. 2024) and Intuitive Eating Scale-­2 (IES-­2) (Ji et al. 2024). Therefore, this method of developing a psychometric estimation of the AIA&RCS could provide a dimensional perspective in the context of AI acceptance.

1.4   |   Current Study

The current study developed the Arabic version of the AIA&RCS. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA), EGA, and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were employed to validate the scale. Additionally, convergent validity was assessed using the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) criteria. Finally, the study examined the scale's internal consistency, with a minimum acceptable threshold of 0.70 considered for reliability.

2   |   Theoretical Basis

In the case of AI technologies, the accelerated growth has created new possibilities for research creativity on the part of university faculty. The design of this article takes up some major models and theories of AI acceptance and AI research innovation.

Ajzen  (1991) created the theory of planned behaviour (TPB) based on three elements: attitude to the behaviour, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control (Ajzen 1991). For AI acceptance, faculty members' views about AI, social encouragement (from the institution or other faculty members) and skills with AI tools can all affect whether a faculty member uses AI for research and teaching.

UTAUT, the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (Venkatesh et al. 2003), four constructs of performance expectancy (the expectation that the technology will enhance performance), effort expectancy (ease of use of the technology), social influence (social coercion to employ the technology) and facilitating conditions (infrastructure and resources for the application of technology) predict behavioural intention (Venkatesh et al. 2003). The model can help account for how faculty accept AI—for different reasons—a mixture of personal and cultural influences and how that acceptance can shape research imagination.

The componential theory of creativity (CTC) is a meta-­theory describing the social and psychological factors driving creativity within an organisation. This theory explains the influence of the at-­home and workplace on employees' creative actions (Amabile  1983, 2018). The faculty defines Amabile's  (2018) creativity theory research creativity as the capacity to generate innovative thoughts, create new projects and tackle challenging problems in the academy. According to it, creativity arises from the interaction of three things: domain-­specific knowledge (what is needed and skillful in the domain under study), cognition (the cognitive capacity leading to new thought) and motivation (the personal desire to act out of interest and pleasure) (Amabile 2018). AI could thus provide access to massive amounts of data and sophisticated analyses, thus contributing to faculty research creativity theory. Creative thinking has been positively influenced by technologies, for instance, as we employ them to analyse data, create new ideas and design novel research initiatives (Figoli, Mattioli, and Rampino 2022).

The human-­AI co-­creation model describes the creativity process in the age of AI and how AI changes the playing field at each step. The model also looks at how AI can optimise each swoop of imaginative action and make the meaning more efficiently presented. Its focus on co-­design speaks to the importance of collaboration and is an innovation in human-­AI co-­creation. Results from practical use examples indicate that AI creativity has influenced many fields, from opening new possibilities for individuals and society to opening new problems and possibilities in technology, society and education (Wu et al. 2021).

2.1   |   Summarising the Conceptualisation of Literature

The rapid expansion of AI has changed the nature of research creativity, particularly among university faculty. Studying related theoretical frameworks gives us a mixed picture of the relationship between AI adoption and research creativity. TAM focuses on ‘perceived usefulness’ and ‘perceived ease of use’ as primary drivers of technological adoption, driving attitudes and indirectly driving intentions to use AI in research. This is followed up by TPB, which consists of ‘attitude’, ‘subjective norms’ and ‘perceived behavioural control’, focussing on faculty perceptions, social support and self-­efficacy for AI use. UTAUT adds to this framework by treating ‘performance expectancy’, ‘effort expectancy’, ‘social influence’ and ‘facilitating conditions’ as drivers of behaviour intention, which include personal motivation, cultural norms and institutional setting.

From the creativity angle, CTC focuses on domain expertise, thinking and motivation and suggests that AI will boost research creativity through access to big data and advanced analytics. In the human-­AI co-­creation model, AI optimally facilitates the entire creative cycle, making it possible to collaborate and create. The creative empowerment approach uses AI to lift the bar and accelerate ideation by simplifying data creation and analysis. Combined, such theories give us an integrative picture: AI helps but also works more closely with scientists, changing habits and reinventing possibilities. Such a synthesis calls for standardised measures to evaluate AI acceptance and techniques to utilise fully in the lab and learning lab.

3.2   |   Phase 1: Qualitative Phase

This section consisted of literature analysis and participant interviews used to develop the study items. The literature review conducted exhaustive searches on databases including Scopus, PubMed, ProQuest, Iranmedex, Elsevier, and Medlib using keywords for AI adoption, ethics, and research ingenuity. Interviews used purposive sampling to identify faculty who had used or knew AI tools in research. A semi-­structured interview (35 min on average) took place remotely through WhatsApp and Telegram.

The interviews were meant to gather comprehensive views of what drives AI adoption and the effects on research creativity. According to Graneheim and Lundman (2004), a general content analysis framework was used. These were the phases of qualitative analysis that we included:

Statistics and interviews: Interviews were transcribed to an exact copy from the audio.

Initial coding: The transcripts were double-­checked to find valuable information and were reduced to initial codes.

Design for themes: Codes were sorted into categories and subcategories according to frequency and conceptual interest. Themes were inductively deduced—by the participants themselves.

Data saturation: We identified data saturation at 15 interviews with no new themes or subcategories.

Reliability: The themes were blind-­reviewed by two separate researchers. Disagreements were solved by argument until a consensus was reached.

Software used: We used MAXQDA 10 to arrange and analyse qualitative data.

3.3.3   |   Statical Analysis

The SPSS 26 for EFA and AMOS 24 for CFA were used for data analysis. Further EGA was conducted using R-­Studio software to supplement the validation.

Face validity was validated by qualitative and quantitative methods to ensure the tool's reliability and veracity. For the qualitative assessment, ten of the target group members reviewed the scale and gave suggestions on the tool's usability, accuracy and layout. Their suggestions were carefully incorporated into the final draft. Quantitatively, all items whose scores fell below the cutoff of 1.5 were excluded so that only items that met the predefined relevance threshold were retained (Johnson 2021).

A group of 10 subject-­matter experts tested content validity with decades of experience in quality research, tool development and online learning. These professionals scrutinised the instrument for fluency, grammatical correctness, item order and consistency. The content validity ratio (CVR) and content validity index (CVI) were calculated for this analysis. Items with a CVR above 0.62 and CVI above 0.79 were judged valid and retained to follow accepted validation guidelines (Almanasreh, Moles, and Chen 2019; Binti Hassan 2018).

EFA and CFA assessed the construct validity. The data were randomly assigned to two subgroups in a descriptive cross-­sectional study of 360 people. The principal axis factoring (PAF) with Promax rotation performed EFA to detect clusters of highly correlated variables. Tests to be tested initially included the Kaiser– Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test for sampling adequacy (if it was more significant than 0.60, it was suitable) and Bartlett's test of sphericity (to ensure the existence of significant correlations in the data [p < 0.05]) (De Winter and Dodou 2012; Hill 2011; Zhang et al. 2024; Kordbagheri, Abdelrahman, et al. 2024).

The maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) for CFA was conducted with AMOS 24 software. This step was to try out the proposed factor structure described in the EFA. We measured model fit using the root-­mean-­square error of approximation (RMSEA < 0.08), comparative fit index (CFI > 0.90), incremental fit index (IFI > 0.90), goodness-­of-­fit index (GFI > 0.90) and Tucker-­Lewis index (TLI > 0.90) (Cheung and Rensvold 2002; Marsh, Balla, and Hau  2013; Van Laar and Braeken  2021; Kordbagheri, Kordbagheri, et al. 2024). These indices provided a strong indication of the tool's structural integrity. Furthermore, multigroup analysis was used to test the four-­factor structure for invariance based on gender and work experience: fit indices that changed below 0.1 indicate invariance (Kenny, Kaniskan, and McCoach 2015; Rajaeiramsheh et al. 2021). 4   |   Results

Based on the findings from interviews with faculty members, literature reviews and tools related to AI, the pool of items included 82 items (32 items derived from the literature review and 50 items from the qualitative section) in a 5-­point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. After several rounds of review by the research team, 40 items were either deleted or merged, and the remaining 42 items were carried forward to the psychometric evaluation phase. During the face validity and content validity stages, no items were removed.

4.1   |   Exploratory Factor Analysis

To assess the adequacy of the sample size, the KMO test was used. The results showed that the sample size was adequate for factor analysis (KMO = 0.859). Additionally, Bartlett's test of sphericity indicated that the correlations between variables were sufficiently large and significant, making them appropriate for CFA (Chi-­Square = 9672.39, df = 861, p < 0.000).

The AIA&RCS consists of 25 items and 4 dimensions. The three dimensions—‘trust in AI capabilities’, ‘ethical challenges in research’ and ‘willingness to use AI’—are related to the acceptance of AI in research, while the dimension ‘perceived usefulness and effectiveness of AI in research creativity’ pertains to the use of AI in research creativity. Scoring is based on a five-­point Likert scale: strongly disagree (1), disagree (2), neutral (3), agree (4) and strongly agree (5). It is important to note that all items in the dimensions ‘trust in AI capabilities’ and ‘ethical challenges in research’ are reverse scored. For the three dimensions related to AI acceptance in research, the score range is between 25 and 125. A score lower than 50 indicates low acceptance, a score between 50 and 90 represents moderate acceptance and a score above 90 indicates high acceptance of AI in research. A score below 25 indicates low effectiveness, a score between 25 and 40 reflects moderate effectiveness and a score above 40 represents high effectiveness.

5   |   Discussion

The study developed and validated the AIA&RCS among faculty members, identifying a four-­factor structure: ‘perceived usefulness and effectiveness of AI in research creativity’, ‘ethical challenges in research’, ‘trust in AI capabilities’ and ‘willingness to use AI’. The scale was found to be remarkably psychometrically sound and exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were reliable and valid. The salient results suggest that faculty are comfortable with AI as a means to stimulate research creativity, but ethics and trust matter. The research offers a generic scale for measuring AI adoption, which will allow for targeted interventions to facilitate AI adoption in the university setting.

In the present research, ‘perceived usefulness and effectiveness of AI in research creativity’ has the same proportion with ‘Perceived usefulness’ in the TAM (Davis 1989). 6   |   Conclusion

The 25-­item AIA&RCS has proven to be a valid and useful way to determine whether faculty values and uses AI in an experimentational creative way. The scale's correct AVE and CR values mean that its items and factors measure precisely the theoretical objects intended. Furthermore, the EFA and CFA results also verify that the AIA&RCS is stable and robust in factor structure, making it more apprehensible in academic and research environments. The EGA further confirmed that the tool was reliable and stable, making it easy for the researcher and educational administrators to deploy it.

6.1   |   Limitations and Future Direction

This research reveals a wealth of information on the creation and validation of the 25-­item AIA&RCS as a measure of AI acceptance and research creativity. Yet, there are some limitations to consider that might limit the interpretation and generalisation of the results.

The first was that they had faculty members working at universities in specific areas—say, from certain Middle Eastern countries, Jordan and Saudi Arabia. This demographic circumscription might hinder the application of the results to other regions or cultures. Extending the study to faculty from more countries and institutions might make the tool more universal. Second, the cross-­sectional design is only an ebb and flow of views and actions, so the temporal effects of AI acceptance and its impact on research innovation are still to be discovered. A longitudinal study would be more informative in showing how these are changing over time.

The research was also based on self-­reports, which could be biased by social attractiveness or memory biases. Future studies might also include observational or experimental techniques to validate and supplement self-­reports. And that was all quantitative analysis; adding qualitative approaches might help us understand contextual and subjective aspects of AI adoption and its influence on research imagination.

6.2   |   Practical Implications

This research has practical lessons for universities and policymakers seeking to bring AI into research communities. With the AIA&RCS, institutions can measure faculty interest in AI and plan interventions (trainers, infrastructure) to promote adoption. Knowledge of moral issues, confidence and usefulness can then inform policies to overcome challenges such as data privacy and algorithmic bias and build a society of ethical AI use. In addition, the scale can be used to detect demographic or disciplinary differences in AI acceptance for personalised interventions to encourage the human–AI interaction and research creativity across different academic environments.
