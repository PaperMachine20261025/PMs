While digital innovations promote organizational sustainable development, they put higher de­ mands on organizational members’ digital competencies. Especially with the widespread diffu­ sion of generative artificial intelligence (GAI), GAI literacy has become a critical competency for fostering the human dimension of organizational sustainability. This study aims to explore the antecedents of GAI literacy and its roles in human sustainability. We conducted a two-phase study using mixed methods. In the qualitative phase, 30 semi-structured interview transcripts were analyzed using a grounded theory approach to identify the antecedents of GAI literacy. In the quantitative phase, based on social cognitive theory, we constructed a research model to examine the formation mechanisms of GAI literacy and its impact on employee well-being and innovative performance. An examination of survey data from 398 respondents indicated that critical thinking, problem solving skills, peer influence, and organizational support were positively associated with GAI literacy, which in turn fostered employee well-being and innovative per­ formance. Furthermore, innovation climate strengthened the relationship between GAI literacy and innovative performance. This study reveals the antecedents of GAI literacy and its facilitating role in human sustainability, refining the research on digital sustainability in organizations from a human-centric perspective. It also provides practical implications for policymakers and managers to promote organizational sustainable development in the digital age.

Digital sustainability, which refers to the use of digital innovations to promote sustainability in economic, environmental, and social aspects, is gaining substantial attention from both academia and industry (Brown, 2025; Kotlarsky et al., 2023). Due to their transformative impacts on economic growth (Lin & Xu, 2025), environmental performance (Guan et al., 2023), and social welfare (Tim et al., 2020), digital innovations are increasingly recognized as the key driver for organizations to achieve and maintain their sus­ tainable development. Among the most cutting-edge digital innovations, GAI has been rapidly adopted across various organizations and is fundamentally reshaping the way people work (Mayer et al., 2025; Kanbach et al., 2024; Serra-Simon et al., 2025). Despite the growing research interest in the roles of GAI in organizational sustainability, two critical gaps remain. First, according to the three-pillar framework of sustainability, organizational sustainability typically encompasses economic prosperity, environ­ mental integrity, and social sustainability (Florea et al., 2013; Purvis et al., 2019). However, most studies have only focused on the economic and environmental dimensions, neglecting the impact of GAI on social sustainability, which is also referred to as human sustainability and emphasizes the health and flourishing of organizational members (Kim & Park, 2017; Spreitzer et al., 2012). In the organizational context, human sustainability manifests as employees’ ability to maintain physical and mental health while achieving personal growth and generativity (Barnes et al., 2023). As the core value of humanistic management, human sustainability exerts a profound influence on the overall sustainability of organizations (Townsend & Romme, 2024). Especially in the AI age, in which work practice and organizational governance have been profoundly transformed, it is necessary to study organizational sustainability from a human-centric perspective.

Second, digital sustainability is not solely concerned with the impact of digital innovations, but also extends to how people appropriately use digital technologies to obtain greater benefits and minimize corresponding harms (Brown, 2025). However, most existing studies have only focused on the impact of GAI technology itself on organizational sustainability performance, neglecting the role of users' competencies in GAI use. In practice, while some individuals and organizations have effectively used GAI, many others misuse or have misconceptions about it (Liu, 2025), which limit the benefits they can obtain and even cause unintended harms to their sustainability (Begen & Atasoy, 2024). The core of this polarization lies in the widening GAI literacy gap, which has evolved into a core challenge that constrains organizations’ sustainable development (Suarez & Garcia-Marinoso, 2025). Therefore, as improving AI literacy for digital sustainability has become a global consensus, organizational managers and policymakers should also recognize the importance of cultivating individual GAI literacy while investing in digital infrastructure. Motivated by the above gaps, this study aims to link individual GAI literacy to human sustainability, providing a micro and human-centric perspective for the research on GAI-driven organizational sustainability. Specifically, this study proposes two questions:

(1) What factors influence individuals' GAI literacy? (2) How does GAI literacy influence human sustainability?

To address these questions, we conducted a mixed-methods study. During the first phase, we conducted a qualitative study to identify the antecedents of GAI literacy. We conducted semi-structured interviews with 30 GAI users, and then analyzed the interview transcripts using a grounded theory method. Four antecedents were identified: critical thinking, problem solving skills, peer influence, and organizational support. During the second phase, we constructed a quantitative model based on social cognitive theory to empirically examine these antecedents of GAI literacy and its effects on human sustainability. In this model, GAI literacy was conceptualized as a second-order construct encompassing four sub-dimensions: awareness, use, evaluation, and ethics (Wang et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2026). Human sustainability was characterized by employees' well-being and innovative performance in the workplace (Tordera et al., 2020; Tuyet & Nguyen, 2026). The results indicated that critical thinking, problem solving skills, peer influence, and organizational support positively affected GAI literacy, which in turn facilitated well-being and innovative perfor­ mance. Besides, the effect of GAI literacy on human sustainability may also be shaped by contextual conditions. To capture the boundary conditions, we incorporated innovation climate and job autonomy as moderating variables. Innovation climate reflects the extent to which organizations encourage, support, and reward innovation. It serves as a critical contextual factor influencing in­ dividuals' innovative behavior and performance (Ma et al., 2021). Job autonomy reflects the degree of individuals’ independence in how they perform tasks. It determines whether they can flexibly use GAI in their work and leads to positive consequences (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). By including these two moderators, this study accounts for both organizational and job-related factors that may influence how GAI literacy translates into human sustainability. The results showed that organizational innovation climate positively moderated the relationship between GAI literacy and innovative performance.

This study makes the following contributions. First, this study refines the research on digital sustainability in organizations to the individual level, revealing the micro mechanisms of organizational digital sustainability from a human-centric perspective. Second, it extends the application of social cognitive theory to GAI literacy research by identifying and validating its individual and environ­ mental antecedents, providing an integrated framework for understanding the formation of GAI literacy. Third, this study deepens our understanding of how GAI literacy promotes human sustainability in organizations and reveals the boundary conditions under which GAI literacy promotes innovative performance from the perspective of organizational innovation climate. 2. Literature review and theoretical background

2.1. Human dimension of organizational sustainability

Organizational sustainability refers to an organization's ability to achieve and maintain sustainable development, which typically involves economic prosperity, environmental integrity, and social sustainability (Florea et al., 2013; Hart & Milstein, 2003). As it is crucial to an organization's competitive advantage and long-term success, organizational sustainability has been regarded as a core agenda in business practice and has received extensive attention from academia in recent years (Kiron et al., 2012). Meanwhile, the rapid technological advancement has accelerated the digital transformation of organizations across various sectors. It not only opti­ mizes business processes and fosters innovation in products and services, but also creates new opportunities for organizations to meet their sustainability objectives (Kotlarsky et al., 2023). A large body of empirical studies has examined the impact of digital technol­ ogies such as GAI on organizations' financial performance and ecological benefits (Baquero et al., 2026; Wang & Zhang, 2025c; Ye et al., 2024; Zu et al., 2025), shedding light on the roles of digital technologies in enabling organizations to achieve economic and environmental sustainability. However, there is still a marked lack of attention to the social dimension of organizational sustainability, specifically the human sustainability in organizations.

As a central element of organizational sustainability, human sustainability refers to organizational members' ongoing ability to maintain health without undermining growth or generativity (Barnes et al., 2023). From the perspective of individuals' long-term career development in organizations, human sustainability primarily encompasses two core dimensions: well-being and innovative performance (Tordera et al., 2020). Specifically, well-being represents individuals' physical and mental health, which directly affects their job satisfaction, organizational commitment, work engagement, and turnover intentions (Guest, 2017; Wright & Cropanzano, 2004). Innovative performance, as a core metric of individuals' productivity, relates to their personal growth and can exert a signif­ icant impact on organizations' overall productivity, innovation capacity, and competitive advantage (Shalley et al., 2004; Yuan & Woodman, 2010). Therefore, this study aims to focus on the human dimension of organizational sustainability and examine in­ dividuals’ well-being and innovative performance to provide a human-centric perspective for the research on organizational digital sustainability.

2.2. GAI literacy

In recent years, GAI has been widely applied across various organizations. With advantages such as automatically generating content, enhancing human intelligence, and optimizing decision-making processes, GAI is profoundly reshaping industrial operating models and injecting new momentum into sustainable development (Wang and Zhang, 2025a). Existing research indicates that GAI technology can not only significantly enhance enterprises' sustainability performance (Wang & Zhang, 2025a, 2025b), but also pro­ vides technological support for the implementation of multiple SDGs through diverse pathways, such as empowering quality education (SDG 4), promoting health and well-being (SDG 3), and driving industrial innovation (SDG 9) (Ojutkangas et al., 2022; Sharma et al., 2025). Despite its broad application prospects, the deployment and value realization of GAI heavily depend on users' cognitive and operational capabilities. In practice, most ordinary users lack sufficient awareness of GAI's functional boundaries and application scenarios, making it difficult to fully leverage its actual value. In this context, individuals' GAI literacy is not only crucial for their own equitable career development opportunities but also serves as a critical factor in promoting stable and long-term organizational development (Chen et al., 2024).

Based on the concepts of digital literacy (Chen et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024) and AI literacy (Kaya et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023), Wang et al. (2024) have defined GAI literacy as the comprehensive ability to accurately identify, effectively utilize, and critically assess GAI while adhering to ethical standards. Subsequently, Yan et al. (2026) have further proposed a four-dimensional framework of GAI literacy: awareness, use, evaluation, and ethics. This study adopts this framework to conceptualize GAI literacy (Wang et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2026). Compared to general digital literacy frameworks, this framework has several advantages. First, this framework covers the full range of competencies required for GAI use, from cognitive foundations to ethical norms. Second, the evaluation and ethical dimensions correspond to the uncertainty of GAI output and its social impacts related to sustainability. Third, this framework has been validated in multiple domains and is suitable for the general public population (Yan et al., 2026). Specifically, the awareness dimension refers to an individual's ability to recognize the application of GAI in different scenarios and to understand its decision-making mechanisms; the use dimension emphasizes the ability to effectively utilize GAI to complete a certain task; the evaluation dimension is the ability to judge the reliability and limitations of AI-generated contents; and the ethic dimension refers to the ability to be aware of the responsibilities and risks associated with GAI use (Wang et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2026).

Existing research has preliminarily revealed the antecedents and consequences of GAI literacy. Factors influencing individuals' GAI literacy include critical questioning skills (Shi et al., 2025), hands-on experience (Honigsberg et al., 2025), and relevant guidance and training (Beninger et al., 2025). Although these studies provided valuable insights into the cultivation of GAI literacy, most of them have focused primarily on the educational context and have not yet systematically integrated or validated the key factors affecting GAI literacy. Furthermore, existing research has also explored the effects of GAI literacy on individuals’ attitudes and behaviors, such as psychological well-being (Shi et al., 2025), GAI risk awareness (Wu et al., 2025), GAI usage (Choe & Woo, 2025), and task performance (Shi et al., 2025; Wang & Zhang, 2025c). 2.3. Social cognitive theory

The formation and development of an individual's GAI literacy are deeply intertwined with personal cognition and external environment. This dynamic interrelationship resonates precisely with the core tenets of social cognitive theory (SCT). Proposed by the psychologist Bandura, this theory serves as a classic theoretical framework for explaining individual behavior. Its core lies in the concept of triadic reciprocal determinism, which posits that there are continuous and dynamic interactions among the external environment, cognitive factors, and individual behavior. According to Bandura (2006), there is a bidirectional relationship between any two of these factors, and they continually evolve across different environments, cognitions, and behaviors. According to SCT, individual behavior is viewed as the outcome of the interplay between individual cognition and external environmental factors.

SCT is often used to explain the formation of individual attitudes and behaviors. It is appropriate for this study to use SCT to explore the formation mechanisms of GAI literacy and its impact on human sustainability, including individuals' well-being and innovative performance. First, GAI literacy integrates individuals' cognitive understanding and practical application skills regarding GAI. Its development relies not only on individual cognitive foundations but is also significantly influenced by environmental factors (Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b). Second, as a crucial cognitive and behavioral competency, GAI literacy is expected to directly influence individuals’ status and behaviors in organizations. Meanwhile, this influence may also be moderated by environmental factors such as innovation climate and job autonomy. Therefore, this study takes SCT as the core theoretical foundation for constructing the research model.

3. Research design

To increase the relevance and rigor of this study, we employed a mixed-methods approach that comprised qualitative and quan­ titative stages. This mixed-methods approach is effective for both exploration and validation in research, as it allows us to gain an indepth understanding of the phenomenon (qualitative) while testing the causal relationships based on a large amount of quantitative data (quantitative).

In terms of GAI literacy, its antecedents have not been examined in depth. Moreover, the research on its potential consequences on human sustainability and corresponding boundary conditions in the organizational context is still lacking. Therefore, we conducted a qualitative study (Study 1) to examine the antecedents of GAI literacy. Then we applied the findings to a quantitative study (Study 2). Based on the antecedents of GAI literacy identified in Study 1 and SCT, Study 2 developed a theoretical model to validate the ante­ cedents, consequences, and boundary conditions of GAI literacy. 4.1. Data collection

During the first phase, we conducted qualitative interviews to identify the critical antecedents of GAI literacy. In order to find suitable interviewees, we recruited GAI users across various industries through social platforms and screened the subjects following the sampling principles: (1) having experience using GAI; (2) being able to understand the interview questions and express their opinions; and (3) agreeing to the recording of the interviews. Finally, 30 GAI users with diverse industry backgrounds were selected as the participants (as shown in Table A.1 in Appendix A), covering different age groups and education levels.

We employed semi-structured interviews to collect qualitative data. Based on extensive literature research, we designed the pre­ liminary interview outline. Subsequently, we conducted pre-interviews with two respondents to refine and finalize the interview outline. During the interviews, participants were asked about: (1) their recent experiences using GAI; (2) the challenges they encountered when using GAI; and (3) the factors that influenced their perceptions and use of GAI. We conducted in-depth semistructured interviews with these respondents, with each interview lasting 30 to 50 min. We collected over 70,000 words of interview data through this process.

4.2. Coding process

We applied Corbin & Strauss (1990) systematic open, axial, and selective coding procedure to identify the critical factors influ­ encing GAI literacy. To enhance reliability, the coding process was jointly discussed with two doctoral students in information management, thereby reducing subjective bias (Birks et al., 2013; Vannoy & Salam, 2010). After coding the 30 semi-structured interview materials, we found four factors that influence individual GAI literacy: critical thinking, problem solving skills, peer influence, and organizational support. To ensure the validity of the study, all the factors listed in this study were mentioned multiple times by different respondents, and the coding results are shown in Fig. 3.

Critical thinking refers to the process of questioning, making sense of things, events, and people, and analyzing them (Kucharska & Erickson, 2023). It includes proactively identifying errors, biases, or logical inconsistencies in information; assessing the reliability and currency of information; and understanding the limitations of various information sources. In the interview data, respondents indi­ cated that they habitually scrutinize, cross-validate, and make independent judgments about the information they receive (A1-A4).

Problem solving skills refer to individuals’ ability to identify, analyze, and implement effective strategies in response to complex or stressful situations (Korkmaz et al., 2017). It reflects their ability to translate domain-specific knowledge into actionable steps and effective solutions. From the textual data, we found that respondents are concerned about their ability to express their needs accurately and emphasized the necessity of having a problem-solving approach in mind first (B1-B4).

Peer influence refers to the demonstration effect and social pressure individuals experience from peers’ attitudes and behaviors while learning and using GAI (Choi et al., 2025). When they observe their peers improving efficiency, gaining recognition, or creating innovative solutions through GAI, they are motivated to learn and adopt the technology. This social influence from colleagues or friends is a key force shaping the GAI adoption climate within an organization. In the interview, many respondents reported using GAI because their colleagues did (C1-C4).

Organizational support refers to individuals’ perception of institutional, resource-related, and attitudinal support from their or­ ganization on GAI use (Venkatesh et al., 2003). It includes providing relevant training opportunities, procuring or granting access to the necessary tools and resources, and leaders demonstrating and advocating for GAI adoption. In the interview, respondents indicated that the professional training provided by their company enables them to use GAI more effectively (D1-D4).

5. Study 2: quantitative research

Based on qualitative research findings, we identified key factors influencing GAI literacy. To further clarify the mechanisms un­ derlying the development of GAI literacy and its impact on human sustainability, this study constructed a conceptual model based on SCT. We examined how individual factors (critical thinking and problem-solving skills) and environmental factors (peer influence and organizational support) influence GAI literacy, and how GAI literacy influences innovative performance and well-being. Moreover, by testing the moderating effects of the innovation climate and job autonomy, we identified which organizational and job-related factors shape the impact of GAI literacy on human sustainability. Critical thinking refers to the process of questioning, making sense of things, events, and people, and analyzing them (Kucharska & Erickson, 2023). Research has shown that critical thinking is essential for users to circumvent technological biases and make rational decisions (Walter, 2024). In the GAI context, where the output of GAI carries the risk of uncertainty and hallucination, critical thinking enables users to proactively question and cross-validate the accuracy and logic of the AI-generated content, rather than blindly accepting it. This continuous process of scrutiny and reflection not only strengthens individuals’ ability to deepen their understanding of how GAI works and improve the quality of human-GAI interaction, but also forms the core cognitive foundation for building high-level GAI literacy. It drives users to transform from passive recipients of information into active evaluators and collaborators, and this role transformation is the essence of GAI literacy. Therefore, we hypothesized the following:

Hypothesis 1. (H1): Critical thinking is positively associated with GAI literacy.

Problem solving skills refer to individuals’ ability to identify, analyze, and implement effective strategies in response to complex or stressful situations (Korkmaz et al., 2017). Research has shown that the explicit goal of problem solving is the core motivation that drives technological exploration and learning (Rom´an-Gonz´alez et al., 2017). In the GAI context, the interaction between users and GAI is essentially a human-machine collaborative process aimed at solving problems. Users with strong problem-solving skills can integrate GAI into their workflow more effectively. Not only do they know how to break down tasks and plan steps, but they can also guide GAI to generate more responsive outputs through precise questioning and iterative feedback. This process continuously strengthens the efficiency and depth of their collaboration with GAI, thus systematically improving their GAI application capabilities. Therefore, individuals who can solve problems effectively usually demonstrate higher GAI literacy. We hypothesized the following:

Hypothesis 2. (H2): Problem-solving skills are positively associated with GAI literacy.

Peer influence refers to the demonstration effect and social pressure individuals experience from peers’ attitudes and behaviors while learning and using GAI (Cho et al., 2025). Observing and learning from peers is an important way for individuals to acquire new skills (Lee, 2014; Purani et al., 2019; Wolske et al., 2020). In the GAI context, when individuals observe their colleagues or friends successfully applying GAI to improve work efficiency, solve complex problems, or create new value, this demonstration not only provides them with examples to follow but also creates a healthy competitive environment. Such environmental pressure and learning opportunities formed by peers can effectively stimulate their motivation to learn GAI, reduce their uncertainties and concerns about using the new technology, and thus promote the systematic development of their cognition, skills, and judgment related to GAI. Therefore, we hypothesized the following:

Hypothesis 3. (H3): Peer influence is positively associated with GAI literacy.

Organizational support refers to the resources, training, incentives, and institutional safeguards provided by an organization to promote the application of GAI (Le & Lei, 2019; Venkatesh et al., 2003). This support encompasses not only material and institutional investments but also conveys the organization's emphasis on technology application at the cognitive level (Cho et al., 2025). When individuals perceive that their organization or community is willing to invest substantial resources in their skills development, they tend to exhibit stronger learning motivation and consequently engage more proactively in acquiring new tools and knowledge (Chen et al., 2015; Lin, 2014; Razmerita et al., 2016). With the rapid development of GAI, people often face challenges such as high tech­ nology barriers, substantial learning costs, and uncertainties in practical application. In this context, organizational support will effectively reduce the economic and time costs of skills acquisition, while also mitigating risk perception arising from unfamiliarity with the technology. It also sends a clear signal that the organization not only encourages the exploration and practice of GAI but also regards it as an important way to enhance organizational competitiveness. The positive institutional environment and resource protection build a solid foundation for the continuous development of GAI literacy. Therefore, we hypothesized the following:

Hypothesis 4. (H4): Organizational support is positively associated with GAI literacy.

Previous research has indicated that higher GAI literacy can increase innovative performance (Ji et al., 2025). For example, employees with high GAI literacy can utilize intelligent tools more efficiently to solve complex problems, resulting in significantly improved task execution efficiency and output quality (Liu et al., 2025a). 5.1.3. The moderating effect of innovation climate Innovation climate refers to individuals' perception that the environment encourages, supports, and rewards innovation (Ma et al., 2021). It serves as a critical contextual signal that influences individuals' decisions to engage in innovative activities. Existing research has indicated that a supportive organizational innovation climate can significantly strengthen employees’ willingness to innovate by providing essential resources and fostering psychological safety (Shanker et al., 2017). In the context of GAI use, GAI literacy is an important foundation for driving innovative behavior (Ji et al., 2025). Nevertheless, even for employees with high GAI literacy, the transformation of such literacy into tangible innovative performance is still subject to contextual constraints. In a climate lacking innovation support, individuals may hesitate to take risks in using GAI for innovation. Hypothesis 7. (H7): Innovation climate positively moderates the effects of GAI literacy on innovative performance.

5.1.4. The moderating effect of job autonomy Job autonomy refers to the degree of control an individual has over organizing their work, making decisions, and choosing how to carry out tasks (Hackman & Oldham, 1976). According to the job demands-resources model, job autonomy serves as a critical job resource that helps employees foster positive outcomes (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017). In the context of GAI use, GAI literacy is an important foundation for enhancing employee well-being (Valtonen et al., 2025). In low-autonomy work contexts, employees are constrained by institutional rules and standardized workflows. Even those with high GAI literacy lack sufficient flexibility to make full use of their GAI capabilities, which weakens the positive effect of GAI literacy on well-being. In contrast, under high job autonomy, individuals with high GAI literacy are empowered to independently determine the timing, methods, and scope of GAI integration into daily work. They can strategically deploy GAI to automate routine tasks, reduce cognitive load, and reallocate energy to meaningful work, thereby strengthening the positive impact of GAI literacy on well-being. Therefore, we hypothesized the following:

Hypothesis 8. (H8): Job autonomy positively moderates the effects of GAI literacy on well-being.

5.2. Data collection and analysis results

5.2.1. Measurement and data collection We used a survey method to conduct this research. To ensure that respondents were employees with experience interacting with GAI, we used three methods to screen respondents: (1) We attached a “Respondent Guide” to the front of the questionnaire to remind respondents that this questionnaire was designed for employees who have used GAI in their work. (2) We designed a logical link between the questions at the beginning of the questionnaire: “Have you ever used GAI in your work?” If the user selected “No,” the subsequent questions on the questionnaire were not displayed on the electronic questionnaire. (3) We asked the platform to screen the respondents for us, and the people who filled out the questionnaire were the working population.

This study used scales that have been verified in previous studies to ensure measurement reliability and validity, as shown in Table A.2 in Appendix A. This study utilized a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “completely disagree” to 7 = “completely agree”). In the first part, we briefly introduced the research purpose and then asked questions related to critical thinking, problem solving, peer influence, organizational support, GAI literacy, and two innovation behaviors. In the second part, we asked about respondents’ demographic characteristics, such as age, gender, education, industry, and use frequency.

We collected 450 valid responses from Credamo.com, one of China's largest professional online survey platforms. We screened the questionnaires based on the following procedures. First, we excluded questionnaires in which responses were overwhelmingly consistent across virtually all questions, which indicated that the respondents may not have filled out the questionnaire carefully. Second, we excluded questionnaires that provided incorrect answers to our trap questions. Finally, we identified the remaining 398 valid respondents. We used G*Power to estimate the minimum sample required (Faul et al., 2007). The sample size of 398 in this study exceeded the minimum of 139 responses required to detect medium-sized effects (f2 = 0.15, α = 0.05, power = 0.80) based on the a priori power analysis. The sample size also satisfied the widely recognized ten-times rule for structural equation modeling.

In terms of age distribution, respondents were mainly concentrated in the 20–29 year old group (47.74%), followed by the 30–39 year old group (43.22%), reflecting that younger people are more likely to be active in the workplace and accept new technologies. In terms of education distribution, the respondents with four-year college degrees dominated (69.10%), which was consistent with the greater technological sensitivity and learning ability of the highly educated group. 5.2.2. Measurement model test In the measurement model examination, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis and then tested the reliability and validity of the scales. Table A.4 in Appendix A presents the scale properties, with Cronbach's α and composite reliability (CR) values above 0.7, indicating good reliability of the scales. The average variance extracted (AVE) values for each factor were higher than the required value of 0.5, and the standardized loadings of the items were above 0.7 and all significant at the 0.001 confidence level, indicating that the scales had good convergent validity. The cross loadings are shown in Table A.5. The loading values on the corresponding factor were higher than 0.7, while the values on other factors were lower than 0.5, indicating that the scales had good discriminant validity. We also compared the AVE values' square root and the items' correlations. The results (Table A.6 in Appendix A) indicated that the square roots of the AVE values were higher than the corresponding correlation coefficients, indicating good discriminant validity. Besides, we examined the values of the heterotrait–monotrait ratio (HTMT). The results (Table A.7 in Appendix A) indicated that the value of HTMT did not exceed the recommended threshold of 0.85, indicating that the scales had good discriminant validity.

To ensure that the dataset was free of common method bias (CMB), we adopted three methods. First, when collecting data, in addition to ensuring the anonymity of respondents, we set one attention trap question to help us screen out questionnaires that were not answered carefully. We then conducted Harman's single factor test. The results indicated that nine factors were extracted, with the first factor accounting for only 12.75% of the variance. Finally, we examined the data using the marker variable method. We selected the item “How concerned are you about your health?” as the marker variable and compared the path coefficients of the model, incorporating this marker variable with those of the base model. The results are shown in Table A.8 in Appendix A. The marker variable exerted no significant effect on GAI literacy, innovation performance, and well-being, with correlations of 0.016, 0.019, and −0.052 respectively, close to zero. Meanwhile, the results of the marker variable model were similar to those of our base model, and the results from all assumptions remained qualitatively unchanged. Thus, CMB was not a significant issue in this study.

5.2.3. Structural model test The results of the main effects are shown in Table 2. The impact of GAI literacy on innovative performance is stronger than that on well-being. Furthermore, we tested the effects of the control variables, and the results revealed that their effects were nonsignificant.

After the main effects were tested, we further tested the moderating effects of innovation climate and job autonomy. As shown in Table 3, the results indicated that innovation climate had a significant positive moderating effect on the relationships between GAI literacy and innovative performance (β = 0.093, p = 0.005). The high innovation climate group exhibits the steepest slope, while the low innovation climate group shows the flattest, indicating that innovation climate strengthens the positive impact of GAI literacy on innovative performance. The results indicated that the impact of GAI literacy on well-being remained consistent regardless of the level of job autonomy.

6. Discussion

6.1. Discussion of the results

With the rapid proliferation of GAI, the core challenge for achieving digital sustainability has shifted from technological acces­ sibility to human capital development, specifically to the systematic enhancement of individual GAI literacy, which is essential for translating technological potential into human sustainability in organizations. This study uses a mixed-methods approach to inves­ tigate the antecedents and human sustainability outcomes of GAI literacy. The qualitative study in the first phase identifies critical antecedents of GAI literacy. The quantitative study in the second phase constructs a research model based on social cognitive theory to empirically examine how these factors influence GAI literacy and its impact on individual well-being and innovative performance, as well as the moderating effects of innovation climate and job autonomy.

The results indicated that critical thinking, problem solving skills, peer influence, and organizational support were positively associated with GAI literacy. At the individual level, critical thinking is positively associated with the ability to critically evaluate AI- generated content, while problem solving skills are associated with the ability to effectively break down complex tasks into instructions that GAI can process. These two competencies represent the essential foundation for mastering and applying GAI. At the environmental level, peers’ demonstrations and exchanges provide individuals with learning references, while organizational support provides the practical conditions for enhancing GAI literacy by providing the necessary tools and training. These factors constitute a supportive environment for developing GAI literacy.

Furthermore, this study shows that GAI literacy is positively associated with individuals' well-being and innovative performance. Moreover, innovation climate positively moderates the relationship between GAI literacy and innovation performance. In an envi­ ronment that encourages innovation, the positive association between GAI literacy and innovative performance is stronger. However, job autonomy did not show a significant moderating effect on the relationship between GAI literacy and well-being. A potential explanation is that GAI literacy is primarily rooted in individuals’ technological mastery and self-efficacy. This internal psychological experience can directly enhance psychological well-being without necessarily relying on external work conditions (such as job au­ tonomy). Consequently, the effect of GAI literacy on well-being may not be moderated by situational factors.

6.2. Theoretical implications

First, this study focuses on individual GAI literacy and human sustainability in organizations, providing a micro and human-centric perspective to the research on organizational digital sustainability. While existing studies have primarily examined the impact of GAI on economic and environmental aspects of organizational sustainability, this study reveals the effects of individual GAI literacy on their well-being and innovative performance, providing a theoretical basis for understanding human sustainability in the digital age. This perspective shifts the focus from what technology can do to what individuals can do with technology to achieve sustainability,

Second, this study expands the application of social cognition theory in the GAI context, constructing and validating a triadic interaction model covering individual, environment, and behavior. Unlike previous digital literacy studies that have focused only on personal cognition, this study empirically reveals that the formation of GAI literacy is a socialization process in which individual factors and environmental factors jointly exert influence by introducing critical thinking, problem solving skills (individual level), peer influence, and organizational support (environmental level) as antecedents. Meanwhile, GAI literacy further affects individuals’ innovative performance and well-being. It not only validates the applicability of social cognitive theory in the emerging GAI domain but also extends the boundaries of the SCT in explaining the formation and outcomes of digital literacy.

Third, this study reveals the boundary conditions of GAI literacy's influence on human sustainability. While existing research has largely explored the moderating role of contextual factors in general terms, this study finds that the innovative climate can positively moderate the relationship between GAI literacy and innovative performance, whereas job autonomy does not significantly moderate the relationship between GAI literacy and well-being. It indicates that different types of contextual factors play distinct roles in the influence of GAI literacy on different outcomes. Enhancing innovation performance requires the support of an external innovation environment, whereas the facilitating effect of GAI literacy on well-being is largely context-independent. These findings provide new insights into the boundary conditions and contextual factors underlying the formation process of sustainability driven by digital literacy.

6.3. Practical implications

Our study highlights the importance of cultivating individual GAI literacy to enhance organizational human sustainability and provides practical guidance for organizational managers and policymakers. First, this study indicates that GAI literacy can promote human sustainability in terms of innovative performance and well-being. With the continuous evolution and rapid popularization of GAI, policymakers should regard the improvement of public GAI literacy as a key strategic initiative. For instance, when evaluating the national or regional level of digital development, a higher weight should be assigned to public digital literacy. Besides, governments or enterprises should not only focus on the construction and investment of technological infrastructure, but also establish special funds for the cultivation of GAI literacy.

Second, this study indicates that developing GAI literacy requires not only individuals’ critical thinking and problem solving skills, but also environmental factors such as peer influence and organizational support. Therefore, organizations and institutions should implement training interventions to strengthen individuals' critical thinking, problem-solving skills, and GAI literacy. For example, policymakers could develop systematic and hierarchical GAI literacy curricula and incorporate them into the local education systems. It is also crucial to foster a social environment conducive to the cultivation of public GAI literacy. Governments could launch public GAI learning programs, offer GAI competency certification, and hold online GAI interaction communities to provide external support for the improvement of individual GAI literacy.

Third, this study indicates that a positive innovation climate can enhance the impact of individual GAI literacy on innovative performance. Therefore, managers and policymakers should create a supportive innovation environment to maximize the benefits of GAI literacy. Specifically, it is crucial to build an environment that encourages experimentation, tolerates failure, and facilitates the flow of resources and knowledge. This requires policy promotion across multiple dimensions. Culturally, policymakers should promote an open, collaborative, and risk-tolerant spirit. Institutionally, they could strengthen intellectual property protection, establish innovation coordination mechanisms, and provide more subsidies, rewards, or tax incentives to innovative enterprises and individuals. These measures will ensure that individual digital capabilities can be translated into practical innovation and sustainability performance.

6.4. Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations and offers directions for future research. First, despite using statistical tests to examine common method bias, self-reported innovative performance may still be subject to social desirability effects. Future research could adopt multisource assessments (e.g., supervisor evaluations) or track actual workplace implementations. Second, this study employed a crosssectional survey method, which may limit the ability to draw causal inferences. Future research could employ longitudinal designs (e.g., time-lagged survey or random intercept cross-lagged model) or quasi-experimental designs to examine the causal relationship between GAI literacy and human sustainability. Third, the theoretical model of this study could be further expanded. For example, according to Wang and Sun (2025), technological and individual characteristics like system convenience and user familiarity may also influence individuals' attitudes and behaviors towards AI. Therefore, future research could explore whether these factors play a role in individuals' GAI literacy and its sustainability consequences. Similarly, to reveal more boundary conditions, future research could also further explore the moderating effects of factors such as organizational AI strategy on GAI literacy and GAI-driven sustainability (Wang & Zhang, 2025c).
