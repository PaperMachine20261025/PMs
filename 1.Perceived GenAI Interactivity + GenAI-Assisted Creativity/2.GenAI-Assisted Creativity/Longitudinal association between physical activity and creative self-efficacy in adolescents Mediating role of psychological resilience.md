Longitudinal association between physical activity and creative self-efficacy in adolescents: Mediating role of psychological resilience

A B S T R A C T

Keywords: Creative self-efficacy Physical activity Psychological resilience Longitudinal mediation Random intercept cross-lagged panel modeling (RI-CLPM)

Creative self-efficacy (CSE), an individual’s belief in their ability to produce creative outcomes, is fundamental to adolescents’ creative development. Embodied cognition theory and current empirical evidence link physical activity and creativity. Yet, the longitudinal association between physical activity and CSE remains poorly understood. This study investigated the longitudinal association between physical activity and CSE in adolescents, with a particular focus on the mediating role of psychological resilience. A total of 751 Chinese adolescents (48.20 % boys, Mage=16.27) completed self-report questionnaires on physical activity, psychological resilience, and creative self-efficacy at three time points over 12 months. Using both cross-lagged panel modeling (CLPM) and random intercept cross-lagged panel modeling (RI-CLPM), we differenti­ ated between-person from within-person effects. Results showed that physical activity was significantly associated with CSE over time. Notably, at the within-person level, psychological resilience was a significant mediator, with sensitivity analyses demonstrating the relative robustness of the mediation effect. These findings not only advance embodied cognition and stress adaptation theories by illuminating a time dynamic mechanism among physical activity, psy­ chological resilience, and creative self-beliefs, but also highlight the potential practical implica­ tions of promoting physical activity and resilience-building programs to foster adolescents’ creative self-beliefs. Finally, the limitations and future directions of this study are discussed.

1. Introduction

Creativity has long been recognized as a crucial competency, with early frameworks established by Guilford (1950). As the de­ mands of the 21st century continue to evolve, creativity remains indispensable for individual and societal advancement (Parkhust, 1999). In this context, fostering students’ creativity is an essential goal of education and is greatly emphasized (Bereczki & K´arp´ati, 2018). Creative thinking is vital for addressing challenges and dilemmas (Segundo-Marcos et al., 2023). Creative self-efficacy—defined

Creative self-efficacy is grounded in Bandura’s (1977) social cognitive theory, which posits that self-efficacy beliefs fundamentally influence individuals’ motivation, perseverance, and performance across various domains. Building on this framework, Tierney and Farmer (2002) conceptualized creative self-efficacy as the confidence that individuals hold regarding their capacity to engage in creative processes and produce original and useful outcomes. A substantial body of research has demonstrated a robust association between creative self-efficacy and students’ creative thinking and skills (Beghetto et al., 2011; Jaiswal & Dhar, 2016; Puente-Díaz & Cavazos-Arroyo, 2017). Specifically, individuals’ creative self-efficacy has been shown to positively predict creative output (Gu et al., 2015); a meta-analysis revealed its significant correlation with both verbal and figural creative performance (Haase et al., 2018). Individuals with higher creative self-efficacy tend to have more positive beliefs about their abilities in all subject areas (Beghetto, 2006), and are more inclined to pursue challenges (Yüce et al., 2023). Given these findings, identifying the predictors and contextual factors that contribute to creative self-efficacy, particularly during adolescence, a formative period of cognitive, emotional, and social development, is essential for designing educational practices that effectively nurture students’ creativity.

1.1. Theoretical framework

Embodied cognition theory posits a close link between body and mind, suggesting that cognitive processes are deeply rooted in physical experiences (Wilson, 2002). This perspective emphasizes the role of bodily actions, such as gestures, object manipulation, and whole-body movement, in shaping cognitive functions, including attention, problem solving, and thinking style (Song et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2025). From this perspective, cognitive functions, including creative thinking and problem solving, are not isolated from bodily experiences but are fundamentally rooted in sensorimotor activities. Notably, the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) has recognized embodied learning as a promising pedagogical approach, and recent research has highlighted the effectiveness of movement-based performing arts in fostering creative learning (Cappello & Ortiz, 2025).

A growing body of empirical evidence echoes claims of the embodied cognition framework. The literature has demonstrated that physical activity enhances various cognitive outcomes, including general cognition (Ellemberg & St-Louis-Deschˆenes, 2010; Este­ ban-Cornejo et al., 2015; Ratey & Loehr, 2011), executive functioning (Xue et al., 2019), and creative thinking (Frith et al., 2019a, 2019b). Intervention studies have further revealed that engaging in physical activity can significantly boost students’ creative per­ formance (Rodríguez-Negro et al., 2024). Evidence from neuroscience also supports this link; for example, in tasks that require physically breaking a wall, participants had better creative performance and triggered neural activity related to rule breaking (Wang et al., 2019). A systematic review of neuroimaging research has implicated the motor system in generating creative thoughts (Matheson & Kenett, 2020), suggesting that the motor system could support higher-order cognitive goals, such as generating creative ideas. Although a few studies reported null results (Donnegan et al., 2018; Frith & Loprinzi, 2018), the overall body of theoretical and empirical evidence supports the positive association between physical activity and creative cognition.

1.2. Physical activity and creative self-efficacy

Physical activity is widely recognized for its benefits to both physical (Lear et al., 2017) and mental health (Cabanas-S´anchez et al., 2022; Zuckerman et al., 2021). However, its relationship with creative self-efficacy—individuals’ beliefs about their ability to produce creative outcomes—remains under-explored. Although creative self-efficacy is a critical factor in sustained creative engagement and resilience in the face of challenges, relatively little research has examined the link between physical activity and this self-belief.

Emerging evidence suggests that physical activity facilitates creativity either through task-related performance or self-evaluated creativity (Jung et al., 2021; Piya-Amornphan et al., 2020; Colzato et al., 2013); it may thereby support creative self-efficacy. Neu­ roimaging studies further support this perspective, showing that engaging in physical movement not only activates motor regions, but also stimulates brain networks involved in creative ideation and self-referential thinking (Matheson & Kenett, 2020; Wang et al., 2019). However, most existing literature has focused on creative performance or divergent thinking, with far less attention being paid to creative self-efficacy as an outcome.

Moreover, research to date, particularly surveys of adolescents, has been largely cross-sectional, providing only static snapshots of the association between physical activity and creativity. However, the longitudinal dynamics linking physical activity and creative self-efficacy remain insufficiently explored. For instance, correlations have been observed between physical activity and various facets of creative thinking, such as fluency, originality, and flexibility, using cross-sectional and correlational methods (Romance et al., 2023), as well as drawing-based creativity scores in students (Piya-Amornphan et al., 2020). Although informative, these findings do not capture the dynamic developmental trajectory of creative self-efficacy over time. Similarly, most intervention studies have assessed only short-term gains in creative performance, rather than long-term changes in underlying self-beliefs. Given that creative self-efficacy is subject to change across developmental stages (Segundo-Marcos et al., 2023), there is a pressing need for longitudinal research to explore how physical activity is associated with creative self-efficacy.

To address this gap, this study aimed to explore the longitudinal relationship between physical activity and creative self-efficacy among adolescents. By adopting a longitudinal design, this study seeks to provide insights into the dynamic relationship between physical activity and creative self-efficacy, offering valuable implications for educational practices aimed at fostering creativity among the youth.

Psychological resilience, defined as the ability to recover from difficult situations (Connor & Davidson, 2003), is understood as an outcome of the human adaptive system (Masten, 2001). It enables individuals to maintain or regain psychological well-being in the face of stress and has been consistently associated with better mental health and life satisfaction (Chuning et al., 2024; Ong et al., 2006). Does physical activity correlate with psychological resilience? Salmon (2001) proposed that, beyond its physical health benefits, physical activity could facilitate mental health, including antidepressant and anxiolytic effects. In particular, he claimed that exercise training recruits adaptive processes that confer resilience to stress (Salmon, 2001). Empirical studies have further demonstrated that participation in sports is associated with higher levels of psychological resilience and stress management compared to non-participation (Yildirim Gurkan et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). Additional interventional findings indicate that physical activity contributes to stress regulation and resilience building in educational and medical contexts (Hall et al., 2016; Toth et al., 2024). Neurobiological findings corroborate this relationship, suggesting that physical activity promotes neuroplasticity, which strengthens stress tolerance and adaptive functioning (Greenwood & Fleshner, 2011). Stress adaptation theory also emphasizes that regular physical exercise enhances an individual’s ability to adapt to stress, increases stress tolerance, and therefore strengthens resilience (Salmon, 2001). Collectively, this empirical evidence and theoretical framework underscore the positive link between physical activity and psychological resilience.

Although direct evidence linking psychological resilience and creative self-efficacy is limited, the existing research supports their conceptual and empirical convergence. Empirically, resilience has been positively associated with self-reported creativity among university students (Lei & Lei, 2023), and even teachers’ psychological capital—a construct closely related to resilience (Xu, 2024). Additionally, creative thinking training can enhance the resilience of elementary school students (Fletcher et al., 2023). Theoretically, psychological resilience and creative self-efficacy share conceptual similarities and reflect adaptive psychological resources; resilience supports coping with adversity, while creative self-efficacy underpins persistence and originality in problem solving. Specifically, psychological resilience, recognized as a psychological resource for coping with challenges (Stover et al., 2024), partially overlaps with the definition of creative problem solving, which also requires individuals to deal with problems originally, appropriately, and usefully (Van Hooijdonk et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2022), and has been found to be strongly correlated with creative self-efficacy (Stolz et al., 2022). Given the empirical evidence and conceptual overlap, particularly in coping with adversity and persistent problem solving, it is plausible to expect that higher levels of resilience are associated with stronger creative self-efficacy.

Taken together, this evidence suggests that psychological resilience may mediate the relationship between physical activity and creative self-efficacy. Therefore, this study aimed to examine not only the longitudinal association between physical activity and creative self-efficacy, but also the mediating role of psychological resilience in this relationship over time.

1.4. Between and within person effects

While existing research supports the exploration of the present hypothesis, short-term designs have limited their ability to capture the dynamic and developmental nature of the relationships among physical activity, resilience, and creative self-efficacy. The reliance on cross-sectional designs in prior studies restricts the ability to draw inferences regarding the temporal sequence of the relationships among physical activity, psychological resilience, and creative self-efficacy. Therefore, it is essential to employ a longitudinal design to capture developmental trajectories and establish cross-lagged associations over time.

Recently, advanced methods of data analysis have emerged, such as the random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM), which offers more nuanced insights into the dynamics between variables (Hamaker et al., 2015), can distinguish between-person effects (i.e., stable differences across individuals) and within-person effects (i.e., fluctuations within an individual over time). This distinction is critical for understanding how physical activity and resilience relate to creative self-efficacy across both trait-level tendencies and time-varying processes. This method has gained traction in both psychological (Archambault et al., 2024; Cook et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024) and psychiatric (Best & Cosco, 2022; Smarason et al., 2024) research for disentangling the contributions of individualand group-level variations.

Specifically, the between-person effect represents the overall average difference across individuals over time, such as whether students who generally engage in more physical activity have higher levels of creative self-efficacy than those who engage in less or no physical activity. Conversely, the within-person effect assesses how changes in a student’s physical activity level relate to changes in their creative self-efficacy over time (Chen et al., 2023). For instance, it examines whether individuals’ creative self-efficacy increases over time as their physical activity level increases. By partitioning these two sources of variance, the RI-CLPM offers a more nuanced and accurate understanding of the dynamic associations among physical activity, resilience, and creative self-efficacy over time.

1.5. The present study

Building on prior research and addressing the current gaps, this study employed a three-wave design with six-month intervals to systematically investigate the relationship between physical activity and creative self-efficacy in adolescents, with a particular focus on the mediating role of psychological resilience. In the first stage, we used a standard cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) to evaluate preliminary associations across the sample. Subsequently, to further capture the dynamic association at the individual level, we applied the RI-CLPM to disentangle between-person and within-person effects over time. 2. Method

2.1. Participants

The study involved first-grade high school students from a city in Anhui Province, China. Data collection occurred in three waves: May 2023 (Time 1, T1), November 2023 (Time 2, T2), and May 2024 (Time 3, T3), with six-month intervals between each assessment. Initially, 984 adolescents participated at T1; 929 continued at T2, and 751 at T3 (Mean age = 16.27, SD = 0.55; age range = 15 to 18 years; 362 males and 389 females). With reference to previous studies (Best & Cosco, 2022; Wang et al., 2024a), only participants who completed the questionnaire in all three waves (T1, T2 and T3) were included in the main analysis. Attrition was due to absences on assessment days and students transferring to other schools. Adolescents in this age group possess are capable of reading comprehension and sufficient completing the questionnaires. The study procedures and consent forms were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Normal University (Num: 2024,044).

2.2. Measures

Physical activity was assessed using two questions: (I) the frequency of physical activity per week, with responses scored as “1″ for “1 time or less” “2″ for “2–3 times” “3″ for “4–5 times” and “4″ for “6 times or more”; and (II) the duration of each physical activity session, scored as “1″ for “0–20 min” “2″ for “20–40 min” “3″ for “40–60 min” and “4″ for “60 min or more”. The physical activity score was calculated as the average of the two objective items. These two questions directly represent the intensity level and time dedication of physical activity and have been used as measures of physical activity in previous studies (Romance et al., 2023).

Psychological resilience was measured using the Chinese Version of Brief Resilience Scale, which was contended (Smith et al., 2008) and validated to have reliable reliability and validity in Chinese student populations (Wei et al., 2020), The scale comprises 6 items (e. g., "I tend to bounce back quickly after hard times") and uses the following response options: 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the scale were 0.73, 0.82, and 0.80 at Time 1, Time 2, and Time 3, respectively.

Creative self-efficacy was measured with reference to Beghetto’s study, which draws on prior research into creative self-efficacy and definitions of creativity (Beghetto, 2006). The scale included three items: (a) “I am good at coming up with new ideas” (b) “I have a lot of good ideas” and (c) “I have a good imagination”. Responses were rated on a 5-point scale, with “1″ indicating “strongly disagree” and “5″ indicating “strongly agree”. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the scale were 0.80, 0.88, and 0.98 at Time 1, Time 2, and Time 3, respectively.

Covariates in the study included gender (1 = male, 2 = female), age, and socio-economic status (SES). SES was assessed using two components: the educational levels of parents and household income. Parental education was measured with two questions regarding the highest educational attainment of fathers and mothers, rated from 1 (middle school and below) to 4 (master’s degree and above). Household income was assessed with four categories: 1 (<5000 yuan), 2 (5000–10,000 yuan), 3 (10,000–20,000 yuan), and 4 (>20,000 yuan). The responses were standardized and summed to create a composite measure of socio-economic status for the study, a common practice in similar studies (Chen et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2021).

2.3. Procedures

The study was approved by the principle, teachers, students, and their parents of the sample school. With parental consent, students voluntarily signed a consent form outlining the voluntary and confidential nature of the study, including their right to withdraw at any time. Trained research assistants encouraged participants to provide honest responses and ensured they had adequate time to complete the questionnaires. To protect privacy, students completed the questionnaires without the presence of teachers. Each participant was assigned a unique code to ensure confidentiality and facilitate data matching.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were conducted to summarize demographic characteristics, with means and standard deviations reported for continuous variables. To ensure the longitudinal comparability of our self-reported measures, we tested measurement invariance over the three waves for both subjective variables, i.e., psychological resilience and creative self-efficacy. We estimated confirmatory factor models—configural, metric (weak), and scalar (strong) invariance—evaluating changes in fit indices (ΔCFI, ΔSRMR, and ΔRMSEA). For creative self-efficacy, fit indices across invariance models were well (CFI = 0.990–0.999; SRMR = 0.011–0.026; RMSEA = 0.005–0.053), and none of the ΔCFI or ΔSRMR values exceeded 0.015 at the scalar level, indicating that strong invariance held satisfactorily. Psychological resilience also demonstrated acceptable longitudinal fit (CFI = 0.900–0.910; SRMR = 0.051–0.059; RMSEA = 0.059–0.062), with all ΔCFI, ΔSRMR, and ΔRMSEA changes below 0.01 for both metric and scalar models—supporting well

To explore the longitudinal relationship between physical activity and creative self-efficacy, we first constructed cross-lagged panel models (CLPM). To further disentangle intra-individual (within-person) and inter-individual (between-person) effects, a random intercept cross-lagged panel model was established (RI-CLPM). We controlled for gender, age, and socio-economic status (SES) as covariates in each model, following established approaches (Guo et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2023), and maximum likelihood robust estimator (MLR) was adopted to achieve more accurate estimates (Joshanloo, 2024).

Then, we incorporated psychological resilience into the models as a potential mediator to formally test the hypothesized indirect effect. Bootstrapping analysis, using 5000 samples, was employed to assess the significance and strength of the mediating role of psychological resilience. Additionally, we used multiple group models to evaluate the invariance of the RI-CLPM across genders. Models were considered acceptable if the CFI was above 0.90, and if the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) were below 0.08 (Marsh et al., 2004). Analyses for both CLPM and RI-CLPM were conducted using Mplus Version 8.3 (Muth´en & Muth´en, 2019).

Furthermore, a simulation-based sensitivity analysis was performed using R software (version 4.5.0), following the methodology of Qin and Yang (2022). The unmeasured confounder (U) was simulated from its conditional distribution, with the unconditional probability set at p = .5. Sensitivity parameters were specified along two dimensions, representing the strength of association between U and both the mediator (βm

u ) and the outcome (βy

u), each varying from –1 to 1 in accordance with prior research (Carnegie et al., 2016). The natural indirect effect (NIE) and natural direct effect (NDE) were then estimated for each combination of sensitivity pa­ rameters according to the R package (Qin & Yang, 2022).

3.1. Descriptions

3.2. Physical activity and creative self-efficacy

A longitudinal CLPM model (Model 1) was initially constructed to explore the relationship between physical activity and creative self-efficacy. The model demonstrated a moderate fit, with CFI =0.92, TLI = 0.83, RMSEA = 0.11, and SRMR = 0.05. Fig. 1 illustrated the standardized results for physical activity (PA) and creative self-efficacy (CSE) across people over time. Specifically, PA at T1 positively predicted CSE at T2 (β = 0.08, p < .05), and PA at T2 also positively predicted CSE at T3 (β = 0.15, p < .001), which was consistent with our expectations. In addition, we found that increasement in CSE at the T2 was associated with an increase in PA at the T3 (β = 0.11, p < .001), while CSE at T1 did not significantly predict PA at T2 (β = 0.03, p = .27).

3.3. Between/Within-person dynamics of physical activity and creative self-efficacy

The RI-CLPM model (Model 2) was constructed to distinguish the between-person and within-person effects in the relationship between physical activity and creative self-efficacy. The model showed a strong fit, with CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, RMSEA = 0.03, and SRMR = 0.01. The standardized results are presented in Fig. 2. At the between-person level, the random intercept of PA was positively associated with the random intercept of CSE (r = 0.15, p < .001). However, after accounting for between-person effects, no significant associations were found between PA and subsequent CSE at the within-person level.

3.4. The mediating role of psychological resilience

We developed an RI-CLPM model (Model 3) with psychological resilience as a mediator. The model provided an acceptable fit to the data (CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.98, RMSEA = 0.03, SRMR = 0.02) and the standardized results were shown in Fig. 3. Specifically, the random intercepts for PA, psychological resilience (PR), and CSE were significantly correlated, with correlations ranging from 0.35 to 0.46 (ps < 0.001). Turning to the within-individual level, PA at T1 positively predicted PR at T2 (β = 0.16, p = .05), and PR at T2 positively predicted CSE at T3 (β = 0.23, p < .001). These results indicated that PA at T1 predicted CSE at T3 through PR at T2 (indirect effect = 0.04, 95 %CI = [.003, 0.089]). Additionally, multiple group comparisons revealed that the RI-CLPM model did not show significant differences between genders.

4. Discussion

This longitudinal study aimed to clarify the dynamic relationships among physical activity, psychological resilience, and creative self-efficacy in adolescents. By employing a three-wave design and panel modeling approach, this study provides novel insights into the temporal associations and potential mechanisms linking these constructs. Specifically, the findings revealed that adolescents who reported higher levels of physical activity also exhibited greater creative self-efficacy over time at the between-person level, as shown by both the CLPM and RI-CLPM analyses. Notably, at the within-person level, psychological resilience was identified as a significant mediating factor as increases in physical activity in an individual over time predicted subsequent increases in resilience, which, in turn, were associated with higher levels of creative self-efficacy.

Taken together, these findings have prac­ tical implications for educators and policymakers who seek to design holistic interventions that integrate physical activity and resilience-building strategies to cultivate students’ creative self-efficacy.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly explore the longitudinal relationship between physical activity and creative selfefficacy among adolescents, providing new empirical evidence that bridges a notable gap in creativity literature. By leveraging both the CLPM and RI-CLPM analytical approaches, we found that physical activity was robustly associated with creative self-efficacy at the between-person level; that is, adolescents who habitually engaged in more physical activity over the study period consistently reported higher levels of creative self-efficacy.

Notably, the outcomes of the current research not only align with earlier experimental evidence that indicate physical activi­ ty—even simple activities like squeezing a ball (Kim, 2015)—benefit creative thinking (Bollimbala et al., 2023; Frith & Loprinzi, 2018; Rom´an et al., 2018; Tastanova et al., 2024), but also add to this body of knowledge by showing that the positive relationship of physical activity is not confined to performance-based measures of creativity; rather, it extends to creative self-efficacy—the belief in one’s ability to produce creative outcomes (Beghetto, 2006; Tierney & Farmer, 2002) —which strongly associated with creative behavior (Beghetto et al., 2011; Jaiswal & Dhar, 2016). In this sense, our findings broaden the scope of the cognitive benefits of physical activity by demonstrating the correlation between physical activity and self-beliefs that underpin creative engagement and perseverance.

From a theoretical standpoint, these findings offer novel support for embodied cognition frameworks (Leung et al., 2012; Murali & Handel, 2022; Wilson, 2002), that propose that cognitive processes, including creative ideation, are grounded in sensorimotor activity. Because previous experimental context neuroimaging studies have revealed the effect of embodied metaphors on creative task per­ formance (Wang et al., 2019) and highlighted the role of the motor system in creative thinking (Matheson & Kenett, 2020), the present findings add ecological validity to this body of work by highlighting that sustained real-world physical activity, not merely short-term experimental interventions, may play a foundational role in shaping creative self-perceptions.

4.2. The mediating role of psychological resilience

Using a random intercept cross-lagged model (RI-CLPM), this study provides compelling evidence that psychological resilience mediates the within-person relationship between physical activity and creative self-efficacy. Specifically, when an adolescent reported higher physical activity than at the previous time point, this was followed by increased psychological resilience at the following time point, which, in turn, predicted elevated levels of creative self-efficacy. This aligns with, and significantly extends, the growing body of research highlighting the positive association between physical activity and psychological resilience (Kuchar et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Yildirim Gurkan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2022), as well as the significant link between psychological resilience and various aspects of creative functioning, including creative thinking and problem solving (Fletcher et al., 2023; Lei & Lei, 2023). This finding offers longitudinal evidence for a pathway that has been inferred, but has rarely been empirically validated in previous literature.

Theoretically, these findings are consistent with and could be explained by psychological capital (Luthans & Youssef-Morgan, 2017) and stress adaptation theory (Salmon, 2001), both of which posit that regular engagement in physical activity fosters not only physical health but also psychological resources, such as resilience, optimism, and hope. These psychological resources, in turn, equip individuals with the capacity to navigate adversity, persist in the face of setbacks, and approach novel and challenging problems flexibly. Supporting this view, previous studies have shown that physical activity promotes overall physical and mental well-being (Inoue et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Werneck et al., 2021), which provides individuals with robust psychological capital to navigate challenges (Luthans & Youssef-Morgan, 2017), strengthening psychological resilience. Further, elevated psychological resilience equips individuals with persistence and adaptive coping skills (Stover et al., 2024), encouraging the exploration of novel approaches to problem solving (Ong et al., 2006), ultimately leading to increased creative self-efficacy.

Simulation-based sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the mediation results, indicating that the observed indirect ef­ fects were unlikely to be easily overturned by unmeasured confounding factors, lending additional confidence to the validity of our findings. While it is important to acknowledge that other factors, such as motivation, enjoyment (Jung et al., 2021), and well-being (Inoue et al., 2024), may also be associated with both physical activity and creative self-efficacy, the integration of the theoretical framework, empirical evidence, and rigorous analysis supports the plausibility of psychological resilience as a significant mediator in this developmental process.

These results underscore the need to broaden our understanding of creativity and highlight the importance of considering physical activity and psychological resources as foundational contributors to creative self-belief. By acknowledging and leveraging the interplay among physical, psychological, and creative development, educators and policymakers can design more holistic and impactful pro­ grams that empower students to thrive in complex and dynamic environments.

4.3. Limitations and future directions

Despite the methodological rigor of the present study in examining longitudinal associations and clearly distinguishing withinand between-person effects, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the longitudinal design provides stronger evidence for temporal ordering than cross-sectional studies, the findings remain correlational and cannot establish causality. Future research should employ experimental designs to rigorously test the causal pathways between physical activity, psychological resilience, and creative self-efficacy.

Second, all variables were assessed through self-report, a common approach in adolescent research (Chen et al., 2025), which carries potential biases such as social desirability effects and recall inaccuracies that might inflate or distort the observed associations among physical activity, resilience, and creative self-efficacy. Although our findings support psychological resilience as a plausible mediator based on theoretical reasoning, empirical evidence, and simulation-based sensitivity analyses, it may not be the sole mediating mechanism. Other unmeasured factors, such as motiva­ tional factors, prior creative ability, and general self-efficacy, may also play roles in shaping creative self-belief. Future research should explore more comprehensive models incorporating multiple mediators and moderators. By acknowledging these limitations, we highlight critical directions for future research, ultimately contributing to a deeper and more integrative understanding of how physical activity relates to creative self-belief in adolescent development.

4.4. Practical implications

The findings of the present study have several important practical implications. Traditional efforts to enhance creativity in educational contexts have predominantly focused on cognitive training and classroom-based instructions (Jin et al., 2024; Scott et al., 2004; Sio & Lortie-Forgues, 2024). Although valuable, such approaches may be constrained by artificial learning contexts and have limited integration into students’ lived experiences. In contrast, physical activity offers a more holistic, embodied, and accessible intervention capable of simultaneously promoting physical health (Lear et al., 2017; van Sluijs et al., 2021), psychological well-being (Werneck et al., 2021; Zhang & Chen, 2019), and creative potential (Bollimbala et al., 2023; Frith & Loprinzi, 2018; Luo et al., 2023; Piya-Amornphan et al., 2020). Therefore, integrating movement-based activities into school curricula may be a promising strategy to nurture creative self-efficacy and even creativity.

Practically, identifying psychological resilience as a mediator has significant implications for educational and youth development initiatives. Interventions targeting both physical activity and psychological resilience, such as structured exercise programs, teambased tasks, and resilience-building curricula, may generate synergistic benefits, thereby advancing students’ physical health, adap­ tive functioning, and creative potential. For instance, schools could incorporate movement-rich activities that involve cooperation, persistence, and problem solving, thereby fostering both resilience and confidence in students’ creative capacities.

Ultimately, these findings advocate a shift from narrowly cognitive models of creativity enhancement to more whole-child ap­ proaches in which physical, emotional, and psychological development are seen as interconnected foundations for creative growth.

5. Conclusions

This longitudinal study provides novel empirical insights into the association between physical activity and adolescents’ creative self-efficacy, highlighting psychological resilience as a mediator at the within-person level. Specifically, our findings indicate that higher physical activity is positively associated with increased creative self-efficacy through psychological resilience at the withinperson level. These findings support embodied cognition and stress adaptation theory. Educational programs that incorporate movement-based practices and promote adaptive coping may offer a holistic pathway for supporting adolescents’ creative develop­ ment and adaptive functioning.
