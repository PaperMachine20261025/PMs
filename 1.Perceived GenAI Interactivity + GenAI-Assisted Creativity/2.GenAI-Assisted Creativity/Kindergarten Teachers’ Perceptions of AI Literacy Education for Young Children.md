Kindergarten Teachers’ Perceptions of AI Literacy Education for Young Children

Jiahong Su1

Accepted: 3 January 2024 / Published online: 15 March 2024 © The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature B.V. 2024

Abstract Educators and researchers have increasingly recognized the importance of AI literacy. Despite the growing body of AI literacy literature, the challenges and enablers for introducing AI literacy for kindergarten children is still underexplored and undertheorized in early childhood education. It is critical to understand the challenges and enablers associated with promoting AI literacy and perceptions in kindergartens needs to be further researched and evaluated. This study aims to identify teachers’ views on the importance of AI literacy in kindergartens and the challenges and enablers of promoting AI literacy in kindergartens. Data was collected from 15 teachers through individual interviews. It was found that the four main challenges of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms, including lack of school support, lack of children’s comprehensive ability, insufficient teacher knowledge of AI, and lack of curriculum guidelines. The three main enablers of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms are government support, school support, and social needs. We also found that more than half of teachers in this study considered AI literacy education is crucial for kindergarten children. This study develops an AI literacy policy framework for young children that includes three dimensions—governance, pedagogical, and operational and management. Based on the findings, suggestions for educators, curriculum developers, policymakers, and researchers are discussed.

Keywords  AI literacy · Teachers’ perceptions · Challenges · Enablers · Kindergarten classrooms · Early childhood education · AI literacy policy framework

Introduction

AI literacy education is essential to economic growth, future workforce development, and global competitiveness (Druga et al., 2019). According to Long and Magerko (2020), AI literacy is a set of competencies that are “necessary in a future in which AI transforms the way that we communicate, work, and live with each other and with machines” (p. 1). Introducing kindergarten children to the concept of AI can be approached in a simplified and age-appropriate content (Su & Zhong, 2022). Understanding the challenges and enablers of promoting AI literacy is crucial as it facilitates its implementation in kindergartens. RQ1: What are the main challenges of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms? RQ2: What are the main enablers of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms? RQ3: How do kindergarten teachers view the importance of AI literacy in kindergartens?

Literature review

The framework of AI literacy

AI literacy is becoming increasingly important as artificial intelligence plays a larger role in our society and throughout various industries. Recently, several studies developed an AI literacy framework in education (e.g., Kong et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2021; Wong et al., 2019). These frameworks aim to equip students with the knowledge and skills necessary to navigate an AI-driven world effectively. Kong et al. (2023) designed an AI literacy framework, including cognitive, affective, and sociocultural. One study developed an AI literacy framework that includes three dimensions: AI concepts, AI applications, and AI ethics/ safety to assess students’ AI literacy (Wong et al., 2019). Ng et al. (2021) developed an AI literacy framework that includes four dimensions: (1) understanding and knowledge of AI; (2) AI application and usage; (3) AI evaluation and creation; and (4) AI ethics. These dimensions offer a comprehensive framework that supports the development of AI literacy and enables the integration of AI into early childhood education. These three frameworks are designed to help educators and students better understand AI, its applications, and its implications.

AI literacy in early childhood education

Su et al. (2023) systematically reviewed K-12 teaching tools, teaching methods, and critical content to help educators to integrate artificial intelligence into the classroom effectively. This study found that the focus of AI literacy varies greatly across different educational levels. AI literacy in primary, secondary, and higher education mainly focuses on complex

concepts (e.g., Chiu et al., 2021; Su et al., 2022) and training a machine learning model (Toivonen et al., 2020). However, AI literacy in early childhood education mainly focuses on basic AI concepts/knowledge (Williams et al., 2019a) and simple AI activities (e.g., Quick, Draw! and Teachable machine) (Su & Yang, 2023b; Su & Zhong, 2022; Su et al., 2023a; Vartiainen et al., 2020). Su and Yang (2023c) redeveloped the five big ideas framework for young children, including perception, representation and reasoning, learning, natural interaction, and societal impact. While some AI curricula have been outlined in some studies (e.g., Su & Yang, 2023c; Su & Zhong, 2022), they still need to be implemented.

To improve kindergarten children’s understanding of AI, several scholars designed AI curricula for kindergarten children. For example, Kandlhofer et al. (2016) adopted the discoveryand inquiry-based learning approach and designed storytelling activities to teach children basic AI concepts. It was found that kindergarten children can discover and understand the fundamentals and major concepts of AI through completing inquiry activities. Moreover, Williams (2018) designed learning activities such as drawing AI robots and sorting algorithms to help kindergarten children grasp AI concepts. It has been demonstrated that AI literacy yields numerous benefits. Therefore, developing AI literacy in kindergartens is a worthwhile -research area.

Opportunities of implementing AI literacy in early childhood education

There are many benefits for kindergarten children to learn AI. To begin with, some research studies found that AI literacy programs facilitated kindergarten children’s understanding of basic AI concepts (Su et al., 2023; Williams et al., 2019a). Williams et al. (2019a) used PopBots assessment to assess children’s AI literacy. Results showed that kindergarten children can understand three AI concepts after the AI curriculum: knowledge-based systems, supervised machine learning, and generative AI. Su and Yang (2023a) used knowledge assessment to assess kindergarten children’s AI literacy. Results found that kindergarten children can understand basic AI concepts. To sum up, kindergarten children can understand basic AI concepts and should learn from kindergarten (Preface, 2021).

In addition to enhancing children’s basic AI knowledge, two studies found that children learning AI can enhance their three inquiry skills (i.e., creative inquiry, emotional inquiry, and collaborative inquiry) (Kewalramani et al., 2021) and creativity skills (Su & Yang, 2023a). For example, regarding collaborative inquiry, “the children explored the AI toys, and shared and reflected on previous experiences, making new suggestions about how to use the toys” (Kewalramani et al., 2021, p. 662). Data was collected through video observation. Results show that kindergarten children interacting with AI robots performed better in collaborative inquiry. Although some studies found that AI literacy enhanced kindergarten children’s AI knowledge and skills, studies discussing the challenges and enablers of AI literacy in kindergartens are absent.

Challenges of implementing AI literacy in early childhood education

Some challenges to introducing AI literacy into early childhood education include teachers’ lack of AI knowledge and skills (Su et al., 2023) and curriculum design (Su & Zhong, 2022). The first major challenge is teachers’ lack of AI knowledge and skills (Su et al., 2023). Su et al. (2023), in their review paper, discuss the challenges of implementing AI literacy in early childhood education, including teachers insufficient AI knowledge and lack

of confidence to teach. It is noteworthy that teachers play a primary role in transmitting knowledge and that a lack of expertise in AI literacy could lead to misunderstandings about AI being developed among students (Abramowitz & Antonenko, 2022). Thus, teacher training is necessary to empower teachers to use AI literacy to ensure teaching quality.

The second challenge is the lack of curriculum design regarding challenges of implementing AI literacy in early childhood education (Su & Zhong, 2022). Nowadays, many AI literacy frameworks only focus on primary, secondary, and higher education (e.g., Ng et al., 2021; Wong et al., 2019; Kong et al., 2023). It seems that AI literacy frameworks for young children are limited. Although some review and discussion articles have already outlined some challenges in introducing AI literacy into early childhood education (Su & Zhong, 2022; Su et al., 2023). However, limited empirical research focuses on the challenges of implementing AI literacy in kindergartens. Therefore, this study will address the gap in the challenges of implementing AI literacy in kindergarten by teacher interviews.

Research gaps in the literature

Most AI literacy research focused on teaching and learning AI in K-12 contexts (e.g., Lin & Van Brummelen, 2020; Williams et al., 2019). However, few studies centered on the challenges and enablers of AI literacy in kindergartens and teachers’ perceptions of AI literacy in kindergartens. To fill this gap, individual interviews are used in this study to examine the difficulties and facilitators of promoting AI literacy in kindergartens and understand teachers’ insights concerning the importance of AI literacy.

Methods

Instruments

This research aimed to investigate Hong Kong kindergarten teachers’ perceptions of AI literacy education in kindergartens. In this study, teacher interviews were developed to understand the challenges of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms. The  researcher designed all interview questions to interview teachers regarding their perceptions of AI literacy in kindergarten. Subsequently, two education scholars provided feedback on these interview questions, and the researcher revised the interview questions based on their suggestions.

The interview questions include three parts—the first part concerns demographic information. In contrast, the second part requires teachers to answer the questions “What are the factors that may challenges of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms” and “What are the factors that enablers of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms?”. The third part requires teachers to answer, “Do you think AI literacy education  in kindergarten is important? Why?”. Responses from 15 teachers were collected in September 2022. Individual interviews lasting for 30–40 min were conducted via WhatsApp. All interviews were fully transcribed for analysis.

Participants

Teachers participating in this study from various kindergartens, allowing for the exploration of potential differences and similarities in teachers’ experiences and viewpoints.

Fifteen kindergarten teachers from several Hong Kong kindergartens participated in this research. The researcher personally contacted the teachers by phone to schedule teacher interviews. They were informed about the purpose of this research and the main topics of this interview. They were informed that this research was voluntary and anonymous and that they could withdraw at any time without providing a reason.

Table  1 describes the demographic information. Among these participants, fourteen (93.3%) teachers are female, and one (6.7%) is male. nine (60%) teachers were aged 20 -30  years old. Seven teachers (46.7%) reported having 1 to 5  years of teaching experience. Fourteen (93.3%) teachers had received university degrees. Participants were asked if they had any experience learning AI. Results show that none of them had attended AI workshops.

Data analysis

This study used interpretive description (Thorne, 2008). The important function of openended questions is to “give respondents opportunities to describe their experiences in more detail without the restrictions or biases inherent to fixed-choice format questions” (Chung et al., 2022, p. 4). Moreover, qualitative data collected from individual interviews can be effectively analyzed using Excel (Ose, 2016).

The challenges and enablers of promoting AI in kindergarten classrooms were coded by theme. Data analysis occurred in four steps: first, thematic analysis is used in this study, with the data being coded with the study’s research questions. Second, initial data codes were generated by the researcher and one student helper, who acted as the primary coder and the secondary coder respectively. To ensure objectivity in the coding process, the two coders coded the dataset separately before comparing. Third, potential themes in the codes were identified. The research team then discussed, reviewed, and resolved disagreements and discrepancies. At last, all articles were carefully reviewed, discussed, extracted, and coded by the coders to ensure consistency and validity of conclusions.

This study used memo notes to enhance the credibility of the findings in a qualitative study. To assess potential biases during the data analysis, the researcher documented reflections and insights throughout the qualitative phase of the study.

Ethical approval

The University of Hong Kong provided ethical approval for this study. Participants were thoroughly informed about the purpose of the research and their rights as research participants, including confidentiality and the right to withdraw at any time. An interview protocol was used during the session, and participants were provided with the protocol in advance to prepare before the interview.

Results

Fifteen teachers described the challenges and enablers of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms and articulated their thoughts on the importance of AI literacy in kindergartens through individual interviews.

RQ1:  What are the main challenges of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms?

The four main challenges associated with promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms include lack of school support (nine times), lack of children’s comprehensive ability (eight times), insufficient teacher knowledge of AI, (eight times), and lack of curriculum guidelines (seven times). Other challenges include time and parents’ intentions. Parent’s intention is defined as whether or not parents want their children to learn AI literacy. Challenge #1: Lack of school support

Most teachers reported the challenge of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms is a lack of school support. School support may encompass various aspects to promote AI literacy, including support for AI educational equipment, assistance with educational software, and actual needs. Hong Kong kindergartens promote artificial intelligence (AI) literacy in classrooms may encounter the following significant challenges in school support. The first challenge includes a lack of relevant backgrounds in artificial intelligence (AI) among kindergarten teachers in Hong Kong and the associated need for support staff to assist teachers in using AI devices in the classroom. Second, Hong Kong kindergartens face limited resources, lacking computers, tablets, and other devices, which may impact children’s opportunities to use artificial intelligence applications.

Challenge #2: Lack of children’s comprehensive ability

If AI literacy courses are introduced to Hong Kong kindergartens, kindergarten teachers are particularly concerned about the young children’s understanding level and the curriculum’s appropriateness. Some teachers think that kindergarten children may be too young to grasp AI knowledge and perceive AI as distant from their daily lives. Two teachers shared their thoughts:

Due to children’s young age, they may not understand AI knowledge, which will affect the learning process. (Teacher 15) AI may still be at distance from the lives of children, so my students may not understand AI concept or knowledge. (Teacher 9)

Challenge #3: Insufficient teacher knowledge of AI

Hong Kong kindergarten teachers have not received any formal training in artificial intelligence (AI). Due to a lack of understanding and knowledge about AI, they perceive implementing AI courses in Hong Kong’s kindergartens to be quite challenging.

Five teachers indicated insufficient knowledge of AI to promote AI literacy in kindergartens. All five teachers expressed that they lack the knowledge to teach AI to kindergarteners. One teacher mentioned that:

Adequate knowledge of AI is required for an instructor  to teach these courses. (Teacher 15)

Challenge #4: Lack of curriculum guideline

Currently, there is no specific curriculum outline for AI literacy in early childhood education, and many teachers lack a proficient background and understanding in AI. Therefore, they are uncertain about how to teach AI and design AI-related courses or activities for children. Two teachers shared their thoughts:

Our kindergarten teachers do not know how to teach AI or how to design courses. (Teacher 4) I don’t know how to design engaging activities to attract my students to learn AI. (Teacher 8)

Other challenges

Other challenges of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms are time and parent’s intentions.

In schools in Hong Kong, one of the primary constraints for teachers is time. Teachers express that they lack additional time to research artificial intelligence (AI) literacy due to their regular responsibilities in teaching and various administrative duties. Two teachers remarked that:

The class schedule is so full that it is virtually impossible to allocate time for AI literacy research. (Teachers 9 and 11)

How parents perceive AI literacy can significantly impact the feasibility of implementing such a curriculum in kindergarten. Some parents may be concerned about introducing AI literacy at a young age. They might worry that it could be too complex. It’s essential to address these concerns and educate parents about the potential benefits of AI literacy for their children’s future. One teacher shared her thoughts:

How parents perceive AI literacy may impact the feasibility of implementing this curriculum in kindergarten. Some parents may find AI literacy challenging for children aged 5-6, while others may prioritize subjects like Mathematics and English. (Teacher 13)

Enabler #2: School support

Schools are pivotal in promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms. There are several ways in which schools can support and enable the development of AI literacy in young children. To begin with, schools can hire some support staff to support teaching AI in kindergartens. Second, schools can provide some funding to purchase resources such as artificial intelligence robots that can enhance children’s with AI robot experience. Lastly, schools can provide professional development opportunities for kindergarten teachers to enhance teachers’ understanding of AI concepts. Two teachers remarked that:

If we have support staff to assist in implementing this AI curriculum, it would be beneficial in facilitating the smoother implementation of AI literacy in kindergarten, especially considering the already busy schedules of our teachers. If we can use specialized artificial intelligence educational tools in kindergarten classrooms, it would help stimulate the interest and curiosity of young children in AI technology. RQ3:  How do Hong Kong kindergarten teachers view the importance of AI literacy in kindergartens?

Teachers responded to the question, “Do you think AI literacy in kindergartens is important? Why?”. More than half of teachers think AI literacy education in kindergartens is important. The teacher perceptions of the importance of AI literacy in kindergarten are summarized in Table 4.

The Importance of AI Literacy Education in Kindergarten

Eight out of fifteen teachers stated that AI literacy in kindergartens is essential. When considering introducing AI literacy education in kindergarten, most teachers emphasize its importance. On the one hand, some teachers believe that cultivating AI literacy can enhance children’s 21st-century skills,  particularly critical thinking and problem-solving abilities. On the other hand, some teachers think that with the rapid development of social technology, kindergarten children should know which artificial intelligence applications are useful in daily life. Two teachers mentioned that:

I think AI literacy in kindergarten is very important due to AI literacy can increase children’s 21st skills, namely critical thinking and problem-solving skills. (Teacher 2) Nowadays, technology is developing rapidly, kindergarten children can understand what AI is and which AI principles apply in daily life. Therefore, AI literacy in kindergarten is very important. (Teacher 5)

Introducing AI literacy courses in kindergarten is seen not only as a means to benefit children in terms of technological proficiency but also as a way to enhance their collaborative and creativity skills. One teacher reported that:

I think AI literacy for kindergarten children is very important. Kindergarten children will enhance cooperative and creativity skills after the AI curriculum. (Teacher 11)

Incorporating AI into early education is thought to spark children’s profound interest in novel concepts during the critical stage of cognitive development. Three teachers stated that:

The early childhood period is a crucial stage in children’s cognitive development, where their curiosity towards new things is particularly strong. Introducing AI literacy in kindergarten can help children to engage with and comprehend technology at an early stage. (Teachers 5, 7, 8)

The Unimportance of AI Literacy Education in Kindergarten

Four and three out of fifteen teachers remained neutral and disagreed that AI literacy in kindergartens is essential, respectively. The idea of introducing AI education to kindergarten children is a topic of controversy. For several reasons, some teachers express neutral feeling or may disagree with introducing AI literacy to the kindergarten curriculum. These reasons include that some teachers believe that kindergarten children may struggle to grasp complex concepts, especially those involving high levels of technicality and abstraction of AI. Kindergarten children may also be concerned that such content could be too hard for

young children, lacking the ability to comprehend the associated concepts. One teacher shared:

Kindergarten children are so young, and they cannot understand AI. Therefore, I do not agree that AI literacy education is important in kindergartens. (Teacher 6)

Second, other teachers may argue that, during the kindergarten stage, prioritizing the cultivation of foundational skills such as language, mathematics, and social interaction is more crucial. They may perceive AI education as not being a primary concern at this developmental stage, with other subjects being more beneficial for the comprehensive development of young children. One teacher shared her thoughts:

In the early stages of preschool education, our primary goal is to establish foundational literacy skills such as reading, writing, and basic comprehension. I think other courses are more important for kindergarten children than AI literacy. AI literacy can be learned from primary school. (Teacher 15)

Finally, some teachers may need an in-depth understanding of AI literacy education and may need to be made aware of the potential benefits it can bring to children in the kindergarten stage. This lack of awareness might lead to teachers disagreeing with the idea of introducing AI literacy to the kindergarten curriculum. One teacher remarked that:

While I acknowledge the increasing importance of artificial intelligence in modern society, I still do not fully believe it should be part of the kindergarten curriculum. I am uncertain whether our children need to learn AI at this age. I need more evidence to prove how AI literacy can enhance various aspects of early childhood development. (Teacher 3)

Discussion

AI literacy has gained much popularity in recent years. Many studies focused on teaching AI literacy from primary to higher education (e.g., Su et al., 2022). However, a limited number of studies focused on teaching AI literacy in kindergartens. Little is known about the facilitation  of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten.  Despite one study explored the barriers of promoting AI literacy in education (Wang & Cheng, 2021). The study, nevertheless, was conducted in the secondary school context  (Wang & Cheng, 2021). Therefore, this is the first study probing the challenges and enablers of incorporating AI literacy into kindergarten classrooms. This study also explores teachers’ perceptions of the importance of AI literacy for kindergarten teachers. 15 kindergarten teachers participated in individual interviews, which collected their views on the importance of AI literacy for kindergarten, as well as the challenges and enablers of AI literacy in kindergartens in this study. Research on AI literacy in kindergarten requires input from teachers. This study develops AI literacy policy framework for young children. This study also provides future research directions as a reference for researchers. For instance, researchers may explore these areas in greater detail to raise public awareness of AI literacy.

Upon review of the challenges associated with promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms, results indicate four main challenges of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms: lack of school support, lack of children’s comprehension ability, insufficient teacher knowledge of AI, and lack of curriculum guidelines. To begin, kindergarten

classrooms often lack the necessary resources, such as computers and AI robots, to teach AI concepts effectively. Without these tools, engaging young students in AI-related activities is challenging. Second, kindergarten teachers may not have the knowledge or training to teach AI concepts effectively. Wang and Cheng (2021) also believe that the lack of AI knowledge among teachers is a major difficulty in introducing AI literacy to secondary schools. Without proper professional development and support, they may feel ill-equipped to introduce AI literacy into their classrooms. Su and Zhong (2022) mentioned that AI teacher training is essential for kindergarten teachers considering that many kindergarten teachers lack AI knowledge. Therefore, providing teacher training is vital for implementing AI literacy in kindergartens.

Addressing these challenges will require a concerted effort from various stakeholders. Schools should be encouraged to provide the necessary support, allocate resources, and adapt curricula to incorporate AI education. Moreover, teachers need access to professional development programs that enhance their knowledge and confidence in teaching AI-related topics. Collaboration between educational institutions, policymakers, and the AI community can help overcome these challenges and ensure that kindergarten students are better prepared for a technology-driven future.

Regarding the enablers of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms, this research found that the three main enablers are government support, school support, and social needs. In terms of government support, to begin with, AI literacy in kindergartens may be promoted to a greater extent if it is made mandatory. Given this, the government should consider incorporating some AI activities into kindergartens’ and the science/ ICT curriculum. Second, governments can allocate funding to kindergartens to acquire necessary resources, such as technology and educational materials. Financial support can significantly enhance the quality of AI education in kindergartens. If more government subsidies are allocated to artificial intelligence technology, the implementation of AI literacy can be further facilitated. Technological advancement has helped teachers overcome several challenges in teaching AI (Su & Yang, 2022). Third, government initiatives can provide opportunities for kindergarten teachers to receive training and professional development related to AI education. This initiatives  ensure educators have the knowledge and skills to teach AI concepts effectively. In terms of school support, schools can support AI literacy in kindergarten from the following aspects. To begin with, schools can provide teachers with the time and resources for professional development. Teachers need to be well-prepared to teach AI concepts, and schools can facilitate this by offering training programs and workshops. Second, when schools incorporate AI education into kindergarten, they can purchase necessary tools, such as computers and AI robots, to facilitate AI learning among children. Social needs are important in promoting AI literacy in kindergartens. With the rapid development of technology, it is becoming increasingly important for kindergarten children to understand how AI technology is used in daily life, even if they do not need to understand the complexity of AI knowledge (Su et al., 2023).

We suggest hiring more support staff to assist teachers in delivering the AI curriculum. As artificial intelligence is a relatively complex course that requires in-depth expertise and practical experience. Support staff can serve as teaching assistants, assisting teachers in preparing course materials and providing additional guidance and tutoring. They can also provide technical support in the classroom to ensure children use relevant AI tools effectively. Hiring more support staff not only reducing teacher workloads but also enhance the quality and effectiveness of the AI courses. Therefore, hiring more support staff can help researchers implement AI literacy education in kindergartens effectively.

There is a division of opinions among kindergarten teachers regarding the cultivation of artificial intelligence literacy in preschool education. Over half of the teachers consider AI literacy important for kindergarten children. On the one hand, some teachers believe that fostering AI literacy early on contributes to better preparation for children to adapt to the future technological society. On the other hand, some teachers may perceive that developing AI literacy for preschoolers is too intricate, possibly not aligning with their age and developmental stage. The lack of training and support on integrating AI education may lead to skepticism among some educators. Therefore, providing professional training and support can assist teachers in gaining a better understanding and overall application of AI education. Understanding teachers’ views on the importance of AI literacy in kindergartens can help conduct AI research more efficiently, and researchers need cooperation with teachers to conduct AI research. Therefore, teachers’ perceptions of AI literacy in kindergarten should be investigated. We suggest that share the research findings with kindergarten teachers and public, so that they can understand the benefits of AI literacy education for young children and young children can understand the basic AI concepts and knowledge.

Several points can be considered to address the tension between the acknowledged importance of AI literacy and teachers’ preparedness to teach it: To begin, define clear goals and standards for AI literacy to guide teachers in understanding what content they should teach. Second, update the educational curricula to incorporate AI literacy into formal courses. Third, provide professional development opportunities for teachers, such as workshops and training courses, to enhance their proficiency in teaching AI literacy. Lastly, implement teaching methods like project-based learning and hands-on activities to engage students and deepen their understanding of AI concepts.

Collaboration between educators and AI experts is crucial for successfully integrating AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms (Su & Zhong, 2022). AI experts can develop educational apps with personalized learning features to help young children understand and grasp AI better. Educators can provide professional guidance to ensure that these apps’ content aligns with the abilities of young children.

Partnerships may also be established between educational institutions and foundations to provide professional development for educators. For example, the Li Ka Shing Foundation collaborates with the Education University of Hong Kong to offer AI literacy training for educators (The Education University of Hong Kong, 2023).

Further research directions

Below is a roadmap for the field for future researchers as a reference. These research areas can help advance our understanding of how AI education can benefit young children and improve the quality of AI literacy programs.

•	 To examine the long-term impact of AI literacy education on children’s 21st-century skills, future researchers could conduct a longitudinal study. Researchers could track children who receive AI literacy education throughout the academic year and observe the development of their critical thinking, problem-solving, and creativity skills. Another approach to evaluate the long-term impact of AI literacy education on children’s 21st century skills could be to compare students who receive AI literacy education with those who do not. This comparison could provide deeper insights into the effectiveness of AI literacy education in enhancing these skills. •	 Future researchers could investigate the effectiveness of using AI robots to enhance students’ math and English proficiency. Future researchers could design a two-group experiment: one group of children is using AI robots to learn maths and English, while another group uses traditional teaching methods. Researchers could use the same test paper to compare the maths and English test scores of these two groups of students. This would help evaluate the effectiveness of using AI robots in enhancing students’ maths and English abilities compared to traditional teaching methods.

•	 Future research could focus on analysing the cultural, social, and other factors that influence the implementation and effectiveness of AI literacy programs in different regions. By identifying and understanding these factors, researchers can develop tailored strategies for implementing AI literacy programs in different regions.

These research directions are essential for advancing AI literacy education for young children. This research can inform the development of effective AI literacy programs and help educators and policymakers make informed decisions about integrating AI literacy curricula into kindergarten classrooms. Furthermore, such research can improve early childhood education and better prepare young learners for the digital and AI-driven world.

Conclusion and limitations

AI literacy in K-12 contexts generally receives far more attention than in kindergartens (K1-K3). This study aims to identify the challenges and enablers of promoting AI literacy in kindergartens and analyze teachers’ views on AI literacy education  in kindergartens. Results collected from the 15 kindergarten teachers in Hong Kong pinpoint four main challenges of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten classrooms: lack of school support, lack of children’s comprehensive ability, insufficient teacher knowledge of AI, and lack of curriculum guidelines. On the other hand, government support, school support, and social needs are found to be enablers of promoting AI literacy in kindergarten. It has also been found that more than half of teachers believe AI literacy education is crucial for young children. The challenges and enablers should be carefully considered by researchers for conducting research on AI literacy. This study also develops an AI literacy policy framework for young children, including three dimensions—governance, pedagogical, and  operational and management.

This study has two limitations. To begin with, due to a relatively small sample size, this study only collected data through interviews. Other instruments, such as questionnaires and surveys, were not employed. Future research should consider incorporating a variety of research tools, such as questionnaires, surveys, and interviews, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the challenges and enablers associated with promoting AI literacy in kindergartens. Second, the study was only conducted in Hong Kong. Future research endeavors will extend invitations to schools from different regions and diverse locations to participate in this study.
