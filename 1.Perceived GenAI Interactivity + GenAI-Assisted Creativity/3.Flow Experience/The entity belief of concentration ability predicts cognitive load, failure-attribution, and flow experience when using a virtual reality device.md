The entity belief of concentration ability predicts cognitive load, failure-attribution, and ﬂow experience when using a virtual reality device

Jon-Chao Hong a,b, Jhen-Ni Ye c, Jian-Hong Ye d and Ling-Wen Kungb

aInstitute for Research Excellence in Learning Sciences, National Taiwan Normal University, Taipei, Taiwan; bDepartment of Industrial Education, National Taiwan Normal University, Taipei, Taiwan; cGraduate Institute of Technological & Vocational Education, National Taipei University of Technology, Taipei, Taiwan; dFaculty of Education, Beijing Normal University, Beijing, People’s Republic of China

ABSTRACT Attentional control theory indicates that concentration is considered an important variable that contributes to learning. There are some devices for players to practice their concentration, but there are few virtual reality (VR) designs which can increase the level of diﬃculty for students to discipline their mental concentration with incongruent hands-on movement. To address this gap, a VR system named Tracking Fun was designed, which requires hand-eye coordination to move a ball to a target hole. To explore the eﬀect of playing Tracking-fun on players’ concentration performance, the study explored the relationship among entity beliefs of concentration ability (EBCA), intrinsic cognitive load (ICL), failure attribution of VR operation (FAVRO), ﬂow experience, and gameplay performance. Students from a skills-based senior high school took part in this experimental research, and 259 valid questionnaires were returned. The research results showed that: EBCA can positively predict cognitive load; EBCA can positively predict FAVRO; ICL and ﬂow have a negative correlation; FAVRO can negatively predict ﬂow experience when interacting with VR; and ﬂow can negatively predict gameplay performance. Based on the results, enriching the opportunity and diﬃculty level of Tracking-fun can enhance players’ concentration. Follow-up research suggestions are proposed.

Introduction

Virtual reality (VR) technology can be used to quickly construct simulations to associate environmental elements to provide immersive experiences. The virtual environment is brought closer to the real environment, which is beneﬁcial for the learners’ cognitive development (Çakiroğlu & Gökoğlu, 2019; Passing et al., 2016). In order to understand the eﬀect of VR on cognitive development, Meyer et al. (2019) found that immersive VR learning was very useful for developing participants’ cognition and aﬀect. With the advancement of VR technology, in recent years, an increasing number of companies and schools have been using VR to train and develop the domain-speciﬁc ability, for example, of their employees and students (Fowler, 2015). Some scholars have also used VR modeling to study visual cognition and behavior mechanisms in sports practice (Kim & Ko, 2019), pediatric emergency training (Matthews et al., 2020), car-detailing training (Tai

In the Tracking Fun Labyrinth tasks, players have to move the ball from the bottom to the top designated hole, focusing on avoiding dropping the ball along the way. Two trial types were interleaved in the Tracking Fun Labyrinth tasks: (1) incongruent, where moving goal ≠response direction (i.e. moving the ball up by holding the rope down); or (2) congruent, where moving goal = response direction (i.e. using the right hand to hold the rope down, so the ball moves to the right). A task can control the cognitive processing which introducing conﬂict between the stimulus location and the response eﬀect direction (Hommel, 2011; Simon, 2011). When visual stimulus and the direction of the movement require incongruent movement against the goal direction, it will slow the movement compared with congruent movement (Hilchey et al., 2011). According to the Attentional control theory (Eysenck et al., 2007), the congruent and incongruent movements require players to pay more attention to the operation compared to other VR operations. Moreover, structural learning theories suggest that cognitive and aﬀective learning can be generated if an individual learns with concentration that connects a class of related movements (Harris et al., 2020). In line with this, the present study aimed to have participants play Tracking Fun and to explore the practicing eﬀect.

According to the achievement emotion model (Pekrun, 2006), emotion can be divided into activated emotion and deactivated emotion. Activated positive emotions (e.g. interest or ﬂow experience) can maintain cognitive ability to achieve the purpose of the task, which will have a beneﬁcial impact on learning achievements; on the contrary, deactivated negative emotions (e.g. anxiety or boredom) will reduce the cognitive ability of learning, thus adversely aﬀecting the performance of the learning tasks (Pekrun, 2006). In addition, the interaction between players’ selfbelief and learning cognition in the game will trigger positive and negative emotions (Mullins & Sabherwal, in press). As such, the activated and deactivated emotion may aﬀect players’ gameplay performance; however, how players’ emotional factors could be activated or deactivated has not yet been explored in-depth in the context of playing VR (Shi et al., 2019). To understand how players can achieve their goals while playing, this study used Tracking-fun to explore their emotion activation or deactivation with motion tracking.

Self-concept of ability can facilitate learning and achievement by promoting interest and justifying learning strategies, fostering future learning and motivation (Clem et al., 2021; Pekrun et al., 2017). For example, taking implicit belief of intelligence as one self-concept of ability, students who have an entity belief of intelligence positively predicted negative emotions including anger, anxiety, shame, and boredom (King, 2012). Accordingly, the present study conceptualized a meaning system to explore the link between implicit belief of intelligence, achievement emotion, and game performance. Taken together, the present study aimed to explore the correlates between entity belief of concentration ability, deactivated factors (cognitive load and failure attribution of VR operation), activated factor (ﬂow) and gameplay performance. It was expected that the results of this study could enhance the usage of diﬀerent types of Labyrinth game to discipline players’ concentration.

Theoretical background

Entity belief of concentration ability (EBCA)

Dweck (1986) and Dinger and Dickhäuser (2013) deﬁned two types of implicit belief of intelligence: entity belief and incremental belief. Entity belief means that intelligence is ﬁxed, and it cannot change due to internal or external factors, while incremental belief considers that intelligence is not ﬁxed, but can be improved through one’s own eﬀorts or training. Karlen et al. (2019) indicated

Moran (2004) proposed that concentration means focusing on the task at hand while ignoring other non-task factors, whereas visual selective concentration means that when information enters the brain, only part of it will be considered. Ommundsen (2001) noted that learners with entity belief will have their concentration ability and their information processing eﬀorts impeded. Learners with entity belief will perform poorly in terms of concentration and will lack control, and there will be no external interference in VR that will cause cognitive stimulation (Selzer et al., 2019). To play Tracking Fun, participants have to concentrate on their movement to avoid the ball dropping into the wrong holes. However, the current research on implicit belief of concentration ability is still quite limited in terms of exploring hands-on movement. Based on this, this study proposed EBCA as the basis for measuring learners’ EBCA while playing Tracking Fun.

Cognitive load when using VR

There are three diﬀerent aspects of cognitive loading: intrinsic cognitive load (ICL) (Sweller, 1988) which is related to the task’s structure, extraneous cognitive load (Sweller & Chandler, 1994) which is the result of cognitive resources being allocated to working memory in order to perform operations unrelated to the task, and third, germane cognitive load (Sweller, 2010), characterized as the load resulting from the learning process itself which is related to procedural thinking and action. Considering playing Labyrinth VR, which involves avoiding dropping ball into holes, participants engage in some sorts of procedural thinking and action that require intensive mental eﬀort; therefore, ICL was considered in this study.

The use of VR technology involves individuals generating self-experience, and having bodily immersion and sensation experiences (Armougum et al., 2019; Murray & Gordon, 2001). Additionally, in most studies, cognitive load has been investigated in static contexts, for example, when using a computer (Chen et al., 2017) or smartphone (Hochberg et al., 2018) to perform cognitive tasks. However, this static conﬁguration does not reﬂect the VR environment where we move around and explore the content in VR. This raises the question to be explored in this study: how does ICL vary during navigation in the immersive context of Tracking Fun?

Failure attribution of VR operation (FAVRO)

The antecedents and consequences of intrapersonal attributions (i.e. how one sees oneself) and interpersonal attributions (i.e. how one sees others) are addressed by the attribution theory (Graham, 2020). People tend to attribute successes as happening due to the self, while failures are often thought of as being due to others (Levine et al., 2017). In VR, students need to recognize 3D objects, manipulate 3D components, and use spatial–temporal abilities to observe and control the steps needed to face the challenges posed by the user interface design (Sun et al., 2019). However, if the visual observation and movement control step by step, players may attribute the failure to a “dysfunctional” UI design (Rascle et al., 2015). Those who attribute failure to uncontrollable situational causes experience unfavorable behavioral consequences (e.g. Le Foll et al., 2008; Rascle et al., 2015). Flow experience

Flow is deﬁned as an optimal experience, and has been experienced by people in diﬀerent work or learning settings (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975, 1990). This optimal experience of ﬂow is characterized by cognitive absorption, enjoyment, and time distortion (Chen, Woolcott, & Sweller, 2017). In academia, such optimal experiences have been examined using games to study ﬂow experience (Erhel & Jamet, 2019). The primary function of VR is to provide users with an immersive experience by enhancing their telepresence. A previous study indicated that VR technologies can amplify users’ ﬂow experiences via vividness, interactivity, and telepresence to a greater extent than traditional media (Kim & Ko, 2019). Another study indicated that simulation-based learning enables students to achieve successful learning by concentrating on the activities that can generate ﬂow (Yoo & Kim, 2018). However, few studies have focused on identifying the mechanism of playing VR that informs such user ﬂow experiences in the context of Labyrinth. Thus, considering Csikszentmihalyi’s (2000) assertion that ﬂow experience relies on the individual’s perception of task diﬃculty, players’ ﬂow experience when playing Tracking-fun was explored.

Gameplay performance

Digital learning environments aﬀect students’ activity-related achievement emotions and learning performance. Using technology enables online assessment of students’ progress while they are learning (Schrader & Grassinger, 2021). For example, to identify the learning eﬀects of VR, Choi et al. (2020) designed a VR game to train operators to improve their safety-oriented behavior. Based on this practice, students can improve their learning performance by receiving adaptive, timely, and instant feedback during learning or after suﬃcient eﬀort (Tu et al., 2020). Škola and Liarokapis (2018) found that the group which learned using embodied VR had signiﬁcantly higher average accuracy in their motor actions than the control group which did not use embodied VR. As Labyrinth VR is a kind of motor action with mental imagery, improving the players’ bodily movement may lead them to have higher accuracy to pass those holes. Thus, the improvement of the participants when playing Tracking-fun was explored in this study.

Method

The tracking-fun VR game

The features of tracking fun To perform the Tracking Fun Labyrinth tasks, the players need to combine action observation and motor imagery (AO + MI) with hand-eye coordination, where action observation (AO) means deliberate and structured observation of movement (Neuman & Gray, 2013), and motor imagery (MI) is the mental rehearsal of movement, which is typically accompanied with body or hand movements (Wright et al., 2018). The diﬃculty levels increase by increasing the density of the holes from level 1 to level 5 (level 1 as shown in Figure 2, and level 5 in Figure 3). Players have to move a ball from the bottom to the top and prevent it from dropping into the holes on the way to the ﬁnal goal.

How to play The player needs to grasp the rope through the handle of the game, and use an up and down movement to control the ball in the left and right directions, to send the ball into the hole speciﬁed by the arrow. However, if the player operates it too quickly, the ball will fall oﬀthe wooden board, as shown in Figure 3.

Experiment design and procedure

Participants

A total of 297 students from a senior technical high school in New Taipei City, Taiwan participated in this study, but there were only 259 valid samples used for the statistical analysis. In terms of gender, 50 (19.3%) were males and 209 (80.7%) were females. For school grade, 135 (52.1%) were ﬁrst grade and 124 (47.9%) were second grade, and regarding age, 117 (45.2%) were 15 years old, 93 (35.9%) were 16 years old, and 49 (19.9%) were 17 years old. There were 49 (18.9%) who had experience of using VR and 210 (81.1%) who did not.

Questionnaire

The development of the questionnaire

The questionnaire items were adapted from previous studies and were translated into Chinese. To ensure the face validity of the questionnaire, ﬁve domain experts reviewed and revised the items, and a high level of agreement was reached. A 5-point Likert scale was adopted to assess the degree of agreement for each item, where 1 stands for strongly disagree and 5 stands for strongly agree. After the questionnaires were returned, the reliability and validity of the items and constructs were examined by ﬁrst-order conﬁrmatory factor analysis, and were then subjected to structural equation modeling.

EBCA This study adapted the “Intelligence Theory Scale” compiled by Yeager and Dweck (2012) to analyze the degree of the learners’ entity belief to form the “EBCA Scale”; six items were designed for this

ICL Based on the cognitive load theory, this study revised the “Cognitive Load” scale (Hart & Staveland, 1988) to develop the “ICL Scale”; nine items were designed for this construct. Example items include, “I must pay great attention to using VR to go through the Tracking Fun practice” and “I must pay great attention to complete the Tracking Fun practice.”

FAVRO This study was based on the attribution theory proposed by Weiner (1976), and it referred to the “Internet Cognitive Failure Scale” designed by Hong et al. (2017) to develop the “Failure Attribution Scale”; ﬁve items were designed for this construct. Example items include, “If my performance of operating VR is not good, I think it is because the operating system is too complicated” and “If my performance of the VR operation is not good, I think it is because the system is too sensitive, and it lets the object fall with a slight touch.”

Flow experience This study adapted the “Flow Experience Scale” designed by Pearce et al. (2005), to develop the “Flow Experience Scale”; eight items were designed for this construct. Example items include: “When playing Tracking Fun, I was so attentive that I didn’t feel any time passing at all” and “When playing Tracking Fun, I experienced a state of ecstasy.”

Item analysis

Using ﬁrst-order conﬁrmatory factor analysis to test the internal validity of the questionnaire items, this research retained items with factor loadings larger than 0.6, based on the suggestion of Hair et al. (2010). Accordingly, four items of entity beliefs of concentration ability were retained from six items, seven items of cognitive load were retained from nine items, four items of failure attribution of VR operation were retained from ﬁve items, and seven items of ﬂow experience were retained from eight items.

The interpretation range of the study was judged by measuring the external validity of the items (Cor, 2016), and a t-test was performed on the top 27% and the bottom 27% of the values of all respondents for each question; if the t-value (critical ratio) was greater than 3 (***p < .001), then the external validity was considered to be signiﬁcant (Green & Salkind, 2004).

If the VR scenes had transferred so that they could not track and move quickly, students may have blamed the usability of the VR.

Williams (2018) found the importance of mental models that are dynamic, malleable, and can be adapted to new and incoming information. Such models have received considerable attention in relation to controller eﬀects (McGloin et al., 2016). Related research shows that high ﬂow experience has a signiﬁcant inﬂuence on learners’ game performance (Erhel & Jamet, 2019; Hong et al., 2020). “Challenge” and “interactivity” serve as representatives of ﬂow experience in games and have positive eﬀects on game outcomes (Alan et al., 2022). When playing Tracking Fun, ﬂow experience can make participants experience more challenge and interactivity and be more focused on the activity, in turn resulting in higher performance. When testing whether participants’ ﬂow experience in VR learning had a positive impact on their gameplay performance, the present study found that H5 was positively supported; this ﬁnding is consistent with the research of Erhel and Jamet (2019).

Conclusion

Achievement emotion is deﬁned as an emotion directly related to achievement activities or outcomes (Pekrun, 2006). Achievement emotion is thought to occur when individuals feel in control or have no control over academic activities and outcomes that are important to them (Harley et al., 2019). Players’ achievement emotion states will be aﬀected by the ease or diﬃculty of controlling the activity, which will then inﬂuence their game performance (Yeh et al., 2016). VR has the role of stimulating learners, but VR learning may also cause learners to experience cognitive load and concentration problems, and may therefore inﬂuence their gameplay performance. The results of this study conﬁrm that an increase in participants’ EBCA will also increase their ICL and FAVRO when playing Tracking Fun; an increase in ICL and FAVRO will decrease their ﬂow experience when playing Tracking Fun; and increasing ﬂow experience when playing Tracking Fun will increase their gameplay performance.

Implication

According to Attentional Control theory, this research aimed to explore the correlates between EBCA, ICL, FAVRO, and ﬂow experience when using VR with hand movements. The results advance our understanding of how EBCA can inhibit the ﬂow experience, implying that students with a high level of EBCA should be trained to reduce their entity belief and ﬁnd more enjoyment in interacting with Tracking Fun.

The present research is the ﬁrst study to design a VR game which includes congruent and incongruent movements in hand-eye coordination to explore the correlation between cognition and aﬀect. Meyer et al. (2019) studied the eﬀect of pre-training by learning through immersive VR. The results can be applied to concentration training with hand movements, such as that required by bus drivers.

Limitations and future study

Regarding the help of new technology, if the new technology is simply introduced into the learning environment, there is no guarantee that it can eﬀectively improve the learner’s gameplay performance. If the characteristics and advantages of new technologies cannot be used eﬀectively, learners may encounter more barriers in the learning process, causing additional cognitive load (Liu, Cheng, & Huang, 2011). This study used a “VR concentration system” to evaluate the concentration performance of the participants, but not all participants using VR can have the same acceptance, so other

Quasi-experimental designs usually lack one or two variables that should be controlled for in experimental studies, and randomization is rarely possible (Thompson & Panacek, 2006). The present study encountered problems with randomized participant assignment due to the study size and participant recruitment in the study design. Therefore, it is suggested that subsequent studies may expand participant recruitment when conducting VR experiments and conduct pretesting prior to grouping experiments to ensure homogeneity in participant assignment.

Considering that the design profession needs to cultivate good concentration, this study was conducted to understand the performance of this student group when learning with VR. Therefore, this study recruited participants from advertising design majors in a technical high school in New Taipei City, Taiwan. However, the gender imbalance of the participants who were all advertising design majors is also a problem, and the sample size was too small, which may lead to poor inferencing. Therefore, it is suggested that subsequent studies could expand the sampling of participants from diﬀerent design disciplines, or from university design disciplines, and also expand the sampling cities to increase the ability to infer from the study results.
