A R T I C L E

Flow experience fosters university students' well-­being through psychological resilience: A longitudinal design with cross-­lagged analysis

Yanhui Mao1 |   Xinyi Luo1  |   Shujun Wang1  |   Zhuozhu Mao2  | Mei Xie3  |   Marino Bonaiuto3

1Institute of Applied Psychology, Psychological Research and Counseling Center, Southwest Jiaotong University, Chengdu, China

Abstract Background: Existing research has linked individuals' flow experience – a positive affective and cognitive state of deep immersion and engagement in daily activities – and their well-­being, particularly among university students. A growing number of longitudinal studies have further contributed to this understanding. However, limited attention has been given to exploring the dynamic interplay between these two variables and their underlying mechanisms (i.e., the mediator) of psychological resilience, specifically among university students in the context of the COVID-­19 pandemic. Aims: To address this research gap, the present study draws on self-­determination, flow and broaden-­and-­build theories. It examines the temporal dynamics and relationships between flow experience and well-­being, and the mediating role of psychological resilience among Chinese university students. Sample: The study adopts a three-­wave longitudinal design with a sample of 474 university students in Southwest China. Methods: Participants' flow experience, well-­being and psychological resilience were measured across three time waves. Results: The findings of this study reveal that flow experience predicts well-­being across the three waves and that psychological resilience mediates this prediction. This empirical evidence emphasizes the significance of both flow experience and psychological resilience in contributing to the well-­being of university students over time amid COVID-­19. Conclusions: These findings enrich our understanding of the factors contributing to well-­being in educational settings and provide highly relevant and timely insights for developing strategies to foster well-­being among university

INTRODUCTION

Flow experience is a state of complete immersion in a task or activity that occurs when the individuals perceive their skills and the task's challenges as both high and in balance (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975); it is also an optimal experience characterized by high concentration, full absorption, time distortion and optimal enjoyment (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). Flow experience has been proven to be associated with intrinsic motivation, life satisfaction and reduced stress (Asakawa, 2004; Ryan & Deci, 2001), which are particularly important during pandemic-­induced and, more broadly, any crisis-­induced disruptions (Bassi et al., 2022). University students represent a unique population at a significant juncture in their personal and professional growth, navigating numerous academic, social and personal challenges that can induce flow experience. Previous research has highlighted the importance of flow experience for university students' well-­being and personal development (Wu et al., 2021). Recently, there has been a growing trend in employing longitudinal research methodologies to delve into the dynamics of flow experience over time (Liu et al., 2023; Mao, Guo, et al., 2023).

Well-­being has been defined as a combination of feeling good and functioning well, which includes experiencing positive emotions like happiness and contentment, realizing personal potential, having some control over one's life, fostering a sense of purpose and nurturing positive relationships (Huppert, 2009). Recent research has demonstrated that flow experience serves as a key predictor of subjective well-­being among Chinese university students through the chain mediation of academic self-­ efficacy and self-­esteem, underscoring the potential of these pathways to enhance well-­being in the face of pandemic-­related stresses (Mao et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021).

Psychological resilience, defined as the ability to adapt flexibly and have positive emotions even in the midst of stressful experiences (Tugade & Fredrickson, 2004), is threatened by the upheaval of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-­19) pandemic (Mourad et al., 2022). Flow experience is positively associated with psychological resilience (Mao et al., 2020), a crucial predictor of well-­being (Izydorczyk et al., 2019). This suggests that resilience may mediate the relationship between flow experience and well-­being since it facilitates the increase in students' problem-­solving skills, therefore an improvement of academic performance (Kotzé & Kleynhans, 2013), as well as overall mental health during global crises (Li & Hasson, 2020).

During the study period, which spanned from early September to late December of 2022, the landscape of the COVID-­19 pandemic was evolving, marking a critical juncture in its trajectory. This phase, often regarded as the twilight of the pandemic's most stringent phase, represented a pivotal moment of transition. Most Chinese students physically attended classes in person during this time, while a subset, particularly those in regions still grappling with the pandemic, faced physical attendance challenges (a hybrid model of education was implemented). Rigorous COVID-­19 testing protocols and stringent control measures, including mandatory tests for travel and special permissions for campus departures, characterized the period (English et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). Against this backdrop, a pervasive sense of fear and pessimism towards the prolonged pandemic restrictions permeated the student population (Gu et al., 2023). As this phase symbolized the concluding stages of intense pandemic control measures, a noticeable weariness towards these restrictions was evident

Flow and well-­being

Flow experience, as defined by Csikszentmihalyi (1975, 1990), refers to those moments of deep absorption and optimal enjoyment in one's daily activities. Initially, Csikszentmihalyi (1975) identified six key components of this experience based on interview data: the merging of action and awareness, centred attention, loss of self-­consciousness, a sense of control, coherent and non-­contradictory demands and the autotelic nature of the activity. Expanding on this foundation, nine dimensions of flow were developed (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990), and these dimensions include (1) clear goals, (2) unambiguous and immediate feedback, (3) a balance between challenge and skill, (4) full concentration on the task at hand, (5) a merging of action and awareness, (5) a sense of control over the activity, (6) a loss of self-­consciousness, (7) full involvement, (8) transformation of time perception and (9) the autotelic experience itself. Although the ongoing academic discourse continues to dissect and distinguish the various dimensions and scales of flow experience, resulting in the coexistence of multiple frameworks in the literature, these commonly identified dimensions are well recognized as the key characteristics of flow and they have been applied to identify the experience itself in many walks of life, such as sports and education (Jackson & Csikszentmihalyi, 1999; Wong & Csikszentmihalyi, 1991).

The connection between flow experience and well-­being is evident and multifaceted, spanning both hedonic and eudaimonic dimensions (Delle Fave et al., 2011; Huppert, 2009). From the hedonic perspective, the flow experience is described as an enjoyable and pleasurable experience (Park et al., 2009, p. 274), and adolescents who have experienced flow exhibit increased life satisfaction, hedonic balance and psychological well-­being in comparison to their peers (Bassi et al., 2014). From the eudaimonic perspective, flow experience has been linked to psychological well-­being due to its influence on intrinsic motivation and autonomy (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Flow also carries eudaimonic characteristics such as commitment, persistence and achievement (Csikszentmihalyi, 2003; Smith et al., 2023). According to self-­determination theory (SDT; Deci & Ryan, 1985), which emphasizes three fundamental psychological needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness, participating in activities that induce flow experience aligns with these needs, particularly enhancing competence and autonomy. According to Sheldon and Schüler (2011), this alignment is pivotal for promoting well-­being. By facilitating a sense of achievement and self-­determination, flow experience contributes to both hedonic (immediate pleasure) and eudaimonic (long-­term fulfilment) aspects of well-­being, as observed by Kim et al. (2017).

Flow experience and well-­being have been extensively studied in psychological research, encompassing various groups and contexts. For example, studies have found that flow experience is associated with improved life satisfaction (Rogatko, 2009) and well-­being (Asakawa, 2010) among college students. Furthermore, evidence suggests a positive correlation between the experience of flow in academic and athletic activities and the well-­being of university students (Lin, 2023). Importantly, flow during academic activities has been identified as a consistent positive predictor of well-­being and academic achievement and, conversely, as a negative predictor of burnout among university students (Rijavec et al., 2017). These findings suggest a positive relationship between flow experience and well-­being among university students across different cultural contexts.

While the longitudinal association between flow experience and well-­being has been consistently observed among different demographics (Tse et al., 2021), the nature of this relationship remains unclear

The potential mediating role of psychological resilience

The theory of flow and psychological resilience, embedded within the framework of positive psychology, serves as cornerstones in understanding how individuals cope with challenges and adversities. Flow theory (Csikszentmihalyi & LeFevre, 1989; Massimini et al., 1987; Massimini & Carli, 1988) underscores the importance of maintaining equilibrium between challenges and skills, emphasizing the need to stretch one's abilities to overcome environmental obstacles. Such a concept is particularly relevant in the COVID-­19 pandemic, as university students must adapt to new academic and personal challenges, such as transitioning to virtual learning environments (Habe et al., 2021).

Engaging in flow-­inducing activities has been suggested to foster increased competence and self-­ efficacy, fundamental elements of psychological resilience (Mao et al., 2020). Moreover, based on the broaden-­and-­build theory (BBT), positive emotions elicited through flow experience broaden an individual's range of thoughts and actions, enhancing mental capabilities like flexibility, creativity and openness (Fredrickson, 2001). This broadened cognitive and behavioural spectrum facilitates the development of adaptive coping mechanisms and problem-­solving skills. In the context of the COVID-­19 pandemic, this process becomes crucial, as it enables individuals to handle pandemic-­related stress, ultimately strengthening their psychological resilience effectively (Takiguchi et al., 2023).

Empirical studies, such as those by Tugade and Fredrickson (2004), support the link between flow experience and enhanced psychological resilience. However, these relationships are complex and multidimensional, as Bartzik et al. (2021) showed during the COVID-­19 pandemic, wherein increased stress with fewer flow experiences among nurses resulted in lower work satisfaction and well-­being. Thus, this study aims to address the research gap by exploring the longitudinal relationship between flow and psychological resilience amidst a crisis such as this global pandemic.

In parallel, psychological resilience and well-­being share a strong association, with resilience serving as a potent predictor of various aspects of well-­being, including life satisfaction, positive affect and a sense of purpose (Aboalshamat et al., 2018; Luthans et al., 2007; Tay & Lim, 2020). In the context of academic environments, research has consistently shown that psychological resilience mediates the relationship between optimism and well-­being among university students (Miranda & Cruz, 2020), reduces burnout (Chue & Cheung, 2021) and is positively linked to well-­being in diverse cultural contexts (Li & Hasson, 2020).

Building on the theoretical and empirical evidence discussed above, we propose that psychological resilience could mediate the relationship between flow and well-­being. Not only does resilience enable individuals to bounce back from adversity but it also facilitates personal growth through adversities (Jackson et al., 2007). Upon approaching the end of the COVID-­19 pandemic, university students returned to campus but were still subjected to daily severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-­CoV-­2) nucleic acid testing (Leung et al., 2023), resulting in extended queues that encroached upon their study time and disrupted regular life patterns. Such circumstances, marked by restricted mobility and pervasive uncertainty regarding the pandemic's trajectory, posed psychological and daily commuting challenges. Navigating these unique adversities underscored the critical role of psychological resilience. The ability to foster resilience through flow experience can be particularly beneficial in developing more effective coping strategies and enhancing overall well-­being, especially within the university student population.

Furthermore, flow, characterized by deep concentration and full absorption, aligns with the intrinsic motivation and autonomy in SDT and can contribute to overall well-­being by strengthening psychological resilience. This synergy of flow experience and positive emotional broadening, as suggested by BBT, enhances an individual's capacity to overcome obstacles and adapt effectively to various challenges, reinforcing the findings of Xanthopoulou et al. (2012). Therefore, this study seeks to explore the potential mediating role of psychological resilience in the longitudinal relationship between flow and well-­being, especially in the context of the COVID-­19 pandemic.

While previous research has provided some insights into flow, psychological resilience and well-­being, there remains a diversity of perspectives concerning the specific nature and magnitude of the relationship between flow experience and psychological resilience (e.g., St Clair-­Thompson & Devine, 2023). Flow experience, characterized by a balanced match between skills and challenges, has the potential to contribute to the development of psychological resilience that may facilitate improved well-­being. Therefore, this study aims to elucidate the dynamic interplay over time, shedding light on the intricate relationships among flow, psychological resilience and well-­being in the context of the university student population, particularly during the challenging times of a global pandemic. The findings could inform the development and implementation of positive interventions and programmes to promote resilience and well-­being among university students in the face of major life disruptions. Specifically, utilizing a three-­wave longitudinal design within this unique pandemic context, we hypothesize that (1) flow is predictive of well-­being across time, and (2) psychological resilience plays a mediating role in the relationship between flow and well-­being across time. METHODS

Sample and procedure

A convenience sample of 506 undergraduates in Southwest China voluntarily participated in this study. They were requested to respond to our survey at the beginning (early September), in the middle (mid-­term) and after (end of December) the course of the fall semester in 2022. Among the initial selection of students, 496 provided valid responses at Time 1 (T1: 98.02%), 495 at Time 2 (T2: 97.83%) and 482 at Time 3 (T3: 95.85%). A final sample of 474 students who participated at all three points (response rate = 93.68%) was retained for analysis after excluding the invalid responses and drop out. The difference in the scores of the drained participants and the reserved participants at Time 1 were non-­significant for flow (t = .58, p = .56), psychological resilience (t = .85, p = .40) and well-­being (t = 1.11, p = .27).

It is worth noting that such a three time wave research design with short intervals employing frequent measurements presents researchers with flexibility in studying dynamic variables. By capturing variability in correlation sizes across different time lags and detecting peak effects on dependent variables at different times, we can better understand the changes in these variables (Collins & Graham, 2002). Besides, shorter measurement intervals are beneficial for capturing rapid changes in psychological states such as flow, psychological resilience and well-­being. This frequency strikes a balance between capturing the changes in the dynamic variables of interest and allowing sufficient time for these changes to occur and be measured. This research design aligns with prior studies, such as the work of Wang et al. (2021), who analysed three successive data waves over a 1-­month interval during the COVID-­19 outbreak. Their findings provided valuable insights into the relationship between well-­being and anxiety in the context of an evolving pandemic, supporting the rationale for a relatively short interval in the present study.

It is also worth mentioning that the data collection was conducted at the end of the COVID-­19 pandemic in China. At this juncture, universities were still enforcing strict measures and regulations, including mandatory daily SARS-­CoV-­2 nucleic acid testing, albeit with the allowance for physical presence in classrooms. This distinctive backdrop and the transition period that included adaptations in learning tasks presented a unique opportunity to scrutinize the impact of study–life conflict experiences during this phase of adjustment and explore their influence on the stability of flow experience, psychological resilience and well-­being in this evolving scenario.

Measurements

The tools for measuring our study variables on flow, psychological resilience and well-­being were validated self-­report measures. All questionnaire items tailored to refer specifically to the university context were translated into Chinese and back translated into English (Brislin, 1986) by two bilingual speakers.

Flow

The 7-­item Swedish Flow Proneness Questionnaire (SFPQ, Ullén et  al.,  2012) was adopted in the context of the present study. It corresponded to the dimensions of flow as described by Csikszentmihalyi (1975) and was designed to assess the frequency of participants' flow experience for each of the three distinct contexts (i.e., work, maintenance and leisure). The SFPQ has demonstrated robust reliability and validity across different cultural contexts and diverse populations, including university students (Mao et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2021). The sample items in this study include: ‘When you engage in study activities, how often do you experience a sense of complete control?’ and ‘When you engage in study activities, how often do you find yourself completely focused?’ According to recommendations, all factor loadings based on the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) should ideally be .5 or above (Hair et al., 2006). In the present work, one item (‘When you engage in study activities, how often do you feel bored?’) yielding lower CFA factor loadings (.16 at Time 1, .20 at Time 2 and .14 at Time 3) was excluded from further analysis, therefore, yielding six items representing typical flow characteristics for a single dimension. Each item was measured on a 5-­ point Likert scale ranging from ‘never’ to ‘almost every day’. Higher scores on the SFPQ indicated a greater propensity to experience flow. Cronbach's coefficient alpha was  .84 at Time 1, .93 at Time 2 and .94 at Time 3.

Psychological resilience

Based on the Psychological Capital (PsyCap) Questionnaire, we adopted its resilience dimension of the Resilience Trait Scale developed by Luthans et al. (2007), which comprises six items. This scale has been previously validated in the university student population (Mao, Xie, et al., 2023), demonstrating its appropriateness for assessing psychological resilience in this particular group. The PsyCap resilience dimension assesses individuals' ability to bounce back from adversity and setbacks, and higher scores indicate greater psychological resilience. Sample items from the PsyCap resilience dimension include ‘I feel I can handle many things at a time in my study’ and ‘I can get through difficult times in study because I have experienced difficulty before’. We deleted one item (‘When I have a setback at work, I have trouble recovering from it, moving on’) for its lower factor loadings (.07 at Time 1, .41 at Time 2 and  .35 at Time 3) in the present study. The responses of participants were registered based on five finalized items of the scale. Each item is measured on a 7-­point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’. Cronbach's coefficient alpha was .85 at Time 1, .95 at Time 2 and .96 at Time 3.

The Multidimensional Psychological Well-­Being (MPWB) Scale developed by Ruggeri et al. (2020) was employed in this study. The MPWB scale is a relatively new 10-­item self-­report measure that allows researchers and policymakers to measure well-­being both as a composite construct and at the level of its fundamental dimensions, such as vitality, resilience and positive relationships. Sample items include ‘Most days I feel a sense of accomplishment from what I do’ and ‘I generally feel what I do in my life is valuable and worthwhile’. In the present study, we removed one item (‘When things go wrong in my life, it generally takes a long time to get back to normal’) due to its lower factor loadings (.06 at Time 1,  .30 at Time 2 and .33 at Time 3). Therefore, the responses of participants were registered based on nine finalized items of this scale, with each item measured on a 7-­point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’. Higher scores indicate greater levels of well-­being. Cronbach's coefficient alpha regarding the present sample was .92 at Time 1, .97 at Time 2 and .97 at Time 3.

Data analysis

We first performed some preliminary tests, including the descriptive statistics and correlational analyses in SPSS 22.0. We then examined construct validity, discriminant validity, common method bias, CFA, structural equation modelling (SEM) and measurement invariance within Mplus 8.0. Finally, we tested our main hypotheses by examining the cross-­lagged effects using Mplus 8.0.

Specifically, cross-­lagged panel models were used to examine the longitudinal reciprocal relationships among flow, psychological resilience and well-­being, as well as to test the mediating role of psychological resilience in the relationship between flow and well-­being. Chi-­square difference tests and bootstrapping were used to compare the fit of nested models and evaluate the significance of indirect effects respectively.

To assess the goodness of fit of the models, the following fit indices were employed (Hu & Bentler, 1999): root-­mean-­square error of approximation (RMSEA < .08), the standardized root-­mean-­ square residual (SRMR < .08), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI > .90), comparative fit index (CFI > .90) and finally, a chi-­square/degrees of freedom ratio (χ2 /df < 5). Overall, these statistical procedures and fit indices ensure the reliability and validity of the data analyses and support the conclusions drawn from the study.

A multi-­factor CFA was conducted to check whether there was an adequate level of discriminant validity and convergent validity. The CFA tested the degree to which the measured items in the research model represented their respective latent flow, psychological resilience and well-­being variables. Chi-­ square difference tests were also conducted to compare the three-­factor model with the two-­factor and single-­factor models. Results indicated that the goodness-­of-­fit indices of the three-­factor model were acceptable, and it had the best fit among all the other models in three waves. The CFA results provided evidence of the multidimensional research measurement model's construct validity and discriminant validity and that collinearity was not a problem in specifying the mediation structural model. Tests of common method bias

Given that the participants self-­reported all survey items in this study, Harman's single-­factor method was employed to test for potential common method bias. This technique is predicated on the assumption that a single latent factor accounts for a significant majority of the covariance among the measures (Podsakoff et al., 2003). To this end, a one-­factor CFA was performed on the data gathered from each survey wave. The results revealed unsatisfactory fit indices for the one-­factor models at each time point (T1: χ2 = 1329.74, df = 170; RMSEA = .12; CFI = .80; TLI = .78; T2: χ2 = 1651.35, df = 170; RMSEA = .13; CFI = .87; TLI = .85; T3: χ2 = 1523.90, df = 170; RMSEA = .13; CFI = .89; TLI = .88), implying that a single latent factor does not account for the bulk of the covariance among the measures. This finding suggests that common method bias is unlikely to be a substantial issue in this study. As a result, the results of the analyses should give a trustworthy and valid reflection of the connections between the study variables.

Tests of measurement invariance

Measurement invariance tests were conducted across the three constructs of flow, psychological resilience and well-­being to examine the consistency of the measurements through time. Specifically, following Little et al. (2007), the configural invariance, metric invariance and scalar invariance were evaluated in a gradual manner. Configural invariance constrains measurement errors associated with the same item to equivalence across time. Metric invariance constrains item factor loadings onto the latent variables to equivalence across time. Scalar invariance constrains the intercepts of the items to equivalence across time. Our assessment of measurement invariance was guided by the criteria recommended by Cheung and Rensvold (2002), which stipulate that a decrease in CFI of less than 0.01 and an increase in RMSEA of less than 0.015 would support invariance across time. In order to be able to interpret the structural paths in the cross-­lagged models, the measurement model should at least hold the level of configural and weak factorial invariance (Little et al., 2007; Stiglbauer & Batinic, 2012; Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). The results indicated that psychological resilience showed strong measurement invariance over time, satisfying the criteria for scalar invariance (∆CFI = .003, ∆RMSEA = .000). Likewise, both flow (∆CFI = .009, ∆RMSEA = .005) and well-­being (∆CFI = 0.004, ∆RMSEA = 0.001) demonstrated good fit and met the criteria for metric invariance. These results provide strong evidence that our measures were stable and consistent across the three data collection points, reinforcing the credibility of the longitudinal analyses to follow. Drawing on flow theory, self-­determination theory (SDT) and broaden-­and-­build theory (BBT), we employed a three-­wave cross-­lagged model to investigate the temporal dynamics and potential relationships between flow experience and well-­being, with a keen interest in exploring their possible underlying mechanism by testing the mediating effect of psychological resilience in a sample of Chinese university students. We found that flow was predictive of well-­being through psychological resilience across three time waves.

General discussion

First, our autoregression analysis demonstrated the inherent stability and cumulative effects of flow, psychological resilience and well-­being among university students. This continuity indicates that past experiences of flow, resilience and well-­being are reliable predictors of future occurrences. Thus, students who have previously shown high levels of flow or psychological resilience are likely to continue doing so, resulting in enhanced well-­being (Tse et al., 2021). Thus, it is critical to consistently instil these attributes to reap their cumulative benefits over time. This finding is particularly pertinent given the protracted epidemic and its associated stressors. It emphasizes the value of sustaining such positive attributes in university students to promote resilience and overall well-­being.

Flow and well-­being

Second, our findings demonstrated an indirect relationship between flow and well-­being, which enriches our comprehension of the complex dynamics between experiencing flow and well-­being (Tse et al., 2021). Interestingly, the influence of flow on well-­being appears to be facilitated through various psychological constructs, such as self-­efficacy or self-­esteem (Mao et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021). This mediation aligns with the principles of SDT, which emphasizes the role of autonomous motivation and psychological need satisfaction in fostering well-­being.

Students frequently engaged in flow-­inducing activities are more likely to strengthen their psychological resilience, subsequently improving their well-­being. This process mirrors BBT, which suggests that positive experiences broaden one's awareness and build lasting personal resources. As suggested by our study, the indirect and long-­term impact of flow experience on students' well-­being extends beyond their immediately rewarding nature, nurturing important psychological constructs (Tse et al., 2021).

Thus, the absence of a direct relationship between flow and well-­being can be attributed to the interplay of these mediating variables, a concept that resonates with flow theory. Flow experience, while inherently satisfying, also contributes to well-­being through a more complex mechanism involving personal growth and psychological resilience. This insight emphasizes the necessity for future research into these mediating psychological constructs and their pivotal roles in connecting flow experience with well-­being, further exploring the intersections of these theoretical frameworks.

Psychological resilience as a mediator

Lastly, this study further revealed that psychological resilience fully mediated the relationship between flow experience and well-­being among university students. This aligns with and expands on the established direct relationship between flow and psychological resilience (Mao, Kang, et al., 2023) and between psychological resilience and well-­being (Chue & Cheung, 2021).

Importantly, our study also sheds light on the ‘psychological resilience to flow’ pathway, suggesting that resilience may not only be a consequence but also a precursor to experiencing flow. Resilient

In the face of pandemic-­related stressors, students have increasingly relied on psychological resilience as a crucial tool to navigate these challenges. During this period, marked by the final stretch of intense pandemic control measures, there was a pervasive sense of fatigue and irritation among the student population towards the ongoing pandemic, adding to the urgency of fostering resilience for their academic and personal pursuits (Mao et al., 2020). Our findings suggest that flow experience, as indicated by such factors as the congruence between challenges and skills, clarity of objectives, heightened focus and control over the task, significantly contribute to building this resilience. Students who engage in flow-­inducing activities, such as adapting to remote learning or mastering new educational technologies, demonstrate a marked increase in psychological resilience. This, in turn, is instrumental in managing stress, sustaining positive emotions and finding purpose during the pandemic (Smith & Yang, 2017; Tan et al., 2021), which are vital components of well-­being. The ongoing stress of the pandemic underscores the value of consistently engaging in flow activities – activities that are engaging, not overly challenging or boring and provide a clear sense of achievement – to bolster psychological resilience. This approach creates a positive feedback loop, enhancing students' overall well-­being.

Our findings also dovetail with several established psychological theories. They support SDT by illustrating that activities fostering autonomy, competence and relatedness – key elements of flow experience – are conducive to building psychological resilience and well-­being (Ryan & Deci, 2001). Furthermore, they align with flow theory, emphasizing the significance of the challenge–skill balance (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975). As indicated by our survey items, engaging in tasks that are well-­matched to students' skill levels can facilitate flow, which, in turn, enhances resilience and promotes well-­being. Additionally, our results resonate with BBT, indicating that positive emotions from flow experience can expand an individual's coping strategies, bolstering psychological resilience and well-­being (Fredrickson, 1998).

Finally, the utilization of a three-­wave longitudinal design in our study, with its focus on cross-­ lagged effects, offers a more comprehensive, robust insight than a cross-­sectional or two-­wave design. This approach allows for a clearer understanding of the temporal and dynamic interplay among flow, psychological resilience and well-­being.

Implications

This study offers significant theoretical implications for understanding the intricate connections among flow, psychological resilience and well-­being in university students. We extend prior research using a three-­wave longitudinal approach by establishing a predictive model where flow experience and psychological resilience longitudinally influence well-­being. This sequence does not work in reverse. Particularly, this study underscores the role of psychological resilience as a potential outcome of flow experience and a critical bridge to multidimensional well-­being. Psychological resilience has the potential to act as a buffer against the unique challenges caused by the pandemic, such as social isolation, the strain of adjusting to remote learning and the anxiety associated with constant health concerns (Ye et al., 2020). Moreover, while our research was carried out near the end of the pandemic context, its conclusions are applicable to the present ‘post-­pandemic’ era. The resilience building discussed in our study is not limited to stresses associated with pandemics but is relevant to a broad spectrum of adversity. Amid a time characterized by frequent uncertainties and quick fluctuations, understanding how to cultivate psychological resilience remains crucial. Therefore, this study offers valuable perspectives on promoting well-­being among university students and potentially other groups, even as the world navigates the evolving challenges of the post-­pandemic landscape.

Limitations

While these findings carry noteworthy theoretical and practical implications, it is inevitable that the study also comes with certain limitations that warrant further discussion and future investigation. First, an important limitation of this study pertains to the use of SFPQ as a proxy for measuring flow. While the SFPQ is a validated and widely accepted tool, it is designed to assess the likelihood or propensity of individuals to engage in flow states during various life domains. Although subtle, this distinction holds significant implications for interpreting and generalizing the present findings. Therefore, these results should be considered as reflective of a tendency towards flow experience. It suggests that future research would benefit from incorporating direct measures of flow alongside measures of flow proneness to gain a more comprehensive understanding of this complex phenomenon.

Second, the participants involved in this study were all undergraduate students from the same university in Southwest China, which might limit the generalizability of our findings to other groups with different cultural and educational backgrounds, particularly in the global context of the COVID-­19 pandemic. Different cultural contexts may also present different responses to the pandemic, which could influence the relationships among flow, psychological resilience and well-­being. Future research should involve individuals from various colleges and cultural backgrounds to ensure the robustness and applicability of our findings across different settings.

Conclusion

Drawing on flow, self-­determination and broaden-­and-­build perspectives, this study, conducted amidst the COVID-­19 pandemic, employed a three-­wave longitudinal design intended to examine the longitudinal reciprocal relationships between flow, psychological resilience and well-­being among university students. The research emphasizes the importance of facilitating the human experience of flow in promoting students' well-­being during adversity via psychological resilience.
