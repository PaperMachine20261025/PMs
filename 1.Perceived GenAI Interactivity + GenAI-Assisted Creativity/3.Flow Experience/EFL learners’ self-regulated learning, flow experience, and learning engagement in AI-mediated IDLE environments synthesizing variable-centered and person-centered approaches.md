EFL learners’ self-regulated learning, flow experience, and learning engagement in AI-mediated IDLE environments: synthesizing variable-centered and person-centered approaches

Gang Yanga and Chenxi Du b

aSchool of Foreign Languages and Literature, Shandong University, Jinan, People’s Republic of China; bSchool of International Studies, University of International Business and Economics, Beijing, People’s Republic of China

ABSTRACT Purpose: The present study synthesizes the variable-centered and person-centered approaches to examine the mediating role of flow experience in the relationship between SRL and AI-mediated IDLE learning engagement, identify latent proﬁles of AI-mediated IDLE SRL, and compare flow experience and learning engagement across different SRL proﬁles. Methodology: Questionnaires were collected online among 1956 college students in China to measure self-regulated learning, flow experience, and learning engagement. Mediation analysis, latent proﬁle analysis, as well as BCH method were adopted for data analysis. Findings: Mediation analysis results demonstrate that flow experience mediated in the relationship between SRL and AI- mediated IDLE learning engagement. Latent Proﬁle Analysis results reveal four AI-mediated IDLE SRL proﬁles. In addition, BCH results demonstrate that all four proﬁles differed signiﬁcantly in learners’ flow experience and AI-mediated IDLE learning engagement, with the ‘all-high SRL proﬁle’ and ‘all-low SRL proﬁle’ displaying the highest and lowest level of engagement, respectively. Originality: The study provides empirical proof for an extended SRL model with multidimensional engagement (emotional, behavioral, cognitive) as the outcome of SRL and with flow experience as a mediator in the relationship between SRL and engagement. At last, it adds literature on the distribution and nature of SRL proﬁles in an AI-mediated IDLE environment. In addition, the study synthesizes variable-centered and person-centered approaches to compensate for the shortcoming of over-reliance on one single approach.

However, students may suffer from disengagement due to low feedback literacy (Zhan and Yan 2025), the digital divide (Bentley et al. 2024), and over-dependence on AI-generated content (N. While previous studies have primarily employed variable-centered approaches to examine how SRL predicts learning engagement – potentially mediated by flow, a sole dependence on such an approach generalizes the research results to a homogeneous population while ignoring the within-person differences (Bergman and Andersson 2010). Since students’ SRL levels differ across genders (X. Liu et al. 2021), grades (Guo 2020), ethical backgrounds, and urbanity statuses (Zheng et al. 2023), college students who are heterogeneous in these aspects may exhibit differences in how they self-regulate their learning, especially in GenAI-mediated IDLE environments that demand high autonomy (Lyrigkou 2019). The relationship between SRL and flow experience, learning engagement may be not linear or uniform across different SRL subgroups. Therefore, a personcentered approach that features the sample’s complexity and heterogeneity (Bergman and Andersson 2010) is needed to unravel the underlying proﬁles of learners’ SRL. In addition, although studies have examined whether students across different SRL proﬁles differ in terms of L2 self-efficacy, motivation, and proﬁciency (Chen et al. 2023; Chen, Wang, and Kim 2020; Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022), it remains unknown whether students’ flow experience and AI-mediated IDLE learning engagement differ across SRL proﬁles.

To address the aforementioned research gaps, the present study integrates the variable-centered and person-centered approaches to examine the mediating role of flow experience in the relationship between SRL and learning engagement, identify the SRL proﬁles and their relationship to flow experience and learning engagement in an AI- mediated IDLE setting. The present study not only unravels the interplay between SRL, flow experience, and learning engagement in the unique AI-mediated IDLE setting but also adds evidence to the limited SRL proﬁle research.

2. Literature review

2.1. Theoretical framework: model of student motivation and SRL in the college classroom and flow theory

The relationship between SRL and engagement can be conceptualized by the model of student motivation and SRL in the college classroom (Pintrich and Zusho 2007), which speciﬁes that individual and contextual antecedents influence learners’ self-regulatory processes, which, in turn, translate to academic outcomes. According to the model, learners’ SRL leads to high levels of efforts and persistence, which are manifestations of behavioral engagement. This is because students with high levels of SRL employ metacognitive and cognitive strategies effectively, which empowers them to cope with learning difficulties and get involved in the learning process effectively.

In addition to the model of student motivation and SRL in the college classroom, flow theory (Csikszentmihalyi 2013; Shernoff et al. 2003), which describes flow as a psychological state of deep focus and enjoyment and proposes its antecedents and impacts, is adopted as the theoretical framework to hypothesize the SRL-flow-learning engagement

Flow theory can also explain the relationship between flow experience and learning engagement. When students are immersed in a flow experience, they are more likely to possess high levels of interest, concentration, and enjoyment in learning (Shernoff et al. 2003), all of which are potential indicators of learning engagement. In addition, since the flow experience is an intrinsically rewarding experience, individuals are naturally motivated to replicate, which, in turn, drives them to engage in further learning activities, leading to sustained learning engagement.

Although flow theory originates from traditional learning contexts, a multitude of studies have extended its applicability to technology-enhanced and AI-mediated learning environments. Recent studies have demonstrated that augmented reality and GenAI can effectively reduce learners’ cognitive load, trigger flow experiences, and enhance learning performance (Chen and Mokmin 2024; Chien, Chan, and Hou 2025; Hsiao and Chang 2024; S. J. Shi, Li, and Zhang 2024). These ﬁndings suggest that flow theory provides a robust framework for exploring how learners interact with AI technologies and sustain engagement in AI-mediated learning contexts.

While SRL and flow theory do originate from different motivational traditions – with SRL emphasizing deliberate cognitive and metacognitive strategies and flow theory emphasizing spontaneous immersion, the two processes of SRL and flow are complementary rather than contradictory. In fact, empirical studies such as Tomas and Poroto (2023) have supported the integration of the two theoretical frameworks. In addition, Hui et al. (2025), in their review article, illustrated the alignment between the theories of SRL and flow, according to which, the emergence of flow does not arise from deliberate efforts, but from subjective interpretation of effort as manageable or minimal. In other words, the intentional use of SRL strategies (e.g. goal setting, environment structuring, feedback seeking, and skill-challenge balance) can facilitate the emergence of flow as learners can perceive their efforts as minimal when the SRL strategies align with their goals.

2.2. SRL and learning engagement

Learning engagement, characterized by learners’ level of devotion to the learning activities, consists of behavioral, cognitive, and emotional engagement (Fredricks, Blumenfeld,

One of the possible predictors of learning engagement is SRL, deﬁned as the ability with which learners voluntarily manage their learning experience to set learning goals, adopt learning strategies, and monitor learning progress using their motivational, meta-cognitive, and cognitive resources (Zimmerman 1989; Zimmerman and Schunk 2011). Although both SRL and cognitive engagement involve deliberate and goaldirected mental efforts, they differ from each other in scope and phase. Cognitive engagement refers to learners’ use of cognitive and metacognitive strategies and emphasizes depth of learning (Li and Lajoie 2022) while SRL encompass a broader and cyclical process where learners proactive plan, monitor and control their learning before, during and after tasks and engage in environment regulation (Zimmerman 1989).

In fact, the predictive impact of SRL on learning engagement has empirical evidence in various technology-enhanced learning environments ranging from synchronous online learning environments (Dai, Li, and Jia 2022; Stan et al. 2022; X. Wang et al. 2022), asynchronous online learning environment (Kim et al. 2021), flipped learning (Doo et al. 2021), to the blended learning environment.

The relationship between SRL and learning engagement in AI-mediated IDLE environments may differ from that in other technology-enhanced learning environments due to the self-directed nature of IDLE setting and the unique affordances and barriers of the GenAI tools in the IDLE environments. For one thing, building on the four-dimension framework of out-of-class L2 learning (formality, location, pedagogy, locus of control) (Benson and Reinders 2011), scholars have acknowledged that the IDLE context stands in contrast to the formal, technology-enhanced classroom environments subject to school rules and teacher monitorship (Lee and Lee 2019; Nguyen and Stracke 2021), provides learning resources and affordances complementary to the formal setting (Lai 2015), and poses a higher requirement on students’ agency (Lyrigkou 2019). For another thing, the potential of the setting in promoting SRL and engagement can be both augmented and reduced by the application of GenAI tools. Studies have revealed that GenAI tools are effective in enhancing learners’ levels of SRL (Lo, Hew, and Jong 2024; Ng, Tan, and Leung 2024). Empowered by the GenAI tools, the AI-mediated IDLE learning environment enables learners to obtain diverse, personalized learning feedback and resources (Chan and Hu 2023; Soyoof et al. 2023), experience higher levels of enjoyment and self-conﬁ- dence and lower levels of learning anxiety (Ng, Tan, and Leung 2024; Tai and Chen 2024), to compensate their originally disadvantageous non-native status (Liu and Darvin 2024), all of which may enhance students’ SRL and learning engagement. 2.3. Flow experience as a mediator in the relationship between SRL and learning engagement

Empirical studies have revealed that the relationship between SRL and learning engagement is mediated by transactional distance (Doo et al. 2021), teacher presence (Lyu 2024), and academic hope (Izadpanah and Rezaei 2022). Given the complexity of the relationship between SRL and learning engagement, more empirical studies are necessitated to identify other psychological factors as mediators.

The mediating role of flow experience in the relationship between SRL and learning engagement has empirical support. Concerning the SRL-flow experience path, scant education studies have demonstrated that SRL directly impacts flow experience in online learning environment (Joo, Joung, and Kim 2014) and virtual reality learning environment (Z. Liu et al. 2023). In addition, regarding the flow experience-engagement path, limited education studies conﬁrm the role of flow experience in predicting learning engagement (Chung and Pan 2023; Gao, Wang, and Reynolds 2025; Mesurado, Cristina Richaud, and José Mateo 2016). Among these, Gao, Wang, and Reynolds (2025) collected questionnaire data from 512 Chinese EFL learners and revealed that their flow state signiﬁcantly predicted their learning environment in the IDLE context. Despite the aforementioned empirical evidence, the mediating role of flow experience in the relationship between SRL and learning engagement remains unexamined. Also, considering the AI-mediated context has the potential to facilitate flow (S. J. Shi, Li, and Zhang 2024), SRL (Chiu 2024; Ng, Tan, and Leung 2024), the relationship in the AI-mediated IDLE environments awaits further scholarly attention.

2.4. Exploring the relationship between SRL, flow experience, and online learning engagement: adopting the person-centered approach

In the SLA ﬁeld, the person-centered approach remains in its infancy in the SRL research. Existing person-centered research on SRL proﬁles has identiﬁed 3 SRL proﬁles in English reading (Chen et al. 2023), English writing (Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022), English learning (Chen, Wang, and Kim 2020) as well as in learning Chinese as a foreign language (Lin, Gong, and Xu 2021). All the research has demonstrated that in L2 learning, learners use a variety of SRL strategies at the same time. In particular, the students were classiﬁed into three SRL proﬁles: high SRL proﬁle (highest level of SRL in total and all sub-dimensions), moderate SRL proﬁle (moderate level of SRL in total as well as all sub-dimensions), and low SRL proﬁle (lowest level of SRL in total and all sub-dimensions) (Chen, Wang, and Kim 2020; Chen et al., 2023; Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022; Lin, Gong, and Xu 2021).

All four studies (Chen, Wang, and Kim 2020; Chen et al., 2021; Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022; Lin, Gong, and Xu 2021) were conducted among college language learners in China and concur that the moderate SRL proﬁle accounts for the largest proportion of the sample. However, they differ from each other in the lowest-ranking SRL proﬁle. One possible reason is the context-speciﬁc nature of the SRL proﬁle. While the low SRL proﬁle

Due to the distinct SRL questionnaires employed, existing SRL proﬁle studies emphasized different facets of SRL proﬁles. Three proﬁle studies (Chen et al., 2023; Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022; Lin, Gong, and Xu 2021) employed more comprehensive framework to reveal SRL proﬁles of distinct motivational, cognitive, metacognitive and affective strategies while Chen, Wang, and Kim (2020) focused on the cognitive and metacognitive domains of SRL. In addition, all existing SRL proﬁle studies relied on SRL questionnaires that were originally developed in the face-to-face learning environments.

With factor structures of goal setting (specifying speciﬁc learning goals), task strategies (cognitive strategies such as summarizing, outlining), time management (the ability to plan and allocate time effectively), help seeking (resorting to help in face of difficulties), environment structuring (creating a physical or digital learning environment conducive to learning), and self-evaluation (monitoring and evaluation of one’s learning process and performance), the Online Self-regulated Learning Questionnaire (OSLQ) developed by Barnard et al. (2009) provides a holistic but parsimonious framework to reflect the three phases of Zimmerman’s (1989) SRL cycle and emphasizes the cognitive and metacognitive domains of SRL. In addition, with a factor of ‘environment structuring’, the questionnaire reflects the unique learner responsibility in online and blended learning environments and therefore more aligns with the AI-mediated learning environment.

Distinguishing SRL proﬁles is necessitated because learners in various SRL proﬁles have exhibited signiﬁcant differences in motivational and affective factors such as self-concept, learning motivation, attitude (Ning and Downing 2015), L2 self-efficacy (Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022), L2 intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (Chen et al. 2023), as well as learning outcomes ranging from general learning achievement (Dörrenbächer and Perels 2016), English proﬁciency (Chen, Wang, and Kim 2020), L2 writing achievement (Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022) to L2 reading scores (Chen et al. 2023).

To our knowledge, scant empirical studies have demonstrated the relationship between SRL proﬁles and learning engagement (Cleary, Slemp, and Pawlo 2021; Dai, Li, and Jia 2022). Among the US middle students, students in high SRL proﬁles tend to report a high level of math learning engagement (Cleary, Slemp, and Pawlo 2021). This result echoes the ﬁnding of Dai, Li, and Jia (2022), who revealed that among Chinese junior high students, both the high and low SRL proﬁles exhibited a signiﬁcantly positive association with their math learning engagement. However, empirical evidence regarding how EFL students’ SRL proﬁles in the AI-mediated IDLE learning environment relate to their flow experience and learning engagement is still lacking.

3. Methodology

3.1. Data collection

Convenience sampling was adopted in the study in December 2023. Two thousand students were recruited from four Chinese universities including two First-Class Universities,1

and two Non-First-Class Universities. The questionnaire was distributed to the college students via wenjuanxing, a questionnaire-collecting website (www.wjx.com), which consisted of three sections. At the ﬁrst section of the questionnaire, the participants were required to indicate where they have adopted GenAI and non-GenAI tools when learning English outside class. Those who had no such experience were excluded. At last, 1,956 valid questionnaire responses were retained.

Students were also required to take the multiple-select question to choose the scenarios where they employed GenAI and non-GenAI tools outside class. Responses indicated students employed a variety of GenAI tools (e.g. ChatGPT, yiyan, kimi) and non-GenAI tools (e.g. Baidu Translate, Baicizhan). And their purposes ranged from seeking advice on grammar and vocabulary, seeking information on English culture, practicing speaking, facilitating reading (through text summaries and follow-up questions), to supporting writing (through brainstorming, and revision).

The second section of questionnaire consisted of three adapted questionnaires of SRL, flow, and learning engagement. Since data on the independent and dependent variables collected from the same source and in the same format (e.g. self-report questionnaire) may incur common methods variance bias and distorts the actual results (Podsakoff et al. 2003), in distributing the questionnaire, the researchers ensured respondent anonymity to minimize social desirability and randomized the order the items of three questionnaires presented to participants. These two approaches help lower the likelihood of CMB triggered by response consistency bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003).

3.3. Instruments

Due to a lack of questionnaires speciﬁcally designed in the AI-mediated context to measure SRL, flow, and engagement, three established questionnaires (Barnard et al. 2009; Egbert 2004; Hoi and Le Hang 2021) were adapted. Following previous practices of adapting questionnaires (Xu and Li 2024), we changed the expression ‘online learning’ and ‘English course’ to ‘AI-mediated IDLE environment’ and thoroughly reviewed the content to conﬁrm that all items were contextually appropriate for the AI-mediated IDLE environment.

The items of the questionnaires were presented on a 5-Likert scale with responses ranging from 1 to 5 indicating ‘totally disagree’ to ‘totally agree’. The three scales displayed high reliability with Cronbach’s Alpha values ranging from 0.85 to 0.91. Also, the three scales had a high construct validity, convergent validity, and composite validity because all of them had a good model ﬁt and had appropriate AVE (all higher than 0.5) and CR values (all higher than 0.7).

3.4. Data analysis

All the data underwent normality tests, and the missing data and outliers were handled before data analysis. To ensure the validity and reliability of the scales, Conﬁrmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using AMOS 24.0 and items with loadings lower than 0.6 were removed (Hair et al. 2021). The Cronbach’s Alpha value (no less than 0.7) was used to examine the scales’ reliability and Composite Reliability (CR) (no less than 0.7) and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) (no less than 0.5) were used to evaluate scales’ internal consistency reliability and convergent validity (Peterson and Kim 2013).

Before formal data analysis, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted via Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) to assess the presence of common method bias. If the percentage of variance explained by the ﬁrst unrotated principal component is less than 50%, it is generally considered that CMV is not a serious concern (Podsakoff et al. 2003).

Then, to verify the mediating role of flow experience in the relationship between AI- mediated IDLE SRL and engagement, AMOS 24.0 was used to conduct structural equation model (SEM). Fit indices were used to evaluate ﬁtness of the structural model including

To explore the SRL proﬁles, Latent Proﬁle Analysis (LPA) was conducted to identify the optimal amount of proﬁles in continuous variables (Muthén and Muthén 2017). The LPA analysis constructed 6 different models by adding the number of latent proﬁles stepwise from 1 to 6. After the models were speciﬁed, they were evaluated based on ﬁtness index, classiﬁcation quality, as well as the theoretical interpretability. A number of ﬁtness indicators were used including AIC (Akaike information criterion), BIC (Bayesian information criterion), aBIC (adjusted Bayesian information criterion), entropy, LMR (Lo-Mendell- Rubin), and BLRT (Bootstrap Likelihood ratio test) (Ferguson, Moore, and Hull 2020) and minimal proﬁle percentage (Masyn 2013; Nylund, Asparouhov, and Muthén 2007). The model ﬁtness increased when the AIC, BIC, and BIC values decreased (Tein, Coxe, and Cham 2013). A BLRT value smaller than 0.05 suggests that the k-proﬁle model better ﬁts the data than the adjacent k-1 proﬁle. As for minimal percentage of proﬁle, while proﬁles comprising no fewer than 50 cases or representing at least 5% of the total sample were recommended (Muthén and Muthén 2017), previous studies have included small proﬁles accounting for 3% to 5% of the sample (Y. Shi and Lee 2025; Zhang, Ren, and Xu 2025) if these classes are theoretically interpretable (Masyn 2013; Nylund, Asparouhov, and Muthén 2007). At last, an entropy higher than 0.8 ensures clear classiﬁcation (Nylund, Asparouhov, and Muthén 2007).

In terms of examining the differences of auxiliary variables across different proﬁles, Analyses of Variance (ANOVA) and T-tests were widely adopted in the SRL proﬁle research (Chen, Wang, and Kim 2020; Chen et al., 2021; Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022). However, such methods are biased as they do not account for the error in classiﬁcation in LPA (Asparouhov and Muthen 2014). To compensate for the shortcoming, the study employed the Bolck-Croon-Hagenaars approach (BCH) method as it considers the uncertainty of proﬁle classiﬁcation and allows the separation of latent proﬁles from auxiliary variables and therefore, is more concise and robust (Asparouhov and Muthen 2014).

5. Discussion

5.1. The mediating role of flow experience in the relationship between SRL and AI-mediated IDLE learning engagement

The present study demonstrates that flow experience served as a mediator in the relationship between SRL and learning engagement. The study enriches the existing SRL research (X. Wang et al. 2022; Zheng et al. 2016) to corroborate the predictive role of AI-mediated IDLE SRL on learning engagement. Regarding the SRL-flow experience path, the present study demonstrates a signiﬁcant association between SRL and learning engagement, which echoes the ﬁnding of extant studies (Dai, Li, and Jia 2022; Doo et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2021; Stan et al. 2022). While the previous studies were conducted in mathematics (Dai, Li, and Jia 2022) and general education (Doo et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2021; Stan et al. 2022), this study veriﬁes the positive impact of SRL in the SLA ﬁeld. In terms of the flow experience-engagement path, this study echoes the ﬁndings of previous studies (Chung and Pan 2023; Mesurado, Cristina Richaud, and José Mateo 2016) and extends the positive association between flow experience and learning engagement to an AI-mediated IDLE context. That echoes Gao, Wang, and Reynolds (2025)’s study which revealed that Chinese EFL learners’ flow state signiﬁcantly predicted their learning engagement in the IDLE context. That can be attributed to the crucial role of flow in driving intrinsic motivation and mitigating pressure (Gao, Wang, and Reynolds 2025). In this way, the present study contributes to verifying flow-learning engagement association in the AI-mediated IDLE context and underscores the crucial role of flow experience in promoting L2 learning engagement.

The study adds to the SRL research by identifying flow experience as a mediator in the relationship between AI-mediated IDLE SRL and learning engagement, which further demonstrates the stability of the SRL-flow-learning engagement nexus across various learning contexts, ranging from face-to-face, blended, flipped, online, to AI-mediated IDLE setting. By effectively self-regulating the learning experience, learners can enter a transient flow experience, which has the potential to be converted into learners’ prolonged learning engagement. The result can be explained by model of student motivation and SRL in the college classroom (Pintrich and Zusho 2007) and the flow theory. In particular, learners who can effectively regulate their learning process can better align their skills to the task difficulty, and may enter into the flow experience easier. In addition, individuals with a high flow experience tend to engage actively in learning to sustain or rekindle the flow experience (Shernoff et al. 2003).

5.2. EFL learners’ AI-mediated IDLE SRL proﬁles

The present study identiﬁed four SRL proﬁles among EFL college students: ‘all-low SRL proﬁle’, ‘all-intermediary SRL proﬁle’, ‘all-intermediary-high SRL proﬁle’, as well as ‘allhigh SRL proﬁle’. The 4-proﬁle result identiﬁed in the study differs from other SRL proﬁle research (Chen, Wang, and Kim 2020; Chen et al., 2023; Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022), which identiﬁed three SRL proﬁles. This may be accounted for by the setting of the present study, which may have contributed to a greater variability in the dataset. The present study is set in the AI-mediated IDLE English learning environment rather than the F2F learning environment in previous studies (Chen, Wang, and Kim 2020; Chen et al., 2021; Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022). While the AI-mediated IDLE setting offers personalized scaffolding that can enhance SRL (Ng, Tan, and Leung 2024), it simultaneously requires learners to exercise higher levels of SRL (e.g. planning and monitoring) due to a lack of monitorship and higher agency of AI (Chiu 2024). Therefore, the uniqueness and complexity of the AI-mediated IDLE learning environment may make the differences in SRL more pronounced and therefore, lead to more SRL proﬁles than the F2F settings.

Among the four SRL proﬁles, Proﬁle 4 (N = 955, 48.8%) (all-intermediary SRL proﬁle) accounted for the biggest proportion, which suggests the highest proportion of the investigated students developed medium levels of SRL competencies. This has echoed the statement that ‘neither the most nor the least competent SRL learners account for a signiﬁcantly larger proportion than others’ (Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022, 958). In particular, Proﬁle 3 (N = 66) (or ‘all high-SRL proﬁle’ with the highest scores in all sub-dimensions) accounted for the tiniest proportion, concurring with Lin, Gong, and Xu (2021) and Esnaashari et al. (2023), which assert that the students in the high-SRL proﬁle have the lowest percentage in the online and blended learning environment. However, the ﬁnding differs from other SRL proﬁle studies which also take place among Chinese college EFL learners (Chen et al. 2023; Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022) as these studies reveal that the low-level proﬁle makes up the smallest percentage. 5.3. Flow experience and learning engagement across different SRL proﬁles

The study shows that EFL learners’ flow experience and AI-mediated IDLE learning engagement differ signiﬁcantly across different SRL proﬁles. As learners’ SRL levels increase, their AI-mediated IDLE flow experience and learning engagement improve accordingly. In other words, the most self-regulated learners demonstrate the highest online flow experience and learning engagement. In contrast, the least self-regulated learners score lowest in online flow experience and learning engagement. In accordance with the model of student motivation and SRL in the college classroom (Pintrich and Zusho 2007) self-regulated learners are likely to use effective learning strategies that can lead to a more engaging learning experience. With higher SRL, learners may align their behavior with clear goals and better navigate through learning challenges and distractions, thus leading to more sustained learning engagement. In addition, students who are more capable of self-regulating their learning process can adjust their learning strategies based on the feedback they receive (Zimmerman and Schunk 2011), which may contribute to the skill-challenge balance and the entry into the flow experience state.

By complementing the previous studies that demonstrate the various SRL proﬁles vary from each other in terms of self-concept, attitude (Ning and Downing 2015), L2 selfefficacy (Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022), L2 intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (Chen et al. 2023), as well as learning outcome including L2 writing achievement, (Chen, Zhang, and Chen 2022) and L2 reading scores (Chen et al. 2023), the present study further underscores the necessity of identifying distinct SRL proﬁles by linking SRL proﬁles to different levels of flow experience and learning engagement.

6. Conclusion and implications

To conclude, the study integrates both variable-centered and person-centered approaches to investigate the relationship between EFL learners’ SRL strategies, flow experience, and AI-mediated IDLE learning engagement. The mediation analysis results demonstrate that flow experience was a mediator in the relationship between SRL and AI-mediated IDLE learning engagement. The Latent Proﬁle Analysis results identiﬁes four proﬁles of AI-mediated IDLE SRL. In addition, BCH results demonstrate that all four proﬁles differed signiﬁcantly from each other in terms of learners’ flow experience and AI-mediated IDLE learning engagement.

Theoretically, the study contributes to the existing SRL models by delineating the relationship between SRL and learning engagement. While the existing SRL models speciﬁed behavioral engagement as the outcome of SRL (Pintrich and Zusho 2007) because they try to ‘differentiate between cognition, affect, and behavior’ when mentioning engagement, this study provides empirical proof for an extended SRL model with multidimensional engagement (emotional, behavioral, cognitive) as the outcome of SRL. Besides, the study complements the model by validating the mediating role of flow experience in the relationship between SRL and engagement. The study further distinguishes the nature and roles of SRL, flow and learning engagement which exhibit some degree of overlap. Moreover, by revealing the distribution SRL proﬁles in the AI- mediated IDLE environment different from previous SRL proﬁle studies in non-AI- mediated environment, the study sheds light on the context-speciﬁc nature of SRL proﬁles. At last, by integrating variable-centered and person-centered approaches, the present study yields a nuanced understanding of the interplay of SRL, flow, and learning engagement in an AI-mediated IDLE setting.

Pedagogically, considering the mediating role of flow experience in the relationship between SRL and learning engagement, English educators can recommend a variety of AI tools (e.g. ChatGPT for ideation, online dictionary for word checking, doubao for practicing pronunciation) and explicitly instruct on their functions and usage. In this way, EFL learners can base their English proﬁciency and perceived difficulty of AI-mediated IDLE learning to select suitable AI tools when learning English outside class and facilitate skill-challenge balance, which is a critical component of flow. In addition, English teachers can encourage students to create weekly AI-mediated IDLE checklists, document their learning progress, and reflect their learning outcomes. In this way, students’ SRL strategies can be enhanced, therefore facilitating flow and converting flow state into stable learning engagement.

In addition, the ﬁnding that the high SRL proﬁle demonstrated the highest levels of flow and engagement further underscores the signiﬁcance of SRL in the AI-mediated IDLE context. Given the smallest proportion of learners in ‘All-high-SRL’ proﬁle (Proﬁle 3) and the relatively low scores in time management and self-evaluation among the most prevalent proﬁles (Proﬁle 2 and Proﬁle 4), English teachers can provide targeted SRL scaffolding to support students’ transitions into the ‘All-high-SRL’ proﬁles. Aside from English teachers, the ﬁndings of the study also provide suggestions for AI tool developers and designers. They can incorporate SRL- and flow-facilitating features into AI interface such as adaptive prompts, time recorder and minder, intelligent feedback, and metacognitive questions, which have the potential to address learners’ shortage in time management and self-evaluation in the AI-mediated IDLE setting and maximize the affordances of AI tools.

7. Limitations and future research directions

The study also has several limitations. First, since the study only used self-report data collected at one single time point, future research may use online user analytic data such as logging time and frequency, and online interactions with learning materials and other participants to measure SRL, flow experience, and AI-mediated IDLE learning engagement. In addition, future studies can adopt longitudinal or intervention designs to establish the causal relationship between the three variables. Second, the study does not reveal the determining factors of AI-mediated IDLE SRL proﬁles due to the limited research scope. Future studies can unravel the determinants of SRL proﬁles. In addition, considering that SRL is context-speciﬁc and factors such as culture, educational system, digital infrastructure and IT technology evolution are key factors at play, whether the distribution pattern of SRL proﬁles reported in the present study replicate in other cultural, educational or technological contexts awaits further scholarly attention. Future research should undertake cross-cultural or urban-rural comparisons, or be situated in settings involving new AI technologies to uncover cultural and regional variations and to examine the impact of new technologies on both the nature and strength of relationships. Moreover, due to the distinctive affordances of GenAI and non-GenAI tools, future research would beneﬁt from a more nuanced understanding of how different types of AI tools might influence the relationship between SRL, flow, learning engagement. At last, due to the limited scope, the present study does not examine the possible predictive effect of SRL proﬁle on flow and engagement. Future research could use latent proﬁle-moderated mediation modeling or multigroup SEM based on proﬁle membership to examine how different proﬁles of SRL influence flow and engagement and to better capture the dynamic interactions within and across proﬁles.

Despite these limitations, the study contributes to SRL research by synthesizing the person-centered and variable-centered approaches to unravel a nuanced picture of the interplay between SRL, flow, and learning engagement and to the IDLE research by shifting from non-AI-based IDLE to AI-mediated IDLE.
