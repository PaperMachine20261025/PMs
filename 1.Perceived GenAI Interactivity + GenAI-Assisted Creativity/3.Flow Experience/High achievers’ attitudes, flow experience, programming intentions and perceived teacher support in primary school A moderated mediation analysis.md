To improve programming performance for a larger student population, this study shed light on the commonalities of high achievers in programming classes. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have investigated high achievers in primary school programming classes. This study adapted the hedonic-motivation system adoption model to identify high achievers’ perceived attitudes, flow experience, programming intentions and perceived teacher support. With a response rate of 72%, 54 primary schools participated in the study. A survey was conducted and four hundred and thirty-two students were included in the moderated mediation analysis. The results showed that high achievers’ attitudes towards programming positively related to their flow experience, which further positively associated with their programming intentions. In addition, teacher support moderated the relationship between high achievers’ attitudes and their flow experience; such that when teacher support increased, the flow experience was weaker for those with comparatively higher positive attitudes. The practical implications of the findings and future research directions are discussed.

Since Wing (2006) coined computational thinking (CT), programming classes have been found to be effective in developing the required basic cognitive skills for young students’ CT skills. CT defines a process that involves “solving problem, designing systems, and understanding human behaviour, by drawing on the concepts fundamental to computer science” (Wing, 2006, p. 33). Papadakis et al. (2016) found that young children’s logical reasoning faculties, such as sequencing skills, were facilitated by learning basic pro­ gramming concepts. Piedade et al. (2020) found that students demonstrated stronger abilities in ‘abstraction, decomposition, logical

Studies investigating outstanding learning performance have shed light on the common qualities of high achievers from different backgrounds (e.g. Kholisiyah & Indriayu, 2018; Lau & Ho, 2020). For example, Kholisiyah and Indriayu (2018) showed that high achievers in explanatory writing demonstrate strong motivation, clear goals and good responsibilities in performing their tasks. Lau and Ho (2020) found that high achievers’ science performance is associated with their high enjoyment and self-efficacy towards science learning. A better understanding of high achievers could make references for average and low-achieving students to employ learning strategies as the same as the high achievers, and for educators to adopt psychological approaches to engage students in effective learning (Afandi et al., 2021; Ras, 2013). To the best of our knowledge, few studies have investigated high achievers in primary school programming classes. Some studies have predominantly focused on efforts to offer special education to high achievers (e.g. Avcu & ER, 2020; Erümit et al., 2020). Specifically, high achievers in programming have been investigated in a gifted education context, and scholars have been more interested in creating a special curriculum for these high achievers (e.g. Erümit et al., 2020). However, other studies have indicated that non-gifted students can also achieve a programming level similar to that of gifted students through learning interventions. Thus, giftedness might not necessarily be a prerequisite for being a high achiever in programming (e.g. Adelson et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2016). More importantly, in addition to knowledge and capacity, psychological factors have been shown to play a critical role in student performance (Kholisiyah & Indriayu, 2018; Ras, 2013). Specifically, students have the free will to decide if they want to learn and how they want to learn, which in turn leads to differences in their learning behaviour and per­ formance (Awang et al., 2013). To fill a knowledge gap, this study is the first to explore high achievers’ underlying psychological interaction by investigating high achievers in programming among Hong Kong primary school students. We adapted a hedonic-motivation system adoption model (HMSAM) to capture high achievers’ attitudes, flow experience and programming in­ tentions. The HMSAM framework was adopted because it could reveal high achievers’ perceptions of the programming activities (i.e. attitudes), psychological state during programming (i.e. flow experience), and their future goals in programming (i.e. intentions). In addition, as social science scholars have been promoting the need to adopt a mediation and moderation approach to explore complex social phenomena (Baron & Kenny, 1986), a moderated mediation method was considered in this study to examine the effect of teacher support on high achievers’ psychological interaction. The objectives of this study were to answer the following two research questions: (1) What are the interrelationships between high achievers’ attitudes, flow experience and programming intentions? (2) How does teacher support interfere in these interrelationships among high achievers’ psychological interaction? By answering these questions, we can offer educators clear information to facilitate their students’ positive attitudes, flow experience and programming intentions.

2. Literature review

2.1. High achievers’ perceptions of programming

Students’ academic achievements become desynchronised because of several factors, such as gender and family background (e.g. Cimpian et al., 2020; Jungert et al., 2019). However, it is more meaningful to evaluate students’ perceptions of education than the resources they have (Sen, 1999). Resources alone (e.g. family socio-economic status [SES]) can only describe students’ opportunity structures (Sen, 1999). Even if all students are provided with the same resources, their perceptions of how to maximise their op­ portunities can vary because of individual attitude towards education (Pham, 2019). Therefore, to understand why students experience different outcomes in schools, we must identify what students consider to be valuable in their studies (Hart, 2019; Pham, 2019). In the field of programming education, studies have investigated students’ perceptions of programming. For example, Kong et al. (2018) developed a 15-item instrument to evaluate primary students’ programming empowerment, by assessing their creative and pro­ gramming self-efficacy, in addition to their perceptions of the meaningfulness and impact of programming classes. Zainal et al. (2012) also investigated the relationship between students’ perceptions of programming and their programming achievements, particularly how their perceptions interacted with their motivation and further influenced their programming skills. However, few studies have explored how high achievers perceive programming in the K-12 education context. An investigation of high performance could reveal the commonalities of these high achievers and bear successful experience to enhance learning effectiveness for a general student population (Lau & Ho, 2020). Moreover, as researchers have found that programming performance could be highly related to students’ perception and motivation (e.g. Krugel & Ruf, 2020; Nikou & Economides, 2014), uncovering the underlying psychological phe­ nomenon of the high achievers may offer educators ideas of improving average and low-achieving students’ programming perfor­ mance by facilitating their psychological variables.

Derived from the technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis, 1986), the HMSAM framework not only evaluates students’ attitudes but also their flow experience and behavioural intentions (BI) (Lowry et al., 2012) (Fig. 1). The framework combines users’ cognitive abilities (CA), attitudes and BI (Lowry et al., 2012). CA is central to Csikszentmihalyi’s (1975) flow theory, which combines various intrinsic aspects of motivation, including control, curiosity, joy and immersion. In addition, users’ attitudes are inherited from TAM and include perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU). Finally, BI retains the concepts from Davis’ (1986) TAM. By adding intrinsic motivation to TAM, the HMSAM framework was developed to ‘address users’ underlying intrinsic motivation in a process-oriented context, especially in online gaming, virtual worlds, social networking, and gamified learning environment’ (Olu­ wajana et al., 2019, p. 158). The HMSAM framework briefly indicates that an increase in positive attitudes (i.e. PU and PEOU) will increase BI and partly increase CA, respectively. The increase in CA will also increase BI. Compared with TAM, the HMSAM framework captures students’ intrinsic motivation when it measures their attitudes and intentions. The high intrinsic motivation caused by a visual programming environment helps students to achieve better learning outcomes (Krugel & Ruf, 2020). Nikou and Economides (2014) also suggested that retaining students’ intrinsic motivation is one of the most effective ways to facilitate their continuous engagement in programming. We argue that the HMSAM framework is more comprehensive and therefore suitable for this study because it allows us to capture the intrinsic motivations of high achievers in their programming activities.

2.3. Hypothesis

Attitudes represent positive or negative feelings about performing certain behaviours (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1977). Users will have a positive attitude towards using technology if they perceive it to be useful (i.e. PU) and easy to use (i.e. PEOU) (Davis, 1986). Furthermore, positive attitudes increase the intention to use technology (i.e. BI), which can further shape users’ actual performance in using the technology (Davis, 1986). Abundant evidence has shown that individuals’ attitudes directly influence their BI. For example, Fathema et al. (2015) found that faculty members’ positive attitudes towards using a learning management system – an internet technology that supports hybrid learning – led to their strong intention to use the system, which led to their actual use of the system. Chawla and Joshi (2020) found that users are more likely to adopt a mobile wallet if they have a positive attitude towards it and perceive it as beneficial and effective. More importantly, in terms of learning, when learners hold a positive attitude and perceive the tasks as authentic, an intention to achieve a cognitive goal could be automatically generated by the learners, which might achieve the ultimate goal of formal education—meaningful learning (Jonassen & Strobel, 2006). Drawing on these findings, we propose our first hypothesis:

H1. There is a positive relationship between high achievers’ attitudes and their programming intentions.

Flow experience describes an optimal psychological state of absorption in an activity (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975). Studies have observed a positive relationship between flow experience, attitudes and intention to perform the target behaviour. For example, Ho and Kuo (2010) found that when learners held positive attitudes towards e-learning, their motivations and feeling of being in control were generated intrinsically. These two dimensions had the greatest influence on flow experience. In addition, Ho and Kuo (2010) found that learners’ flow experience resulting from their positive attitudes could further lead to their intention to use e-learning to understand more content. Zhou (2012) also found that users’ positive attitudes generated by their PEOU played a main role in affecting their flow experience while using mobile banking. Users’ flow experience in turn predicted their intention to adopt online banking (Zhou, 2012). Therefore, the second and third hypotheses are the following:

H2. There is a positive relationship between high achievers’ attitudes and their flow experience.

H3. There is a positive relationship between high achievers’ flow experience and their programming intentions.

3. Methodology

3.1. Sample and procedures

In June 2021, a survey was conducted in 75 Hong Kong primary schools. These primary schools had integrated programming into their school curriculum for at least one year. An invitation email distributed the survey questionnaires to 75 co-ordinating adminis­ trators from the primary schools. An English version of the questionnaire was first developed and then translated into Chinese. Pro­ gramming courses were not formally integrated into the primary school curriculum until students entered Grade 4; therefore, we targeted Grade 5 and Grade 6 students, who are 10–11 years old (Education Bureau, 2020), because they all had at least one year of programming experience. The invitation email asked all teachers of Grade 5 and 6 programming classes to nominate one top student with distinguished performance in CT and coding from each programming class. The teachers were asked to assess their students’ in-class performance in programming instead of their academic gains in other subjects. Specifically, the teachers were required to assess students’ conceptual knowledge, digital creativity, and practical programming skills such as processing information, making strategies, and justifying solutions (Mustafa et al., 2017). The nominated students were invited to complete our questionnaire before receiving a certificate for ‘Distinguished Performance in Computational Thinking and Coding’. 54 co-ordinating teachers (72%) responded to our email and nominated 447 students. All of the students completed the questionnaire.

Among these 447 nominated students, 196 (43.8%) had completed the online CT tests on both CT concepts and CT practices in June 2021 before they participated in this study. Among these 196 students, 99 had completed the CT tests using the Scratch, which were taken by 10,150 Hong Kong students. Ninety-seven students had also completed the tests on MIT App Inventor, which was taken by 5553 students. Both CT concepts and CT practices tests were adopted from Kong and Wang’s (2019), (2021) studies. Kong and Wang’s CT concepts test (2019) and CT practices test (2021) were developed using the Evidence-Centered Design approach (ECD) and vali­ dated by a robust analytical procedure. The test items were developed in the context of Scratch and MIT App Inventor and the full marks for both tests were 17. Table 1 shows the CT test performance of these 196 nominated students. The results show that these nominated students’ average performance (Scratch: M = 13.02, SD = 3.12; MIT App Inventor: M = 10.76, SD = 3.09) was significantly greater than the average performance of the total population of test takers (Scratch: M = 8.48, SD = 4.01; MIT App Inventor: M = 7.02, SD = 3.33). The remaining 251 nominated students (56.2%) did not have the opportunity to take any CT tests because of the different curriculum implemented in their schools. Instead, they were nominated by their teachers through a prudent evaluation of their in-class programming performance and extracurricular activities. Thus, we maximised the objectivity of the teachers’ nominations and the explanatory power of our study design.

The students’ demographics are summarised in Table 2. Most of the 54 participating schools were aided schools (N = 45, 83.3%), while the remainder were direct subsidy scheme schools (N = 5, 9.3%) or government schools (N = 4, 7.4%). In terms of location, 53.7% of the schools were located in the New Territories, 31.5% were in Kowloon District and the remaining 14.8% were on Hong Kong Island. The distribution of schools by type and region closely reflected the composition of the school population in Hong Kong (Education Bureau, 2021): aided schools (88.5%), direct subsidy scheme schools (4.4%), government schools (7.1%), New Territories (44.8%), Kowloon District (36.4%) and Hong Kong Island (18.8%); thus, our sample adequately represented the population of public primary schools in Hong Kong. 3.2.2. Flow experience Flow experience is a psychological state whereby people become fully immersed in tasks for the sake of the activity itself (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975). Instead of dividing experiences into different sub-dimensions, we focused on the students’ flow experience as a general experience that interacts with their attitudes and programming intentions. This simplified combination of the four CA components in the HMSAM framework allowed our parsimonious model to retain the power to capture the students’ intrinsic moti­ vation. A 5-item scale adapted from HMSAM by Lowry et al. (2012) was used to measure flow experience.

3.2.3. Programming intentions The BI component of the HMSAM framework demonstrates the users’ intention to continuously use a certain system, which will further lead to their improved performance in using that system (Lowry et al., 2012). We used the original conceptualization of intention in the HMSAM framework to identify high achievers’ future programming intentions, which were measured using a 3-item scale adapted from HMSAM by Lowry et al. (2012).

3.3. Constructs validation

Table 5 provides the details for Cronbach’s α, composite reliability values (CR), and average variance extracted values (AVE) of the scales. All the Cronbach’s α and CR were well above the 0.7 threshold, indicating good reliability for all of the measurement scales (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Convergent validity was first confirmed as the AVE for all of the constructs were above the 0.5 threshold (Verkijika, 2018). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to further examine convergent validity using AMOS software (v. 27; IBM SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA). A chi-square (χ2)/degrees of freedom (df) value of less than 5 indicates acceptable fit (Marsh & Hocevar, 1985). Confirmatory factor index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) values greater than 0.95 also indicate an excellent fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999). For the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), a cut-off value close to 0.06 is required (Hu & Bentler, 1999). The results showed that the factor loadings of the latent variables ranged from 0.65 to 0.91 (Table 5). The fit index of the CFA model was sufficient (χ2 = 336.73, df = 158, p < .01, χ2/df = 2.13, TLI = 0.96, CFI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.05). Therefore, the scales are appropriate for the further statistical analysis.

4.2. Moderated mediation analyses

Moderated mediation, which is also termed “conditional indirect effect”, refers to a mediation model that involves a moderator in one or more paths (Preacher et al., 2007). Moderated mediation goes beyond the solely moderation or mediation model by revealing how and when a given effect occurs (Frone, 1999). Researchers adopt moderated mediation to evaluate why and under what con­ ditions variables are related to one another (Preacher et al., 2007).

4.2.1. Mediation testing The bias-corrected bootstrap method with 2000 resamples was used to investigate the mediating effect of flow experience using AMOS software (v. 27; IBM SPSS). A significant indirect effect was found in the relationship between attitudes and programming intentions through flow experience (β = .15, p < .01). This result showed that the students’ flow experience partially mediated the relationship between their attitudes and programming intentions.

4.2.2. Moderated mediation testing The proposed moderated mediation model was tested using the PROCESS macro by Hayes (2018). Model 7 of PROCESS macro is specifically designed for the model where an external factor is given to moderate the relationship between the independent variable and the mediator (Hayes, 2018). Fig. 2 presents the proposed model, in which each path represents a hypothesis.

The PROCESS macro output is presented in Table 8. The results of the moderated mediation effect generated two models. While flow was tested as a dependent variable by Model A, programming intentions was tested by Model B.

Model A tested the relationship between attitudes and flow experience. The effects of students’ gender, SES and programming experience were controlled (Table 8). Gender (β = .05, p > .05), SES (β = −0.03, p > .05) and programming experience (β = −0.03, p > .05) had no significant influence on flow experience. Attitudes (β = 0.69, p < .01) had a significant positive relationship with flow experience, supporting H2. Additionally, teacher support moderated the influence of attitudes on flow experience (β = −.08, p < .05). Specifically, the relationship between attitudes and flow experience was weaker when teacher support was higher. Therefore, H4 was partially rejected. A 55% variance in flow experience was observed, with the moderating effect of teacher support contributing 1% of the variance. The PROCESS macro further probed this interaction by examining the conditional relationship between attitudes and flow experience based on different levels of teacher support. Fig. 3 shows an interaction plot illustrating high and low levels of teacher support (1 SD above and below the mean, respectively), suggesting that higher teacher support for students with comparatively low attitudes would help them to improve their flow experience. However, lower teacher support for students with strongly positive at­ titudes could improve their flow experience.

Model B tested the relationship between attitudes and programming intentions. The effects of gender, SES and programming experience were controlled. The effects of gender (β = −.11, p > .05), SES (β = 0.02, p > .05) and programming experience (β = 0.03, p > .05) were not significant. Both attitudes (β = 0.80, p < .01) and flow experience (β = 0.32, p < .01) had a significant positive relationship with students’ programming intentions, in line with H1 and H3.

Overall, the bias-corrected percentile bootstrap results further indicated that both the direct and indirect effects of students’ at­ titudes on their programming intentions via flow experience were significant.

5. Discussion

Although several studies have investigated primary school students’ perceptions of programming (e.g. Kong et al., 2018), this study may be the first to investigate the particular sample of high achievers in programming. The factors that help create high academic achievers have been well explored (e.g. Cimpian et al., 2020; Jungert et al., 2019; Seyranian et al., 2018). However, to date, no psychological interaction that underlying high programming achievers has been identified. This study is also the first attempt to develop a moderated mediation model to evaluate the interrelationships between high achievers’ attitudes, flow experience, pro­ gramming intentions and perceived teacher support.

5.1. Theoretical contributions

The model proposed by this study is parsimonious while well retaining the trunk proposition of the HMSAM. This study contributes to the HMSAM literature by horizontally extending the model to a new context. Although the HMSAM framework has been used widely in online gaming, virtual worlds, social networking and gamified learning environments (Kim & Hall, 2019; Oluwajana et al., 2019), this study is the first to adapt the framework in a primary school programming context. By adding an external variable (i.e. teacher support), this study also vertically enriches the original HMSAM. As teachers play a key role in students’ programming activities at school, our established model is better tailored to its context and provides a new perspective for future researchers to better adopt the HMSAM.

5.1.1. The mediating role of flow experience Our results indicated that high achievers’ flow experience partially mediated the relationship between their attitudes and pro­ gramming intentions. These findings answer our first research question and enrich the literature by revealing the link between atti­ tudes and programming intentions underlying primary students’ aptitude for programming. Studies have predominantly adopted legacy models to examine students’ perceptions and behaviours in programming activities, such as the theory of reasoned action, planned behaviour, and TAM (e.g. Arpaci, 2021; Levy & Ben-Ari, 2015; Rafique et al., 2020). However, these models directly traverse from high achievers’ attitudes to their programming intentions and neglect the possible link between the two variables. We inno­ vatively adapted the HMSAM framework proposed by Lowry et al. (2012) to the primary school programming context. This allowed us to not only reveal the relationship between students’ attitudes and programming intentions but also to open the box underlying the relationship. Flow experience plays a critical role in students’ programming intentions (e.g. Krugel & Ruf, 2020; Nikou & Economides, 2014). For example, Demir and Seferoglu (2021) found that flow experience appears in students’ solo and pair programming activities and further leads to improvements in their programming skills. Kong and Wang (2021) investigated flow experience in primary students’ programming classes and found that it could be evoked in a visual programming environment. We followed the HMSAM of Lowry et al. (2012) and extrapolated that flow experience should be an intermediary in the relationship between students’ attitudes and programming intentions. This study is the first attempt in the programming literature to include the mediating role of flow experience in a model for measuring primary students’ attitudes and programming intentions.

5.1.2. Moderating role of teacher support The established moderated mediation model showed that teacher support moderated the effect of high achievers’ attitudes on their flow experience. Studies have shown that teacher support influences flow experience, such as facilitating a sense of control (e.g. Tezci et al., 2015; Yu & Singh, 2018). For example, Yu and Singh (2018) found that teacher support, especially emotional support for students dealing with challenging tasks, can develop students’ self-efficacy. With stronger self-efficacy, teacher support will further trigger students’ sense of control over their learning, leading to higher levels of motivation and enjoyment in their schoolwork (Yu & Singh, 2018). Our results provide a more complex picture, showing that higher teacher support only benefited those with compara­ tively low positive attitudes towards programming. For students with strong positive attitudes, higher teacher support weakened their flow experience (Fig. 2). Deci and Ryan’s (1985) cognitive evaluation theory (CET) suggests that intrinsic motivation can be either enhanced or undermined by external social factors. While informative and supportive social factors can facilitate intrinsic motivation, perceived control from social factors can be demotivating (Deci & Ryan, 1985). Based on CET, Kong and Wang (2021) argued that intrinsic motivation serves as a bridge between CET and flow theory. They investigated the effects of parental involvement in two student groups with high and low creativity, respectively (Kong & Wang, 2021). Their findings suggested that more parental involvement should be obtained by students with low creativity to facilitate their flow experience, whilst students with high creativity tend to seek personal autonomy and satisfaction on their own rather than look for external support. In a similar vein, students with a strong positive attitude towards programming may perceive teacher support as an external control, which undermines students’ own sense of control and further prevents them from immersing in their tasks, whilst students with a comparatively low positive attitude will benefit from high teacher support and subsequently develop greater competence and sense of control over their tasks. Our results echo the findings of Kong and Wang’s (2021) study. However, Kong and Wang’s (2021) measurement on students’ flow experience was from a parent perspective, which might not fully represent students’ views. In this study, we examined students’ flow experience from the first-person perspective by distributing the survey to students themselves. Therefore, this study overcame one of the limitations of Kong and Wang’s (2021) study and further extended the CET to primary school programming context with a more authentic data source. These findings answer our second research question.

Our findings have several practical implications. First, students’ flow experience plays a critical role in high achievers’ perceptions of programming. Our results indicate that increasing the quality of students’ flow experience can strengthen their programming in­ tentions. According to Wongwatkit et al. (2020), students’ intention to use particular learning systems can also determine their learning success. Students with strong programming intentions are more likely to seek extra teacher support and demonstrate more initiative and persistence in realising their goals (Yang et al., 2017). Thus, improving students’ programming intentions by increasing the quality of their flow experience can effectively enhance their programming performance. According to Reeve et al. (1999), teachers adopt either autonomy-supportive or controlling teaching behaviours in class. While autonomy-supportive teaching conveys the teachers’ appreciative feelings, informative feedback and choice opportunities to their students, controlling teaching behaviour uses external stimuli, such as time pressure and punishment (Reeve, 2002). In addition, while autonomy-supportive teaching increases students’ intrinsic motivation, controlling teaching fosters extrinsic motivation and undermines intrinsic motivation (Reeve, 2002). Therefore, in order to facilitate their students’ intrinsic motivation and further generate flow experience in programming, we recommend that teachers should adopt autonomy-supportive teaching practices in programming classes and reduce their controlling teaching behaviour.

Second, as mentioned above, increasing flow experience is very important for students’ programming achievement. Therefore, teachers should be careful when attempting to improve their students’ flow experience. Teacher support may have a positive influence on students’ flow experience (e.g. Hofferber et al., 2016; Khan et al., 2017); however, the current study showed that students’ flow experience could be weakened by higher teacher support based on their different attitudes towards programming. Therefore, teachers should adjust their level of support based on their students’ attitudes towards programming. If their students have a strong positive attitude towards programming, such as strong PU and the impact of or high self-efficacy towards programming, teachers should reduce their involvement and give more autonomy to these high achievers. In contrast, if students have a comparatively poor attitude towards programming, such as diffidence or technostress, teachers should increase their involvement and give extra support to these students. For example, scaffolding strategies could be applied on students who have comparatively poor attitudes. Teachers could increase their extended contact hours and provide follow-up feedback to students who struggle during programming classes (Robinson et al., 2014). Other scaffolding strategies such as instructing, explaining, modeling, and questioning could also be employed by programming teachers (Van de Pol et al., 2010). However, the use of scaffolding should be temporary and teachers should reduce their roles and give more autonomy to students once students show more positive attitudes.

5.3. Limitations and future directions

Our study also has some limitations. First, our sample included high achievers among primary school students. Although the study results identified these high achievers’ characteristics and drew some lessons for programming students in general, we did not examine whether these features were exclusive to high achievers. Also, our sampling procedure might cause a threat to the validity of our results. First, teachers’ nominations could be biased. Specifically, when there was more than one high achiever in the class, teachers’ decision on the nomination might be more subjective due to the lack of objective assessment. Also, when there was not any outstanding achiever in the class, teachers might still feel obligated to nominate a student, which might affect the representativeness of the sample. Second, the students’ attitudes could be inflated by the fact that they were chosen as the only one in the class. Future studies should validate our proposed model among both high and average achievers in programming to enable a comparison of the differences between the perceptions of high and average achievers, which could minimize the validity threat and help identify any differences.

Second, this study only took teacher support as an external variable because teachers play the most central role in students’ programming classes in primary schools. Scholars should also consider parental support in future studies because parents also play an important role in students’ programming activities after school (Kong & Wang, 2021). This would provide a comprehensive picture to support students’ programming activities in the classroom and after school.

Last, some studies have indicated that gender and SES play critical roles in high academic achievements. For example, Seyranian et al. (2018) found that female students experience more stress in physics courses and they are less likely to see themselves as a group member of the physics major, which hinders them to pursue higher achievements (Seyranian et al., 2018). Plasman et al.’s (2021) systematic review indicated that parents with STEM occupations involve more in their kids’ STEM learning, and thus kids would have stronger STEM aspirations and higher STEM achievement than others (Plasman et al., 2021). Postiglione et al. (2017) found that although rural students can achieve similar average GPAs as urban students do, rural students are less likely to become top achievers than their counterparts from urban areas. The high achievers’ demographic characteristics in this study (Table 2) indicated that teachers were disproportionately inclined to nominate boys and those with low SES. However, we did not capture any effect of gender and SES in our study. Also, we did not dig deeply into how gender and SES influence high achievers’ perceptions, but merely explored the beta effects of gender and SES on students’ flow experience and programming intentions. Therefore, we encourage future studies to explore the interesting effects of gender and SES on students’ attitudes, flow experience and programming intentions.
