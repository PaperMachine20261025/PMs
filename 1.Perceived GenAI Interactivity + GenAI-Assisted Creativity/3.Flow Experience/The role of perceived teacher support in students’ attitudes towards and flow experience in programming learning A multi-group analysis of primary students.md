Keywords: Primary education Programming education Students’ attitudes Teacher support Flow experience Achievement levels Multi-group analysis

Programming education has become essential in primary schools, yet students’ perceptions vary significantly across different achievement levels. While teacher support is crucial for learning, little is known about how it influences students’ attitudes and flow experience across different achievement groups in programming education. This study examined the interrelationships among perceived teacher support, students’ attitudes, and flow experience in programming learning across different achievement levels. Data were collected through surveys from 549 students across 43 Hong Kong primary schools. Students were categorised into three groups based on their programming achievement levels: above-average, average, and below-average. A multigroup mediation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between variables. The above-average group showed significantly more positive perceptions across all variables than other groups. Students’ attitudes mediated the relationship between perceived teacher support and flow experience differently across achievement groups. The findings highlight the importance of differentiated teacher support strategies across achievement tiers and suggest that fostering positive attitudes towards programming is crucial for all achievement levels, particularly through enhancing the perceived usefulness of programming.

1. Introduction

Computational Thinking (CT), as defined by Wing (2006), is a cognitive process that applies fundamental computer science concepts to solve problems, design systems, and understand human behaviour. Programming has been recognised as a direct and efficient way to master CT and has been integrated into K–12 education in many countries (Lindberg et al., 2019). Through hands-on application, programming enables learners to engage with core CT concepts such as abstraction, decomposition, algorithmic thinking, and debugging (Wing, 2006). While programming and CT are distinct, there is significant overlap between them. Shared elements include designing algorithms, implementing sequences, and using conditionals and loops (e.g., Grover & Pea, 2013). Programming is not only an effective way to develop CT but also a practical medium for demonstrating CT-related abilities (Brennan & Resnick, 2012). Given this overlap, programming skills may be effectively evaluated through the assessment of CT (Kong & Lai, 2023). For example, CT

Students’ desynchronised performance in programming has received educational researchers’ attention, with a number of studies indicating that both cognitive and non-cognitive factors contribute to this variability. Cognitive factors that have been explored include those that are related to mental processes, such as reasoning, memory, and problem-solving abilities (Hawi, 2010; Heirweg et al., 2020; Othman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2016), while the main non-cognitive factors that have been investigated include stu­ dents’ motivation and attitudes (Lishinski & Yadav, 2019; Luxton-Reilly et al., 2018, pp. 55–106; Tikva & Tambouris, 2021). In addition, while teacher support is generally beneficial, its impact may vary across students of different achievement levels. This insight is particularly relevant, as it raises questions about how teacher support should be tailored to maximise positive outcomes for different types of students. However, there is insufficient evidence to demonstrate the subtle ways in which these factors may differently in­ fluence students across different achievement levels, especially in the context of primary school education. Understanding these distinctions is crucial for developing targeted educational strategies that can cater to the diverse needs of students within a classroom setting.

The adapted hedonic-motivation system adoption model (HMSAM) of Kong and Lin (2022) shows that students’ flow experience mediates the positive relationship between their attitudes and programming intentions, with their perceived teacher support moderating the relationship between attitudes and flow experience. Notably, they focused on a sample of high-achieving primary school students learning programming. By building on Kong and Lin’s (2022) study, we aim to advance the understanding of how these dynamics operate for students demonstrating various achievement levels. We also examine the relationship between students’ atti­ tudes, flow experience, and perceived teacher support. The results of this study are expected to provide educators with more precise insights and strategies to support students according to their achievement levels, thereby fostering a more effective learning envi­ ronment in programming education. Three main research questions are explored.

Research Question 1. Is Kong and Lin’s (2022) adapted HMSAM applicable to a sample of students demonstrating various pro­ gramming achievement levels?

Research Question 2. What are the differences between students’ attitudes, flow experience, and teacher support across various programming achievement levels?

Research Question 3. What are the differences in the interactions between students’ attitudes, flow experience, and teacher support across various programming achievement levels?

2. Literature review

2.1. High achievers in primary school programming

Desynchronised achievement is common in students’ academic journey. Educational researchers have investigated the factors that may contribute to varying academic achievement levels among students. These factors include demographic factors such as gender and family background (e.g., Cimpian et al., 2020; Postiglione et al., 2017), cognitive factors such as reasoning, memory, and problem-solving ability (Hawi, 2010; Heirweg et al., 2020; Othman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2016), and affectional factors such as attitudes and motivation (Lishinski & Yadav, 2019; Luxton-Reilly et al., 2018, pp. 55–106; Tikva & Tambouris, 2021). While de­ mographic factors are generally stable and difficult to change, addressing cognitive and affectional factors is a more complex and challenging task. Extensive programming literature has focused on enhancing students’ programming skills by assessing their cognitive abilities (Fagerlund et al., 2021; Mannila et al., 2020). Indeed, in most educational practices, academic achievement is evaluated based on students’ comprehension of the course content, with memory playing an important role (Hwang et al., 2020). In a similar vein, the relationship between cognitive abilities and academic achievement in programming is also straightforward. Math­ ematic proficiency, for instance, is a crucial cognitive ability that affects one’s achievement in programming (Wang et al., 2016). Mathematically gifted students generally outperform their peers in programming tasks, which often involve complex logical and structured thinking that is strongly associated with mathematical ability (Wang et al., 2016).

Kong and Lin (2022) deemed that affectional factors should be more meaningful for determining why students’ achievement differs. Affectional factors typically touch on students’ belief systems and reflect what students believe is valuable in their learning. The results of Kong and Lin’s (2022) study indicated that students’ flow experience partially mediates the positive relationship between their attitudes and programming intentions. In addition, the results demonstrated that increased teacher support can para­ doxically diminish the flow experience for students who already hold strongly positive attitudes towards programming. In response to Kong and Lin’s (2022) call for a broader student sample, we replicate their adapted HMSAM across a diverse range of students with varying programming achievement levels. Given that their findings aligned with the literature regarding general student samples other than high achievers, we expect their model to be applicable to this study’s sample. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis.

H1. Kong and Lin’s (2022) adapted HMSAM is applicable to a sample of students demonstrating various achievement levels.

2.2. Closer look at students’ attitudes, flow experience, and perceived teacher support

The intriguing relationship between attitudes, flow experience, and perceived teacher support identified by Kong and Lin (2022) inspires our investigation. Notably, for the high achievers, an increase in teacher support is not necessarily desirable for improving flow experience. Specifically, students with comparatively low positive attitudes seem to benefit from greater teacher support, whereas those with comparatively high positive attitudes experience a diminished flow experience under the same conditions. Kong and Lin (2022) interpreted these results through Deci and Ryan’s (1985) cognitive evaluation theory, suggesting that greater teacher support may be considered as a form of external control by students with high positive attitudes, thereby hindering their autonomy and sense of control in learning. Conversely, it may help foster competence and self-efficacy among those with low positive attitudes.

While Kong and Lin (2022) investigated a broad range of factors influencing students’ programming intentions, this study narrows its focus to the dynamic relationship between students’ attitudes, flow experience, and perceived teacher support. This investigation seeks to understand how the interaction among these variables shapes students’ real-time experiences and engagement in the current programming education. While we acknowledge the importance of programming intentions in Kong and Lin’s (2022) model, their construct mainly addresses future-oriented behaviour, which falls outside the scope of our research. The narrower focus allows us to build on Kong and Lin’s (2022) findings by delving deeper into the nuanced relationships they identified and investigating potential connections within this context.

2.2.1. Relationship between attitudes and flow experience Some programming literature has positioned flow experience as a predictor of one’s attitudes towards programming. Especially in programming learning that involves interactive interfaces, such as immersive virtual reality (Cheng, 2024) and augmented reality (Cevahir et al., 2022; Teng et al., 2018), researchers have argued that an enjoyable and intuitive user interface could promote students’ flow experience, which could further predict their positive attitudes towards programming. Developing an enjoyable and intuitive user interface aligns with the concept of promoting one’s perceived ease of use – the extent to which one perceives a particular system as easy to use (Davis, 1986). According to Davis’ (1986) technology acceptance model (TAM), one’s behaviours are mostly decided by one’s attitudes, which are formed by salient beliefs, such as perceived usefulness (i.e., how useful one perceives a particular system to be) and perceived ease of use. Many TAM researchers have found that perception of external control, computer playfulness, and perceived enjoyment – which are highly associated with flow experience – exert substantial influence on one’s perception of ease of use, thus further affecting one’s perception of usefulness (e.g., Venkatesh, 2000; Venkatesh & Bala, 2008).

Huang (2020) found students’ programming behaviours to be primarily influenced by utilitarian factors (i.e., perceived usefulness) rather than hedonic factors (i.e., perceived enjoyment and satisfaction). Students who learn programming for the purpose of enhancing their skills rather than for enjoyment place higher value on the practical benefits they gain than on how enjoyable the learning process is. This echoes Davis and Grani´c’s (2024) argument that one can tolerate a troublesome system if its usefulness is perceived to be substantial. Therefore, it is plausible that flow experience may not fully predict one’s attitudes but rather only the hedonic aspects. To address this possibility, we reframe the relationship between students’ attitudes and flow experience such that attitudes are a predictor of flow experience rather than the reverse. This approach is supported by empirical evidence indicating that attitudes positively in­ fluence flow experience (e.g., Kim & Hall, 2019; Kong & Lin, 2022; Lowry et al., 2013). For example, Lowry et al. (2013) innovatively incorporated flow theory into the TAM literature, suggesting that attitudes enhance curiosity, joy, and control, thereby affecting the flow experience.

3. Method

3.1. Context

Prior to this study, 43 primary schools in Hong Kong had implemented programming courses for upper primary students. All schools introduced Scratch courses to students in Grade 4. For students in Grades 5 and 6, schools were given the flexibility to implement either the MIT App Inventor Basic or App Inventor Advanced courses.

After the academic year for Grades 4 and 5, students took a CT concept test corresponding to the programming course they had completed. By the time this study was conducted, these students had advanced to the next grade level, making them current Grade 5 and Grade 6 students (ages 11–12). Therefore, these students, who had at least one year of programming experience, were considered suitable participants for this study.

The CT concept test employed items from the assessments developed by Kong and Lai (2022) and Kong et al. (2024) to evaluate primary school students’ proficiency in visual block-based programming. These assessments have been widely utilised in programming education research in Hong Kong (e.g., Kong & Lai, 2023; Kong & Lin, 2022). The finalised assessment consisted of test items for Scratch (Kong & Lai, 2022), App Inventor Basic, and App Inventor Advanced (Kong et al., 2024). For further details regarding the development and validation of these assessments, please refer to Kong and Lai (2022) and Kong et al. (2024).

The test focused on key CT concepts, including sequences, loops, conditionals, and elementary data structures, facilitating an evaluation of students’ progression across programming levels. A sample item, depicted in Fig. 3, was designed to assess the concept of repetition.

3.3. Data collection

To address the research questions, we carried out our data collection in two main steps. First, we conducted a survey to assess the participants’ attitudes, flow experience, programming intentions, and perceived teacher support. Second, we categorised the partic­ ipants into various achievement levels based on their CT concept test results.

3.3.1. Survey instrument We utilised the HMSAM instrument adapted by Kong and Lin (2022), which builds upon the original model developed by Lowry et al. (2013). Kong and Lin’s (2022) adaptation was rigorously validated and tested with a large sample, making it well-suited for studying primary school students. Specifically, Attitudes was measured by 12 items, Flow experience was measured by 5 items, Programming intention was measured by 3 items, and perceived Teacher support was measured by 3 items. While the items for At­ titudes, Flow experience, and Programming intention were measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree, those for perceived teacher support were measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = never to 5 = very often.

3.3.2. Grouping For each course (i.e., Scratch, App Inventor Basic, and App Inventor Advanced), the participants’ CT concept test results were used to group them according to their achievement levels. The Z-score was used to categorise participants’ CT test results as above average, average, or below average. Quartile-based categorisation was then applied: the top 25% were classified as above average (Above group), those in the 25%–75% range as average (Average group), and the bottom 25% as below average (Below group). This method enhances the identification of extreme performers and aligns with standard statistical measures (Altman & Bland, 1994). The middle 50% allows for a clearer understanding of typical data that is not overly influenced by outliers. We used the quartile-based catego­ risation for a more precise differentiation between the top and bottom performers, ensuring the groups are distinct and meaningful. Quartile categorisation is commonly used in educational research and bibliometrics (Asan & Aslan, 2020), enabling meaningful comparisons of performance. Table 2 provides a detailed breakdown of student achievement levels across the courses. The total number of participants in each group is as follows: 154 in the Above group, 253 in the Average group, and 180 in the Below group.

3.4. Data analysis

First, moderated mediation was conducted to test H1. Specifically, Kong and Lin’s (2022) adapted HMSAM was tested on the full study sample to examine whether the model effects are applicable to a broader sample demonstrating various achievement levels. Second, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare the attitudes, flow experience, and perceived teacher support across the three achievement groups (i.e., the Above, Average, and Below groups) to test H3. Finally, a multi-group mediation analysis was conducted to examine the proposed model and to test H2 and H4.

4.3.2. Confirmatory factor analysis Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using R 4.4.1 to assess the fit of the hypothesised model to the data, both for the overall sample and within each subgroup (i.e., the Above group, the Average group, and the Below group) (Table 6). Following Wheaton et al. (1977), the ratio of chi-square to degrees of freedom (χ2/df) was employed as a more robust indicator of model fit compared to the chi-square statistic alone. The χ2/df values ranged from 1.46 to 2.33, falling within the recommended range of 1–3 proposed by Hu and Bentler (1999), indicating a good fit to the data.

Additionally, the confirmatory factor index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) values exceeded 0.90 across all datasets, further supporting model fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999). For the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), Browne and Cudeck (1992) suggested values below 0.05 represent a close fit, while values between 0.05 and 0.08 indicate a reasonable fit. The results showed an almost close fit for the Overall sample, while the Above, Average, and Below groups demonstrated reasonable fit. These results provided sufficient evidence of model fit, allowing for subsequent tests of measurement invariance to proceed confidently.

4.3.4. Multi-group mediation Bias-corrected bootstrapping with 2000 resamples was conducted to examine the mediating effect of attitudes within each group (MacKinnon et al., 2004). A significant indirect effect of teacher support on students’ flow experience through students’ attitudes was identified in all groups (Above group: 95% CI = [0.36, 0.60]; Average group: 95% CI = [0.39, 0.60]; and Below group: 95% CI = [0.39, 0.58]). Therefore, the proposed model and H3 are supported. Table 8 presents the results of the path comparisons across the three groups. Each path was constrained sequentially to test for invariance. The chi-square values for all constrained paths were significant (p < .001), indicating that the relationships between Teacher support and Attitudes, Attitudes and Flow experience, and Teacher support and Flow experience differ across the groups. Additionally, the model with all paths constrained yielded a significant chi-square value (χ2 = 511.06, p < .001), further confirming that the hypothesised paths are significantly not equivalent across the groups (Byrne, 2013). These findings support H4.

Across all groups, the relationship between Attitudes and Flow experience showed strong and consistent significance (β ranging from 0.75 to 0.83). The strength of the Teacher Support to Attitudes path is the highest in the Above group (β = 0.66, p < .001) and slightly lower in the Average (β = 0.58, p < .001) and Below (β = 0.62, p < .001) groups. The direct effect of Teacher support on Flow experience is the strongest in the Below group (β = 0.27, p < .001), followed by the Above group (β = 0.25, p < .001) and the weakest in the Average group (β = 0.21, p < .001).

5. Discussion

5.1. Applicability of Kong and Lin’s (2022) adapted HMSAM

Expanding upon Kong and Lin’s (2022) investigation, we incorporate a diverse sample of students across varying achievement levels. Replicating Kong and Lin’s (2022) adapted HMSAM in this study is relevant given the notable insights provided by their research into the complex dynamics of students’ salient belief systems and intrinsic motivation in the context of programming edu­ cation. Their unique investigation of high-achieving students revealed distinctive psychological mechanisms that characterise this group, offering valuable guidance for educators to tailor their instructional strategies accordingly. By broadening the scope to include students across a range of achievement levels, we confirmed the applicability of their model to such a sample, thereby addressing Research Question 1.

Our findings do not necessarily contradict those of Kong and Lin (2022) regarding the exclusiveness of high-achieving students. They selected high-achieving students via teacher nominations, with each teacher nominating only one student per class. These nominations were based on the teachers’ observations and understanding of their students’ performance both in and beyond the classroom. While this approach provided a holistic evaluation of students, Kong and Lin (2022) acknowledged potential biases due to the subjective nature of teachers’ judgements. 5.2. Differences in perceptions across various achievement levels

The Above group demonstrated significantly more positive perceptions than the Average and Below groups across the attitude, flow experience, and perceived teacher support variables. In addition, no significant difference was found between the Average and Below groups in terms of each measured variable. These findings address Research Question 2.

Our findings align with previous research suggesting that high-achieving students often exhibit more positive attitudes and ex­ periences in programming education (Kong & Lin, 2022; Wang et al., 2016). The more positive perceptions of teacher support among high achievers also echo the findings of Wang et al. (2023), who found that students in high-ability groups often receive more teacher support, possibly due to higher teacher expectations. However, the nonsignificant differences between the Average and Below groups may contrast with previous studies finding linear relationships between achievement levels and attitudes or experiences (Gurer et al., 2019; Kong et al., 2018). It is plausible that the relationship between performance and non-cognitive factors in programming edu­ cation may be more complex than previously thought, particularly at the primary school level.

5.3. Differential effects of attitudes and teacher support

Within each group, the students’ attitudes mediated the relationship between teacher support and flow experience to varying magnitudes. This mediating role of attitudes is consistent with previous research stressing the importance of attitude in programming education (Gurer et al., 2019; Kong et al., 2018). Attitudes function as a critical lens through which students perceive and respond to teacher support, shaping their intrinsic motivation, which is closely tied to the flow experience (Deci & Ryan, 1985). In this study, the mediating role of attitudes varied across achievement levels, highlighting distinct dynamics between external support and intrinsic motivation. For the Above group, the mediating role of attitudes reflected a balance between external support and intrinsic motivation. Conversely, according to previous research, low-achieving students may demonstrate a greater dependence on teacher support to increase their confidence and competence (e.g., Wang et al., 2023). In this study, the mediating role of attitudes among the Below group might amplify the effects of teacher support, thereby enhancing their flow experience. For the Average group, the mediating role of attitudes was much more pronounced than the direct effects of teacher support. Furthermore, teacher support played a more prominent role in influencing attitudes and flow experience for both the Above and Below groups than for the Average group. Little literature aligns directly with these findings, as previous multi-group analysis studies have primarily focused on comparing variables independently across different groups, often neglecting the varying interactions be­ tween these variables across said groups. One relevant study was conducted by Wang et al. (2023), who investigated the role of teacher support in school-level ability grouping for English and mathematics classes in Hong Kong. They revealed that the students in high-ability groups received more teacher support. According to Wang et al. (2023), this is likely because teachers often set higher expectations for high-achieving students, which may indirectly be linked to their higher confidence. Furthermore, the low-ability groups were able to perform at a comparable level to their high-ability peers when they received substantial teacher support. While Wang et al. (2023) directly connected teacher support to students’ performance across different ability groups, we enrich their findings by illustrating the complex interplay of students’ attitudes and flow experiences alongside teacher support. This dynamic relationship highlights additional factors that contribute to the educational experiences of students across varying achievement levels.

The above findings address Research Question 3. The differential effects of attitudes and teacher support across achievement groups highlighted in both our study and that of Wang et al. (2023) underscore the complexity of teacher–student interactions in educational settings. It suggests that a one-size-fits-all approach may not be ideal and that educators should consider tailoring their support strategies based on students’ achievement levels and individual needs.

5.4. Practical implications

Fostering positive attitudes towards programming should be a key focus in primary programming education. This aligns with the findings of Gurer et al. (2019) and Kong et al. (2018) on the importance of positive attitudes in programming education. Particularly for average performers, strategies to enhance attitudes may benefit their flow experience. To establish students’ positive attitudes, teachers could make the utilitarian benefits of programming explicit to students (Huang, 2020). For instance, teachers could relate programming to students’ real lives and help them understand how programming could be useful to their daily lives and future development. This is important because perceived usefulness can not only promote positive student attitudes but also stimulate students’ motivation to conquer difficulties in programming learning and enhance their willingness to continue programming (Davis & Grani´c, 2024; Huang, 2020).

Implementing different amounts and strategies is also essential, as teachers’ assistance may affect students differently based on their achievement level. While teacher support appears crucial for both high and low achievers, educators may need to balance their provision of support with encouragement of independent exploration for average performers. This echoes the findings of Wu and Yang (2022) and Ma et al. (2021) regarding the potential negative influence of excessive teacher support.

Similarly, for curriculum designers and developers, tailoring programming curricula to students’ achievement levels is recom­ mended. Learning programs should foster positive attitudes while balancing autonomy and support to enhance students’ learning outcomes.

5.5. Limitations, and future research

Despite the insights of this study, it had some limitations. The Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.92 and 0.90, which were reported for the Attitudes and Intentions scales respectively, showed high internal consistency. However, these high values might also suggest that some test items are too similar, which could limit the instrument’s ability to capture the full range of the construct being measured (Nunnally, 1978). To address this, future research could consider adding a broader variety of items to improve the instruments’ability to assess more nuanced and diverse aspects of the constructs.

The categorisation of students’ achievement levels was based solely on the CT concept test, which is currently among the most standardised assessments available in Hong Kong for evaluating primary school students’ programming skills. However, this test uses multiple-choice items, which may not fully capture students’ programming abilities, particularly in areas related to code design and implementation. Future research could develop more robust methods for categorising students based on their actual programming performance.

Future studies could also investigate the specific types of teacher support that are most effective for each achievement group. Furthermore, qualitative research could provide deeper insights into why average-achieving students demonstrate different rela­ tionship patterns among the measured variables compared with their aboveand below-average peers.

6. Conclusion

This study examined the interrelationships among perceived teacher support, students’ attitudes, and flow experience in pro­ gramming learning across different achievement levels. The insights provided by this study challenge the one-size-fits-all approach to programming education. First, teachers should foster students’ positive attitudes towards programming regardless of their achieve­ ment levels, particularly through enhancing their perceived usefulness of programming. Second, stakeholders should tailor the amount and strategies of support they provide according to students’ achievement levels. While students with aboveand below-average achievement may need more assistance to meet different goals, average-achieving students may seek more independent exploration to enhance their flow experience.
