A case study on designing online-merge-offline business education: Linking flow experience to learning effectiveness

Yi-Wei (Eva) Chang a,b

aDepartment of Business Administration, Minghsin University of Science and Technology, Hsinchu, Taiwan; bSocial Sciences, International Academy of Astronautics (IAA), Paris, France

ABSTRACT With the growing emphasis on employability-oriented curricula in higher education, Online-Merge-Offline (OMO) pedagogy has emerged as an important instructional design innovation in business education. This study examines how immersive OMO course design translates into learning effectiveness through students’ internal developmental processes. Drawing on engagement-based learning perspectives, a moderated mediation model is proposed in which flow experience enhances learning effectiveness via selfesteem, with career aspirations shaping the strength of this mechanism. Survey data were collected from undergraduate students enrolled in a career-oriented OMO business course. The results show that flow experience does not directly predict learning effectiveness; instead, its effects are fully mediated by self-esteem. Moreover, the indirect effects are stronger among students with higher career aspirations. These findings suggest that immersive OMO instruction becomes educationally effective when curriculum design supports students’ self-worth and aligns learning activities with future career goals.

KEYWORDS Online-Merge-Oﬄine (OMO) pedagogy; instructional design; curriculum innovation; learning eﬀectiveness, career aspirations

Introduction

Traditional instructional approaches are increasingly complemented by digitally integrated models that combine online and offline environments (Graham, 2013; Means et al., 2014). While hybrid and blended learning are widely adopted, emerging approaches emphasise greater integration and continuity across learning contexts (Graham, 2013; Means et al., 2014). At the same time, concerns over graduate employability have prompted universities to redesign curricula that emphasise practical competence, career readiness, and transferable skills (Khan et al., 2025). While prior research suggests that such programmes can enhance learning outcomes, less attention has been paid to the psychological processes through which students benefit from employability-focused learning designs (Cayzer et al., 2026).

Within this context, the Online-Merge-Offline (OMO) model has emerged as an instructional design that integrates digital learning technologies with face-to-face

Self-esteem is one of the psychological mechanisms that may explain the relationship between flow experience and perceived control during task engagement. It reflects individuals’ evaluations of their competence and self-worth and plays a critical role in shaping motivation and learning behaviour (Yong et al., 2026). Experiencing flow may foster self-esteem by reinforcing feelings of mastery and competence, which in turn, promote more effective learning outcomes (Su et al., 2024; Yong et al., 2026). Nevertheless, empirical research has rarely examined self-esteem as a mediating mechanism linking flow experience to learning effectiveness.

In addition, recent educational research highlights the importance of individual differences in shaping how learning processes operate (Yong et al., 2026). Career aspirations may function as a key boundary condition. Students with strong career aspirations are more likely to perceive learning activities as meaningful and instrumental, whereas those with less defined goals may struggle to translate positive learning experiences into effective outcomes (Kahu & Nelson, 2018). In employability-oriented curricula, career aspirations may therefore condition the extent to which self-esteem facilitates learning effectiveness (Otermans et al., 2025)).

To address these gaps, this study develops and tests a moderated mediation model examining how OMO induced flow experience influences learning effectiveness through self-esteem and how this indirect relationship varies according to students’ career aspirations (Qi, 2025; Yen & Lin, 2022). Focusing on undergraduate students enrolled in careeroriented OMO business programmes, the study investigates the psychological mechanisms through which immersive instructional design supports effective learning (Qi, 2025; Yen & Lin, 2022).

Originality and value

This study contributes to higher education teaching and learning research in three ways:

First, it extends flow-based learning research by identifying self-esteem as a key psychological mechanism linking immersive instructional design to learning effectiveness (Zausmer et al., 2024);

Second, it introduces career aspirations as a boundary condition, offering a more nuanced understanding of when flow-based learning is most effective in employabilityoriented contexts (Wang et al., 2025); and Third, by situating these mechanisms within an OMO curriculum framework, the study shifts the focus from technological features to instructional design processes, providing evidence-based insights for educators and curriculum designers seeking to enhance learning effectiveness in digitally integrated business education (Kearney et al., 2025).

OMO pedagogy and experiential learning integration

While the combing model of online and face-to-face instruction model enhances flexibility, it often treats online and offline components as separate or sequential elements (Graham, 2013; Means et al., 2014). In contrast, OMO pedagogy represents an integrative instructional design approach that emphasises the continuity of learning experiences across digital and physical environments. In practice, OMO courses combine online simulations, real-time feedback and in-class collaborative activities (R. Huang et al., 2021). Prior research has focused primarily on observable outcomes such as engagement and performance (Chigbu et al., 2023). Less attention has been paid to the psychological processes through which integrated learning experiences influence student development. Addressing this gap, the present study focuses on flow experience as a key mechanism linking OMO pedagogy to learning effectiveness.

Flow experience in digitally integrated learning environments

Flow experience refers to a state of optimal learning engagement in which individuals are deeply involved in an activity (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). In educational contexts, flow is most likely to emerge when learning tasks are well aligned with students’ skill levels and provide clear goals and timely feedback, enabling sustained concentration and effort (Bassi & Fave, 2012) with persistence, self-regulation, and positive learning attitudes (Kahu & Nelson, 2018). Prior studies in technology-induced and hybrid learning contexts consistently link flow experience to favourable learning outcomes (Akman & Çakır, 2023; J. Kang et al., 2018; Kim & Ko, 2019). In digitally integrated learning environments such as OMO pedagogy, flow may play a particularly important role (Chigbu et al., 2023). Despite extensive research on flow in general educational settings, its role within career-oriented and employabilityfocused programmes remains underexplored (Wang et al., 2025; Zausmer et al., 2024). In the present study, flow experience is conceptualised as a multidimensional learning experience reflecting students’ subjective engagement in career-oriented OMO activities, and operationalised through enjoyment, concentration, and sense of control, to capture learners’ immersion, agency, and satisfaction (Teng & Ma, 2026).

Self-esteem as a mediating learning-related mechanism

Self-esteem refers to individuals’ evaluations of their competence and self-worth, and plays a central role in shaping motivation, persistence, and responses to learning challenges (Bong & Skaalvik, 2003). In educational settings, students with higher self-esteem tend to exhibit greater confidence, sustained effort, and resilience, whereas lower self-esteem is often associated with disengagement and self-doubt (DeNeve & Cooper, 1998; Zhou & Zhang, 2026).

Flow experience provides a critical context for the development of self-esteem. During flow, learners experience deep engagement, intrinsic enjoyment, and a strong sense of control, which signal competence and mastery (Jiang & Tong, 2025; Lu et al., 2025). The immersive learning environments contribute to students’ psychological resources, including confidence and self-belief (Bong & Skaalvik, 2003; Ellis & Bliuc, 2019).

Learning effectiveness in career-oriented learning environments

In career-oriented and OMO learning environments, students are required to integrate digital tools, collaborative problem solving, and applied decision-making, making psychological resources such as confidence and self-concept particularly salient (R. Huang et al., 2021). When students perceive themselves as capable and competent learners, they are more likely to consolidate learning, persist in the face of difficulty, and apply newly acquired skills with confidence (Bong & Skaalvik, 2003). Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2: Self-esteem has a positive effect on learning effectiveness in career-oriented

programmes.

Building on the above arguments, OMO induced flow experience may influence learning effectiveness indirectly through its impact on self-esteem (Yen & Lin, 2022). In careeroriented and OMO-based learning environments, such evaluative processes are particularly salient, as students continuously assess their readiness for future professional roles (Kahu & Nelson, 2018).

From a teaching and learning perspective, this suggests that self-esteem serves as a key mechanism through which experiential engagement is internalised and transformed into meaningful learning effectiveness (Qi, 2025; Yen & Lin, 2022). Prior research supports the mediating role of self-evaluative constructs in linking learning experiences to developmental and career-related outcomes, particularly in applied and work-integrated learning contexts (Bong & Skaalvik, 2003; Jackson & Bridgstock, 2021).

Based on this reasoning, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3: Self-esteem mediates the relationship between flow experience and learning

effectiveness.

Methodology

Research context: OMO-based business management course

This study was conducted within an 18-week undergraduate course entitled Digital Business Transformation at a university of science and technology in Taiwan. The course was explicitly designed using an OMO pedagogical model to support career-oriented skill development by integrating digital learning activities with face-to-face, practice-based instruction. The online component consisted of AI- driven retail simulations involving real-time consumer data, platform analytics, and digitally mediated decision-making tasks. The offline component comprised inperson workshops and strategic discussions in which students synthesised their online analyses, articulated managerial decisions, and received feedback from instructors and industry practitioners. The course provided an appropriate instructional context for examining flow experience, self-esteem, career aspirations, and learning effectiveness.

A quantitative, cross-sectional design was employed to test the proposed conceptual model. Participants were undergraduate students enrolled in the Digital Business Transformation course during the study period. Data were collected using a selfadministered online questionnaire distributed via course communication platforms after the completion of all instructional activities. They completed the questionnaire independently and were informed that participation was voluntary, anonymous, and for research purposes only without affecting course assessment. Conducting the survey after the full completion of the course ensured that students had experienced the entire OMO learning cycle and were able to provide informed evaluations of their learning experiences.

Ethical considerations

This study complied with the institutional ethical requirements of author’s affiliation, Minghsin University of Science and Technology. In accordance with Article 1 of Official Document No. 1,010,034,612, the study met the institutional criteria for exemption from full ethics review. Permission to conduct the study was confirmed before data collection. All participants provided informed consent before completing the questionnaire. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Students were informed that participation or non-participation would not affect their grades, course assessment, or academic standing. No personally identifiable information was collected.

Measures

The OMO induced flow experience was measured based on the flow theory (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990; Kiili, 2005; Shernoff et al., 2003), the self-esteem (Rosenberg, 1965; Zeigler-Hill & Shackelford, 2020), the learning effectiveness and the career aspirations for higher education research (Ellis & Bliuc, 2019; Jackson & Bridgstock, 2021). All items were measured on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The questionnaire was reviewed by three domain experts to ensure content validity and clarity. Control variables

Students’ academic year and prior exposure to employability-oriented courses were included as control variables to account for potential confounding effects.

Data analysis was conducted in four stages:

First stage, descriptive statistics were used to examine sample characteristics and variable distributions (Yen & Lin, 2022); Second stage, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to assess the measurement model, including construct reliability and discriminant validity (Yen & Lin, 2022); Third stage, hierarchical regression analysis was employed to test the direct effects among variables (Ntumi, 2025); and Fourth stage, mediation and moderation analyses were conducted using bootstrapping procedures (5,000 resamples) to estimate indirect and conditional effects (Davies et al., 2024). All predictor variables were mean-centred prior to constructing interaction terms.

Methodological alignment

By situating the analyses within a clearly defined OMO instructional context, the study maintains coherence between instructional design, research objectives, and analytical strategy, ensuring that the findings are interpretable and educationally meaningful (R. The analytical approach aligns with established practices in research on innovative teaching and learning in higher education, particularly studies examining experiential learning, student engagement, and employability-oriented pedagogy in technology-induced environments (R. Data analysis results

The results indicate that students’ learning effectiveness in the career-oriented OMO course is closely associated with the quality of their learning engagement and selfevaluative processes. The OMO induced flow experience was positively related to students’ self-esteem, which in turn was positively associated with perceived learning effectiveness (Hui et al., 2025).

Mediation analyses showed that self-esteem fully mediated the relationship between flow experience and learning effectiveness, indicating that immersive engagement contributes to learning outcomes primarily by strengthening students’ perceptions of competence and confidence (Mostajeran et al., 2025). Moderation analyses further revealed that career aspirations significantly conditioned this relationship. The positive association between self-esteem and learning effectiveness was stronger among students with higher levels of career aspirations (Mostajeran et al., 2025).

Bootstrapping results supported a conditional indirect effect, such that the indirect effect of flow experience on learning effectiveness via self-esteem was stronger for students with clearer and stronger career goals (Cheung et al., 2025). These findings suggest that immersive OMO learning experiences are most effective when they enhance students’ self-worth and align with their future career direction.

As shown in Figure 2, career aspirations were examined as a moderating condition shaping the learning process. More importantly, the interaction between self-esteem and career aspirations was significant (β = 0.124, p = .028), supporting H4. This finding suggests that the positive relationship between students’ confidence and learning effectiveness was stronger among students with clearer and stronger career aspirations. Discussion

Reconceptualizing learning effectiveness in OMO-based business education

This study demonstrates that learning effectiveness in OMO business education is not an automatic outcome of instructional design or technological integration. Instead, it emerges through a structured developmental process in which immersive engagement, students’ self-evaluations, and career alignment interact dynamically. Rather than treating learning outcomes as direct outputs of teaching inputs, the findings highlight learning effectiveness as a process shaped by students’ perceptions of competence, confidence, and professional relevance (D. Huang et al., 2025). In OMO environments – where students move between data-driven online simulations and face-to-face strategic discussions – learning effectiveness depends on whether students experience themselves as capable and career-ready participants in complex, technology-mediated tasks (Qian et al., 2026).

Flow experience as pedagogical engagement

A central finding is that OMO induced flow experience does not directly predict learning effectiveness once self-esteem is considered. From an instructional perspective, this suggests that immersive engagement alone is insufficient to guarantee meaningful learning outcomes. In OMO business education, flow represents active engagement with authentic, practice-oriented tasks rather than simply enjoyment (Obi et al., 2022).

Self-esteem as an instructional resource in hybrid learning

The full mediating role of self-esteem highlights its importance as an instructional resource in OMO pedagogy. Self-esteem enables students to convert challenging learning experiences into perceived learning gains (Garbenis & Kaffemaniene, 2025). When OMO activities are appropriately scaffolded and aligned with authentic professional practices, successful task engagement reinforces students’ confidence and sense of competence (Garbenis & Kaffemaniene, 2025). This explains why immersive engagement contributes to learning effectiveness only when it strengthens students’ self-belief, underscoring the importance of feedback, reflection, and recognition in OMO course design.

Career aspirations as a boundary condition for employability-oriented learning

This study further identifies career aspirations as a key boundary condition shaping the effectiveness of OMO pedagogy. From an employability perspective, OMO environments are most effective when students perceive a clear connection between course activities and future professional roles (Khan et al., 2025). This finding reinforces employabilityfocused pedagogical frameworks, which emphasise alignment between instructional design and students’ career goals (Khan et al., 2025).

(1) OMO learning activities should be designed to reinforce students’ sense of compe-

tence, not engagement alone; (2) Employability signals should be made explicitly within OMO curricula; and (3) Given the moderating role of career aspirations, educators should support students

in clarifying career goals through mentoring and professional mapping activities.

Theoretical contributions

(1) Reconceptualises learning effectiveness in OMO education as a mediated develop-

mental process, rather than a direct instructional outcome; (2) Positions flow experience as a pedagogical condition that supports confidence

development and professional identity formation, rather than as a purely psychological state; and (3) Advances a contingency-based view of OMO pedagogy by demonstrating that the

effectiveness of immersive instructional design depends on students’ career aspirations.

Limitations and future research directions

Through this study, it is found there are still the following limitations and further researches in the future are suggested:

(1) The use of a cross-sectional, self-reported survey design limits causal inference and

the ability to capture learning processes over time. Future studies could adopt longitudinal or mixed-method approaches. (2) The study was conducted within a single career-oriented business course at one

institution, which may constrain generalisability. Replication across disciplines, institutions, and alternative OMO designs would be helpful. (3) Learning effectiveness was measured using perceived outcomes rather than objec-

tive performance indicators. Future research could integrate learning analytics, performance assessments, or employer feedback to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of OMO learning outcomes.

Conclusions

This study examined how learning effectiveness emerges within career-oriented onlinemerge-offline (OMO) business education by focusing on the interplay between immersive engagement, self-evaluative processes, and career alignment. The findings demonstrate that learning effectiveness is not a direct outcome of immersive instructional design alone, but is realised through students’ self-esteem and is contingent upon their career aspirations. From a curriculum perspective, the results highlight the importance of designing OMO learning environments that not only promote engagement but also support students’ confidence and professional self-concept.

Overall, this study underscores the need to view OMO innovation as a curriculum and policy issue rather than solely a technological one.
