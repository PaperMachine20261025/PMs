Effect of active social media use on flow experience: Mediating role of academic self‑efficacy

Shenghui Yao1   · Lifen Xie1 · Yiji Chen1

Received: 4 January 2022 / Accepted: 24 October 2022 / Published online: 5 November 2022 © The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2022

Abstract To explore the effect of active social media use (ASMU) on the mechanisms of flow experience (FE), 433 questionnaires incorporating an active social media use scale, academic self-efficacy (ASE) scale, and flow experience scale were collected from college students. This study is expected to enrich flow experience theory and provide a foundation for follow-up studies. The theoretical model for this study analyses positive social media use as an independent variable, flow experience as a dependent variable, and academic self-efficacy as an intermediary variable. Relevant data were collected via questionnaires. Subsequently, linear regression analysis was used, and the PROCESS v3.3 statistical tool of SPSS 23.0 software was used to process and analyze data. This study found that active social media use had a significant and positive impact on the flow experience. Additionally, active social media use had a significant and positive impact on academic self-efficacy.

Keywords  Active social media use · Flow experience · Academic self-efficacy · Empirical study · Process v3.3

Young people use their mobile phones to check their plans, send important messages, view photos, and contact friends and family on social media (Marty-Dugas & Smilek, 2020). Therefore, why does social media use have a negative impact? This impact may be related to the mode of use (Frison & Eggermont, 2016), with the main difference being between active and passive use (Verduyn et  al., 2015). Follow-up studies provide further evidence for the negative relationship between the passive use of social networking sites and mental health (in fact, the relationship may be mutual); these studies also reveal that active use has a positive impact on mental health (Liu et al., 2019). Active social media use may enhance social connections and subjective wellbeing and reduce loneliness (Brailovskaia & Margraf, 2019; Deters & Mehl, 2012; Verduyn et al., 2017). Additionally, there is growing evidence that the impact of social media on mental health depends considerably on the way it is used (Verduyn et al., 2021). Therefore, this study seeks to examine whether the active use of social media has positive psychological effects on college students and whether it impacts their self-efficacy. We focused on three variables: active social media use, academic self-efficacy, and flow experience.

2  Literature review and research hypothesis

2.1  Main concepts

2.1.1  Flow experience

Flow experience refers to the overall experience of implementing an action in a fully engaged manner (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975; Hyuna et al., 2021). For individual psychology, this implementation is a positive emotion or a positive state. Ghani and Deshpande (1994) state that flow experience has two key characteristics: the concentration on the activity and the pleasure of participating in the activity. Thomas (1999) regards flow as a process that encompasses preconditions of occurrence, the development process, and results. When Compeau and Higgins (1995) studied the flow experience of online consumers, they found that certain activities, such as retrieving information, participating in discussion groups, writing emails, creating webpages, playing games, and chatting with other users, can easily produce a flow experience. Srivastava et al. (2010) found that in network environments, personal flow experience consists of three levels: flow antecedents, flow experience, and flow consequences.

Studies on social media have found that flow experiences can affect users’ psychological states, including their attitudes, emotions, and perceptions. For example, flow experiences can affect user trust (Lee, 2017), time warp (Pelet et al., 2017), and happiness (Asakawa, 2010; Kim et al., 2017). In the synchronous communication environment of social media, users exist in a state of realtime interaction. Due to the strong interactions among multiple people, the emotional experience caused by emotional infection will cause interactions to further strengthen corresponding emotional expressions (Zhu & Ke, 2019). Because the group is in the same situation, the subject’s emotional experience is consistent with the emotional expression clues of others or groups (Hatfield et al., 2009). These emotional cues will further magnify one’s emotional state, either directly or indirectly, and individuals will perceive a stronger sense of emotional experience (Michael et  al., 2012a, b). This good sense of experience will encourage them to pay more attention and make them more likely to produce flow experiences (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990a, b). In addition, flow experience can also affect an individual’s intentions and behaviors (Lin et al., 2020).

2.1.2  Active social media use

Social media platforms, such as Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, and LinkedIn, constitute what is known as Web 2.0 (Huang & Benyoucef, 2014). Gastelum and Whattam (2013) describe such websites as social media websites, including blogs, chat platforms, and photo and video sharing websites. Junco (2014) defines social networks as applications offering specific services that allow users to create and share content through internet-based systems. Turban et al. (2012) explain that social networks are useful tools for creating and exchanging user-generated content. Users can use social

networks to upload pictures, videos, music, and images, as well as comment and share their ideas, feelings, opinions, and experiences.

Scholars have become interested in the topic of social media, and extensive research has been carried out in the fields of sociology, psychology, communication and management, and medicine. As research findings accumulate, scholarly understandings of the concept of “social media use” have become richer. At present, the concept of social media use is composed of the following aspects. First, in the initial research on social media, researchers mainly discussed the concept of “behavior,” a term that encompasses use time, frequency, duration, and more. During this stage of research, the concept of “behavior” mainly captured the “intensity of social media use.” In subsequent research, scholars began studying satisfaction in relation to social media functions, including information acquisition, entertainment, games, and social interaction. According to a report by the China Internet Network Information Center (2016), social media users partake in three main forms of behavior: information acquisition, entertainment and leisure, and social interaction. Frison and Eggermont (2016) divide social media usage into two categories: active and passive use. Active social media use refers to an individual’s direct interaction with friends and other social media users as well as related behaviors that promote communication and interaction, including posting information and photos, updating content, liking other people’s content, and leaving comments (Frison & Eggermont, 2016).

In a study by Kabilan et al. (2010), the author found that students believe that Facebook can be used as an online environment to promote English learning and that active use of social media can increase college students’ social capital (Kim & Kim, 2017) and provide them with social support (Selkie et al., 2020). These effects are all conducive to the development of students’ self-confidence. The use of social media applications, such as Facebook and WhatsApp, can enhance the “alternative experience vicarious experience” and contribute to the improvement of college students’ academic self-efficacy (Boahene et al., 2019).

2.1.3  Academic self‑efficacy

Academic self-efficacy is the specific application of self-efficacy in the field of education, and it refers to students’ judgments of their abilities to successfully complete learning tasks (Bandura, 1986). In his research on the role of networks in shaping teachers’ self-efficacy, Siciliano (2016) states that (a) peer interaction provides opportunities to acquire teaching-related knowledge, which reduces uncertainty, and that (b) self-efficacy beliefs can be formed according to the advice of one’s peers who are directly connected through a network. In this way, Siciliano (2016) demonstrates that these two mechanisms may affect teachers’ self-efficacy. Based on his research, we believe that students can also use social media software to connect with friends and build academic self-efficacy during the learning process. Previous studies have shown that much of people’s understanding of scientific knowledge comes from social media (Gieryn, 1999). A comparative study of college students in Spain and Argentina confirmed that self-efficacy can indirectly affect learning engagement through flow experience (Mesurado et al., 2016a, b). Moreover, CAI and JIA (2020) found that academic self-efficacy can directly affect students’ flow experience.

2.2  Research hypothesis

2.2.1  Active social media use and flow experience

Basing their research on network environments, Hoffman et al. (1996) demonstrate that users will affect their perceived flow experience in the process of using network services, and they will directly or indirectly affect the external factors of flow experience. The main antecedent variables are challenge, skill, and concentration, while the secondary antecedent variables are interaction and telepresence. Following the rapid development of network information, flow experience has come to be widely used in internet research. Users’ full participation and focused attention when using a website produce an extremely enjoyable experience (Chen et al., 2000). Hoffman and Novak (1996), as well as other researchers, believe that people are more likely to encounter flow experience in network environments (Hoffman et al., 2009).

Flow experience is often a state in which individuals actively participate in an environment (Privette, 1983). Steuer (1992) points out that interaction constitutes the real-time participation of users in the form and content of a media environment using computer-mediated communication. This interaction derives from the communication between individuals. In the process of information being transmitted between two parties, the latter information responds to the previous information (Rafaeli, 1988). According to information communication theory, the communication subject sends and receives information simultaneously during the process of communication. That is, communication itself is a cyclical process, and feedback plays an important role in the process of information dissemination. Social media is characterized by collaboration and communities that enable immersive and controllable experiences and provide immediate feedback to users, which, in turn, makes social media fertile ground for flow experiences (Hoffman & Novak, 1996; Pelet et al., 2017). Users are able to interact in real-time because there is a stronger interaction intensity between multiple people, and this emotional experience, which is caused by emotional infection, enables the interaction to further strengthen the corresponding emotional expression. In addition, the emotional experience of the subject is consistent with the emotional expression clues of others (Hatfield et al., 2009). These emotional clues further enlarge one’s emotional state, either directly or indirectly, and because of the mutual infection of emotional states, an individual’s emotional experience will become more intense (Michael et al., 2012a, b). An experiment on Facebook usage has confirmed that active social media use can effectively enhance users’ positive emotional experiences (Deters & Mehl, 2012). Such positive experiences encourage users to be more focused, which, in turn, makes them more likely to have a flow experience (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990a, b).

Based on the above discussion, this essay proposes hypothesis H1: Active social media use has a positive effect on the flow experience.

2.2.2  Active social media use and academic self‑efficacy

Holzinger (2001) point out that from the perspective of computing, technology, and education, accidental learning includes the concept put forward by Ross-Gordon and Dowling (1995), who state, “learn in the network and learn from a series of

interpersonal experiments”. Incidental learning enables people to learn through unplanned learning activities, including academic and non-academic activities (Kerka, 2000). Incidental learning is particularly effective when using computers (Pennington, 1989); computer-assisted learning usually occurs in an online environment (McFerrin, 1999) and incorporates observation, repetition, social interaction, and problem solving (Rogers, 1997). Northcote and Kendle (2001) assert that participating in online learning activities, such as online forum discussions and online information searches, can allow students to acquire practical online skills in a more casual and informal environment. Harasim et al. (1997) state that individuals can have meaningful educational experiences and learn thinking skills when members of online communities, such as Facebook, “translate ideas into words and share and build them with others through their responses and responses.” All these can improve students’ learning beliefs.

Based on the discussion above, this paper proposes hypothesis H2: Active social media use has a positive effect on academic self-efficacy.

2.2.3  Academic self‑efficacy and flow experience

Academic self-efficacy is the application of self-efficacy in the field of education, and it refers to students’ judgment of their ability to successfully complete learning tasks (Bandura, 1986). The emergence of flow experience is often accompanied by a sense of control; that is, users say to themselves, “I can do anything in that state” (Jackson & Marsh, 1996). According to flow experience theory, individual experience consists of three levels: flow antecedents, flow experience, and flow result. Self-efficacy is an important flow antecedent (Srivastava et al., 2010), and people with high self-efficacy are more likely to have flow experiences (Özhan & Kocadere, 2020; Trevino & Webster, 1992). Academic self-efficacy has a significant and positive impact on flow experience (CAI & JIA, 2020; Joo et al., 2015; Mesurado et al., 2016a, b). When learners have a strong sense of self-efficacy, they are more likely to have a pleasant flow experience (Ho & Kuo, 2010). A study by Mesurado et al. (2016a, b) found that when students believe they are capable of learning and coping with academic tasks, they are more likely to have flow experience and participate in learning activities.

Based on the above discussion, this research proposes hypothesis H3: Academic self-efficacy has a positive effect on flow experience.

3  Research methods

3.1  Sample

We selected study participants who were WeChat users attending Jiujiang University, and we used the Questionnaire Star online survey platform (https://​www.​wjx.​cn) to distribute questionnaires. According to questionnaire answer settings, a single IP address is only able to complete one questionnaire. The data collection period lasted 1 day, and a total of 447 responses were collected. After excluding 14 invalid questionnaires (i.e., participants who spent less than 55 s filling out the survey and gave identical answers to each question), a total of 433 valid responses were received. Among the survey sample, there were 234 male respondents (54%) and 199 female respondents (46%).

3.2  Measurement

To ensure the accuracy of scale translation, this paper first translated the variables from English to Chinese and then from Chinese to English. This study used the flow experience scale provided by Wang et al. (2017), which utilizes a 5-point Likert scale (with 1 as “disagree strongly” and 5 as “strongly agree”) and a total of 4 items (e.g., “When I use social media, I feel that time passes quickly.”). Positive social media use uses the scale used in Frison and Eggermont’s (2016) study, uses Likert’s 5-point measurement (with 1 as “never” and 5 as “very frequently”), and modifies it according to linguistic and cultural habits (e.g., using adverbs instead of intensity values in the original scale), such as: “I () post information on my own social media.” Academic self-efficacy was measured using the scale used in Midgley’s et  al. (2000) study, which includes five items (e.g., “I’m certain I can master the skills taught in class this year.”). In the study, a 5-point Likert scale was used to measure academic self-efficacy (with 1 as “strongly disagree” and 5 as “strongly agree”).

3.3  Procedure

This study used a Questionnaire Star online survey distributed using WeChat, China’s most popular social media software. When designing the questionnaire, we set screening items to exclude participants who do not use WeChat. The questionnaire was divided into two parts. The first part contained demographic variables and screening items, and the second featured a standard questionnaire structure. Respondents were asked to review their use of social media and complete the questionnaire following their true feelings. In the questionnaire guide, we assured the respondents that the survey was only for scientific research and that the data would be kept secret and confidential. In distributing the questionnaire, we only targeted students at Jiujiang University. Twelve research assistants and three full-time teachers distributed the questionnaires to respondents. Before issuing the questionnaire, we asked prospective respondents whether they were willing to participate in the survey. Questionnaire collection was completed through the “Questionnaire Star” background, which saved the time associated with data entry for paper questionnaires and eliminated the risk of input errors.

3.4  Data analysis

In this study, SPSS 23.0 and PROCESS v3.3 were used for statistical analysis. First, we carried out data analysis to produce descriptive statistics. Second, the reliability and validity of data were analyzed. Finally, we tested the relationship between active social media use and flow experience; we also tested the mediating effect of academic self-efficacy on the relationship between active social media use and flow experience. Model 4 in the PRO- CESS v3.3 software was used to test the significance of indirect effects, and this random sampling process was repeated 5,000 times. The 95% confidence interval of the non-zero indirect effect indicates that there is a significant mediating effect at 0.05.

4  Results

4.1  Common method bias

Harman’s single-factor test is commonly used in exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to test for Common Method Bias (CMB). The EFA method assumes that there is a single method factor explaining the common variance of all items across traits in a study (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Podsakoff and Organ (1986) conclude that the CMB is not significant if the variance explained by a single factor obtained by EFA (unrotated) does not exceed 50%; this is also supported by Hair et al. (1998). Using Harman’s single-factor test, this study identified three factors with values that had characteristic root greater than 1, and the variance explained by the first factor obtained without rotation was 49.116%, which was below the 50% threshold. Therefore, we believe that the data from this study can be used for subsequent analysis.

4.2  Reliability and validity analysis

In this study, an internal consistency coefficient was used to test the reliability with an alpha value greater than 0.7. In addition, EFA and an analysis of average variance extracted (AVE) were used to test validity. In this study, all eigenvalues of the scales were greater than 1, the cumulative variance contribution was 77.26%, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value was 0.886, the significance level of Bartlett’s test of sphericity was less than 0.001, and the AVE was greater than 0.5, indicating that these scales have high overall reliability and validity, and that the overall data can be hypothesis tested. 4.4.2  Mediating effect analysis

The mediating effect was tested using the bootstrap method, and the sampling process was repeated 5,000 times. The mediating effect was tested to determine whether the confidence interval contained 0. If the confidence interval does not contain 0, the mediating effect is significant; if it contains 0, the mediating effect is not significant (Taylor et al., 2008). The results of the statistical analysis, which are shown in Table 4, demonstrate that ASMU has an indirect effect on FE through ASE. The total effect value of ASMU is 0.3538, the direct effect value is 0.2254, the indirect effect value is 0.1284, and the contribution rate of the indirect effect is 36.29%. The 95% confidence interval obtained from the bootstrap method (0.0677, 0.1966) does not contain 0 in the interval; therefore, the influence of the intermediary effect is considered to be statistically significant.

5  Conclusion and discussion

Three scales were used to measure active social media use, academic self-efficacy, and flow experience among a sample of college students at Jiujiang University, and the relationship between the three operational definitions was discussed. Active social media use has a significant and positive effect on flow experience, and academic self-efficacy has a mediating effect on the relationship between active social media use and flow experience.

Active social media use has a significant and positive effect on the flow experience; this finding is consistent with the findings of Mauri et al. (2011) and Zhao and Zhou (2021). Active social media use involves interaction with others, and in the process of interacting, users gain beneficial emotional experiences, improve their relationships, and generate pleasant emotions. Moreover, these positive experiences encourage users to pay more attention and make them more likely to have additional flow experiences.

Meanwhile, active social media use also increases opportunities for incidental learning. Therefore, active social media use has a significant and positive effect on academic self-efficacy. This study, which assumed the perspective of incidental learning and positive emotions, clarified how active social media use could improve the academic self-efficacy of college students. We believe that the active use of social media will increase college students’ learning opportunities and that positive interaction will enable good emotional experiences.

We believe that academic self-efficacy has a significant and positive effect on flow experience; this assertion is consistent with the conclusion of CAI and JIA (2020). However, one study (Mao et al., 2020) shows that flow experience can also have a significant and positive impact on academic self-efficacy. This study found that this is not a conflict or contradiction because its role in the relationship is involved with a completely different psychological process. Flow experience has a positive impact on academic self-efficacy because the antecedent of flow experience may be immersive because of interest. In this study, we found that improvements in academic selfefficacy can promote the formation of flow experience because students are more likely to face challenges and tasks when they are confident that they can achieve their academic goals.

The use of social media, which has become a technological torrent, inevitably appears in our real life. Instead of simply restricting the use of social media, it is important to first better understand social media and identify its influence. If we understand its effects, we can make better use of social media, guide and encourage students in their use of social media, encourage interaction instead of negative browsing, and provide theoretical support for guidance strategies regarding social media use.

6  Limitations

This study has several shortcomings. First, the theoretical application is not comprehensive enough to consider Kozinets et al. (2012) and Levinson’s theory of new new media (2010). In future research, theories such as new new media can be further used for in-depth research, especially when considering the positive role of interactivity and timeliness in the use of social media. Second, this study fails to consider the content of social media usage. The content of social media will also affect the experience of using social media, such as sharing gaming experiences through social media. Future research should further improve the impact of social media use content on user experience. Finally, this study used questionnaires to collect data. This method is relatively simple, and it used only students of Jiujiang University as samples. Therefore, it is uncertain whether the conclusions can be generalized to all college students. This question needs to be investigated further.
