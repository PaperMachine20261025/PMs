Digital game‑based learning: Pedagogical agent and feedback types on achievement, flow experience, and cognitive load

Yasemin Kahyaoğlu Erdoğmuş1   · Adile Aşkım Kurt2

Received: 22 March 2023 / Accepted: 14 November 2023 / Published online: 11 December 2023 © The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2023

Abstract This study investigated pedagogical agent (with/without) and feedback (explanatory/ confirmatory) types on achievement, flow experience, and cognitive load in a digital game-based learning environment. We developed a game about spreadsheets and prepared four versions of the game in the context of pedagogical agent and feedback types. Through the mixed-methods sequential exploratory design, we applied game versions in four experimental groups (154 undergraduate students) within a two-week spreadsheet subject. We collected quantitative (achievement test, flow experience scale, cognitive load scale, elapsed time, skill, and challenge-level questions) and qualitative (semi-structured interview technique and open-ended interview form) data. According to the results, the achievement scores of all participants increased regardless of the pedagogical agent and feedback type. There was no significant difference in cognitive load, flow experience, skill level, or challenge level according to the pedagogical agent and feedback types. Confirmatory feedback groups spent more time than explanatory feedback groups. The dependent variables had significant relationships. Students expressed that game-based learning promoted improvements in the learning process.

Keywords  Cognitive load · Flow · Game · Learning

1  Introduction

Advances in technology are accelerating in the digital game market, and the number of game players is increasing day by day. According to Statista (2021) data, 83.6% of worldwide internet users played video games, while this rate was 91.5% in Türkiye. The reason why users find games attractive and spend a long time on them is related to the flow experience they have in the game (Chou & Ting, 2003; Sanjamsai & Phukao, 2018). Considering that individuals spend much time playing, the transformation of learning environments to gamebased ones offers an opportunity for investigation. Digital game-based learning (DGBL) combines digital games with instructional goals and focuses on how learning happens (Becker, 2021; Prensky, 2001). DGBL has advantages over other learning environments in that it promotes better learning achievement (Chang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022). In DGBL, feedback that provides information to the students at every stage is a critical component for learning, as well as content and game characteristics (Burgos et al., 2007). In terms of feedback, pedagogical agents in different forms such as sound, image, text, animation, and human-like visuals, play an important role in learning environments (Baylor & Kim, 2009; Clarebout et al., 2002; Heidig & Clarebout, 2011; Martha & Santoso, 2019). However, the presence and type of pedagogical agents need more study (Dinçer & Doğanay, 2017), because the coherence principle and redundancy effect recommend that there should be no unnecessary information in the design (Castro-Alonso et al., 2019; Clark & Choi, 2007; Mayer, 2017). Accordingly, we evaluated different versions of DGBL with pedagogical agent (with/without) and feedback (explanatory/confirmatory) types on achievement, flow experience, and cognitive load.

2  Theoretical background

2.1  Digital game‑based learning

Individuals play games to have fun and to have a good time. Since games are based on problem solving, overcoming obstacles, and collaborating, they have the potential to help learning happen (Koster, 2005). DGBL integrates learning content and digital games to enhance learning (Prensky, 2002). Moreover, in the learning process, DGBL arouses curiosity (Hsiao et al., 2014), reduces fear and anxiety (Yang et al., 2018), and increases fun, motivation, and achievement (Woo, 2014). The degree of interaction distinguishes gaming from other common educational materials and multimedia-based learning environments (Coller & Scott, 2009). DGBL constitutes a multimedia-based learning environment (Byun & Loh, 2015). In a nutshell, multimedia instruction is the presentation of words (narration, text etc.) and pictures (illustrations, animation etc.) to promote

learning. The cognitive theory of multimedia learning is based on the principle of dual-channels, limited-capacity, and active processing (Mayer, 2009). When designing multimedia learning environments, 12 research-based principles are used for reducing extraneous processing, fostering generative processing, and managing essential processing (Mayer, 2017). However, many studies in the literature are short-term or single-session experiments in labs using narrated animations on computers. Mayer (2017) recommended that these principles should be replicated with lessons for students in classroom settings, long-term experiments, delayed tests, and new media environments such as video, games, and virtual reality. In line with these suggestions, the starting point of the study was to design a DGBL by considering multimedia principles for classroom settings and investigate the variables in long-term experiments.

In DGBL, instructional content is combined with game characteristics. As an informational tool, feedback has critical importance in the game cycle (behaviour, judgment, and feedback loop) for learning, user performance, cognitive engagement, and progress (Garris et al., 2002; Gresalfi & Barnes, 2016).

2.2  Feedback in DGBL

Feedback, or information provided by an agent (Hattie & Timperley, 2007), positively influences students’ effort, performance, motivation, learning, and problemsolving skills in DGBL (Burgos et al., 2007). Feedback provides the flow between the game and the player because it provides information about the outcome of the player’s action and the next step (Mason & Bruning, 2001). Feedback helps maintain a high level of cognitive engagement in games when given in a relevant context and offered immediately, frequently, and clearly (Alexiou & Schippers, 2018). According to Prensky (2001), giving feedback in a game is critical and has a complex structure. Too much or an insufficient amount of feedback can create disappointment for players and can lead to cognitive load (Alexiou & Schippers, 2018). The kinds of contributions that the types of feedback provide to the learning process differ (Hattie & Timperley, 2007; Wisniewski et al., 2020). Wouters and van Oostendorp (2013)’s systematic investigation of the function of instructional support in game-based learning indicates feedback types (FT) as confirmatory and explanatory. Confirmatory feedback states whether the given answer is correct or incorrect; explanatory feedback tells why it is correct or incorrect. According to the feedback principle, explanatory feedback is more effective than its counterpart at helping novice students learn and develop deeper understanding (Johnson & Priest, 2014; Mayer & Johnson, 2010). In contrast, some studies have indicated that FT has no effect on motivation, achievement, enjoyment, and learning outcomes (Liu & Rojewski, 2013; Taguchi et al., 2022). Studies suggest that different types of feedback should be examined in terms of different variables in DGBL (Calvo-Ferrer, 2021; Goldberg & Cannon-Bowers, 2015; Johnson et al., 2017; Nikolayev et al., 2021; Tsai et al., 2015; Zheyu et al., 2021).

2.3  Pedagogical agents in DGBL

A pedagogical agent (PA) is a lifelike onscreen character who provides feedback to the user in terms of learning, navigation, and guidance in multimedia learning environments (Baylor, 2002; Heidig & Clarebout, 2011). A PA can assume the form of human-like characters, animation, voice, text, and positively affect success, motivation, and attitude in the learning process (Atkinson, 2002; Dinçer & Doğanay, 2016; Kim & Baylor, 2016) According to the embodiment principle of the multimedia learning, if a PA exhibits human characteristics (gesturing, facial expression etc.), individuals learn more meaningfully (Mayer & DaPra, 2012). PA appears as an indispensable component of DGBL and positively affects retention (Goldberg & Cannon-Bowers, 2015), achievement (Zhan et al., 2022), team performance (Terzidou & Tsiatsos, 2014), and motivation (Osman & Lee, 2014). PA can help the player overcome the challenges and continue to engage, and, as a result, provide flow state. However, PA can cause cognitive load in working memory because of its different elements (Dinçer & Doğanay, 2016). It is therefore recommended to compare PA types to investigate their differences (Dinçer & Doğanay, 2017), and design them to avoid overload (Guo et al., 2015). Also, conducting in-depth qualitative interviews is recommended to reveal students’ perceptions of PA and the game itself in more detail (Guo & Goh, 2016).

2.4  Flow experience in DGBL

Flow experience represents the individual’s being in a positive psychological state by excluding all irrelevant feelings and thoughts while doing a specific activity. Activities that provide a flow experience include features such as clear goals, skill, difficulty (challenge), concentration, control, and ensure intense participation (Csikszentmihalyi, 2013). Flow gives individuals happiness and enjoyment. Flow state investigations began with the examination of individuals engaged in activities such as rock climbing, dance etc., and were later applied in the context of many fields and learning environments (Byrne et al., 2003; Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). When participating in an activity, participants’ concentration, the exclusion of irrelevant thoughts, and the promotion of fun benefit the learning process (Chang et al., 2017). In DGBL environments, participants have higher flow levels (Admiraal et  al., 2011; Kiili, 2005b). Participants’ experience of the flow in the game environment increases performance, makes significant contributions to learning outcomes, helps them undertake new and challenging tasks, and helps in the development of cognitive strategies (Admiraal et al., 2011; Tsai et al., 2016). For these reasons, game designs that consider the elements of the flow experience strengthen players’ engagement with the game (Fu et al., 2009; Kiili, 2005b). According to the research, there is a strong relationship between flow and learning, but more studies are needed on this topic. In addition, different game mechanics need to be researched to explore their impact on the flow experience (Perttula et al., 2017).

Among the prerequisites for flow experience are clear goals, immediate feedback, skill, and challenge (Buil et al., 2019). The balance between challenge and skill has

a significant and positive impact on the experience of the flow (Yen & Lin, 2022). When the level of challenge is matched to the skill level, it can lead to a state of flow in which the individual is fully immersed in the activity (Hwang et al., 2012). Therefore, it is important to investigate the balance of skill and challenge along with flow theory in DGBL with FT.

2.5  Cognitive load in DGBL

Cognitive load theory attempts to explain the effect of information processing load on individuals’ ability to process new information and store it in long-term memory. (Sweller et al., 2019). Owing to the limited capacity of short-term memory, presenting too much information in a complex structure may create cognitive overload on individuals (Kılıç-Çakmak, 2007; Wilhelm et al., 2013). In case of cognitive overload, remembering information, learning, and transfer skills are negatively affected, which may cause students to spend more time in the material and get lost (Eveland & Dunwoody, 2001). According to Duffy and Smith (2014)’s experiment in the game environment, participants spend more time committing the number to memory when they experience a high cognitive load. For this reason, cognitive load is an important variable to be considered when developing multimedia learning materials. It is important to reduce extraneous cognitive load, manage instinct cognitive load, and increase germane cognitive load (Mayer, 2017; Wu et al., 2020).

The use of non-learning resources such as interaction, task completion, navigation, and exploration in DGBL may also cause cognitive load (Kalyuga & Plass, 2011). While extraneous cognitive load negatively affects learning outcomes (Beserra et  al., 2014) and flow experience (Chang et  al., 2018), adding instructional features to DGBL reduces both intrinsic and extraneous cognitive loads (Liao et al., 2019). Agent-based guidance (Moreno & Mayer, 2005) and feedback (Mayer & Johnson, 2010) are examples of Instructional features in DGBL. According to Hawlitschek and Joeckel (2017), the findings on cognitive load are uncertain, possibly due to the use of different educational features in different types of games in the literature. Although pedagogical agents make positive contributions to the learning process, they can create visual intensity and therefore increase the cognitive load on the screen. Research results on whether PA creates a cognitive load do not overlap, and additional studies are needed (Dinçer & Doğanay, 2016). In this research, we designed two different types of human-like PA as visual + voice (with) and voice alone (without). The reason for using these two forms is to reveal the effect of a screen image PA on cognitive load and, accordingly, other dependent variables such as achievement, flow experience, spent time, skill, and challenge level.

4.3  Participants

154 first-year students who took the IT course in the education faculty of a university in Türkiye participated in the study. We chose four classes, considering departments and students’ technical backgrounds. At first, each class had an average of 46 students. Owing to the insufficient number of tools and equipment, and the difficulty of working in a crowded classroom, we divided each group into two. We conducted the experiments in 8 randomly assigned groups in total. We excluded some students because they did not participate in the experiment or did not complete the data collection tools. Table 2 summarizes the detailed information about participants.

4.4  Procedure

We added versions of the game to an LMS as Group A, B, C, D, each protected with user authorization. Each group could enter their authorized page. The experiments lasted for two weeks, as two 70-min sessions. In the ­10th week of the academic calendar, we informed the students about the topics, process, and voluntary participation. After logging into the LMS and completing the pre-test, they started the game and played the first five levels. They completed the cognitive load, challenge-skill level, and flow experience scale. In the ­11th week, they played the remaining five levels of the game. They completed the cognitive load, challenge-skill level measurement, flow experience scale, and post-test. We calculated mean values for cognitive load, challenge-skill level, and flow experience. After the experiments, we conducted open-ended questions to all students (coded as K1, K2…K154) and conducted semi-structured interviews with eight students by identifying two students (post-test scores in the lower group and higher group) from four experimental groups. These students are coded as S1, S2 …S8). Four weeks later, all students completed the follow-up test.

4.5  Data collection and analysis

4.5.1  Quantitative data

Achievement test  Before the experiments, we prepared and tested all data collection tools. First, we developed 30 questions for an achievement test according to competence areas. Regarding the questions, expert opinion was received from five lecturers who were giving the IT course. As a result of these expert opinions, we made corrections in 9 questions, and 3 questions were removed because they were deemed unsuitable. We applied the form consisting of 27 questions to 10 pre-service teachers as a pilot to determine the average completion time (19 min) and to discover whether there were errors. After the pilot, we applied it to 214 (second year and beyond) students via online forms. The selection criteria for participants were based on their having taken a computer course. While scoring the test, correct answers were worth 1 and incorrect answers worth 0 points. Since the kurtosis and skewness values ranged between -1 and + 1, the data was deemed suitable for conducting validity and reliability analysis. With TAP (Test Analysis Program) software, we determined that two questions did not carry critical values. Excluding them, we re-analysed 25 items. As the items in the test had different difficulty values, the KR-20 (Kuder-Richardson) coefficient was used in the reliability analysis (Quaigrain & Arhin, 2017). The KR-20 reliability coefficient for the test was 0.95, which is quite close to 1, and as such indicated that the test was reliable. Additionally, the average difficulty of the test was 0.69; and its distinctiveness was 0.73. The achievement test with 25 questions was used as pre-test, post-test, and follow-up in the experiment process.

Flow experience scale  Chang et al. (2017) developed the flow experience scale in the context of GBL based on the flow experience dimensions proposed by Csikszentmihalyi and the general state flow scale developed by Pearce et al. (2005). This 5-point Likert type scale consists of three factors: enjoyment, engagement, and control. The researchers stated that the validity and reliability of the scale was provided in the context of university students and GBL. In this study, we adapted the flow experience scale to Turkish. Three field experts translated the scale into Turkish. For language validity, the scale was applied to 32 experts. The selection of these experts is based on good knowledge of both English and Turkish. English and Turkish forms were applied with a one-week break. English and Turkish scores had a positive relationship around the 0.90 level (p < 0.01). As a result, the language validity of the scale was determined.

For confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), after playing a game, the Turkish form of the scale was applied to 412 university students. CFA results showed acceptable fit indices (χ2/df = 3.56; GFI = 0.93; NFI = 0.97; CFI = 98; SRMR = 0.09) (Wood, 2008). Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of the scale was 0.91 for total score; enjoyment (Factor 1) 0.93; engagement (Factor 2) 0.69; control (3. Factor) 0.91. The flow experience scale was confirmed in the context of the university students and the Turkish language.

Challenge‑ skill level and elapsed time  Important indicators of flow experience in the literature include a balance between the individual’s skill level and challenge of the activity. The literature offers various approaches to measure challenge and skill level; one of them is the 5-point Likert type with probe, which appears in Pearce et al. (2005) ’s study. They measured challenges and skill levels by asking questions of the participants at the end of each activity. In the current study, based on questions developed by Pearce et al., the following questions were prepared to determine the difficulty and skill level of the participants.

•	 How challenging did you find level x? •	 Were your skills appropriate for understanding level x?

Experts evaluated the questions to determine their validity. In the experiment process, the questions were asked after each level. Each student’s skill level and challenge level were calculated into an average score. Elapsed time is among the indicators of general tendency towards flow (Nakamura & Csikszentmihalyi, 2014). Elapsed time in the DGBL environment was based on the number of repeats participants took at each level and their remaining time; the system calculated it in seconds for each student.

Cognitive load scale  We used the cognitive load scale developed by Paas and van Merrienboer (1993), adapted to Turkish by Kılıç and Karadeniz (2004). This scale consists of a single item as the 9-point scale (Subjective Rating Scale) and measures the efforts of students while performing a task. Its Cronbach Alpha coefficient was 0.78.

4.5.2  Qualitative data collection

We developed a form consisting of open-ended questions to obtain the students’ opinions about the DGBL environment. In this form, there were open-ended questions about the DGBL environment, its effects on the learning process, types of feedback and pedagogical agents, elapsed time, skill, and challenge levels. Following its implementation, the open-ended form was applied to all students via an electronic form, and two students from each group (scoring in the lower group and scoring in the upper group) were identified, and semi-structured interviews were conducted with eight students. In the semi-structured interviews, probe questions were added to the aforementioned questions. We conducted semi-structured interviews with students individually in a laboratory environment outside of class hours. Examples of the questions include:

•	 What are your opinions on DGBL? •	 What are your opinions on the visuals, scenarios, and feedback types? •	 How did the presence/absence of the PA affect your learning process? •	 How did the explanatory/confirmatory FT affect your learning process? •	 What needs to be improved and/or corrected in the DGBL? •	 Were the level times and challenges appropriate to your skill level?

4.5.3  Data analysis

In the analysis of data, we used MS Excel to enter raw data, Lisrel 8.80 software for confirmatory factor analysis, TAP (Test Analysis Program) software for validity and reliability analyses of the achievement tests, and SPSS 24 for the distribution of data and to analyse research problems. In the analysis of quantitative data, we conducted descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean and standard deviation, Table 3) and 2 × 2 × 3 mixed ANOVA for the achievement variable. We carried out Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) and correlation analysis for the relationships among variables

In the analysis of qualitative data, we used content analysis for validity and reliability based on the views of two experts. Following analysis of the data, emerging themes were compared, and the similarity of the themes was calculated as 87.5%. For conflict themes, consensus was reached with experts and themes agreed upon.

5  Results and discussions

5.1  The effect of pedagogical agents and feedback types on achievement

5.3  Relationship between flow experience, cognitive load, achievement, elapsed time, skill level, and challenge level

We conducted Pearson correlation analyses to examine the relationships among achievement (post-test), flow experience, elapsed time, skill, challenge level, and cognitive load variables. Table 6 presents these relationships.

The results showed that flow experience, elapsed time, challenge level, and cognitive load had weak negative significant relationships (p < 0.05). While there was a weak significant positive relationship between flow experience and skill level (p < 0.05), there was a moderate significant positive relationship with achievement (p < 0.01). This implied that students with high flow experience had lower cognitive load, elapsed time, and challenge level, and higher skill level and achievement in the DGBL.

Studies have concluded that there are positive relationships among flow experience and variables representing the learning process, such as learning, performance, and success (Admiraal et al., 2011; Barzilai & Blau, 2014; Brom et al., 2014; Chen, 2017; Kiili et al., 2012; Kiili, 2005a). With the cognitive load, challenge level and elapsed time had moderate significant positive relationships (p < 0.01). Also, there was a negative relationship between cognitive load and skill level (p < 0.01). The study found a negative relationship between cognitive load and achievement, but not a significant one. These results implied that students with high cognitive load faced more challenges and spent more time doing the tasks. Conversely, students with low cognitive load had a higher skill level in the DGBL. In accordance with these findings, Xu (2016) stated that excessive cognitive load causes various problems in the game-based learning environment. Very challenging tasks can cause students to spend too much effort and experience high cognitive load (Wang & Tseng, 2014; Xu et al., 2021). Additionally, studies have shown that there is a significant negative relationship between flow experience and cognitive load (Chang et al., 2017, 2018; Hong et al., 2020).

5.4  Students’ views on DGBL

Qualitative data related to the learning process and achievement presented improvement regardless of the PA and FT. For example, S1 (explanatory FT/ with PA/low group) noted, “I did not know Excel at all, I improved it a lot compared to the past… I did not see the alignment before, I realized it with feedback later, then I learned it.” Similarly, S8 (explanatory FT/ without PA/low group) stated, “The game allows you to learn better, whether it’s feedback or guidance. Even if there was agent on the screen, I don’t think it would change much anyway because you focus on the game and sound affects you.” Although learning improved in confirmatory feedback groups, low group students required more feedback. For instance, S7 (confirmatory FT/ without PA/low group) noted, “I think the game was fun… Feedback was insufficient. …I couldn’t find the indentation feature, especially when it said increase indentation. I had to play that level twice.” S3 (confirmatory FT/ with PA/high group) said, “Little information was enough for me because after the PA told me it was wrong, I eventually understood what I had done wrong. I did not even know where the sound was coming from. So, it did not matter to me (the image of the PA), even if it wasn’t.” or S4 (confirmatory FT/ without PA/high group) said, "…obviously, I did not pay much attention to the PA, I did not see it because I focused to game.” Students generally stated that the appearance of the PA on the screen was unimportant.

Explanatory feedback types have higher effect sizes in the context of achievement than confirmatory feedback types (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). However, these studies are not comparisons in DGBL. The reason for the lack of difference may be that tasks can also be explored without feedback. For PA, in Heidig and Clarebout (2011)’s systematic literature review study, there was no significant difference in terms of the learning process in the context of the PA in most of the studies. However, according to other studies, PA increases achievement (Dinçer & Doğanay, 2016; Dinçer & Yavuz, 2013; İzmirli et al., 2017; Martha & Santoso, 2019; Terzidou & Tsiatsos, 2014; Wiggins et al., 2019). Such studies, however, were conducted in different learning environments.

The qualitative findings uncovered that elapsed time in the game differed according to the types of feedback. For example, in groups where feedback was confirmatory, students spent more time in the game than in the quantitative data. Students who had high achievement with confirmatory feedback stated that the

feedback level was sufficient and that they found the right answer by exploring and trying. Students who had low achievement stated that they needed more explanations and hints. K62, who spent more time in the confirmatory feedback group, indicated, "When there is a mistake in the task, a hint should be given". Similarly, K74 stated “Perhaps the time can be increased a little more”; K63 indicated, “I had difficulties in some parts because it was new learning.” In terms of a solution, they suggested explanatory feedback and additional time.

The expressions of the students who were in the explanatory feedback groups and the students who spent the average time in the confirmatory feedback groups showed that they found the game funny. Expressions gathered under the theme of fun, were interest, curiosity, fluent, immersive, and exciting and providing focus. For example, “..the game made us focus (K89)”, “It increased my participation in this lesson and aroused curiosity (K46) and “Although Excel is a difficult subject, this game both interested me and made it easier to learn (K115)”. They stated that the game provides focusing and arouses interest and curiosity.

According to a student with a low cognitive load stated, “The visuals were simple and understandable, since it was not too complicated, we could play the game more easily. The striking colours were visually clear (S8).” Studies have shown that while multimedia materials with complex designs create excessive cognitive load, environments developed in accordance with design principles do not (Bartsch & Cobern, 2003; Korakakis et al., 2009). The research revealed that students did not have excessive cognitive load and showed that multimedia design principles were considered in the game elements.

A student with low flow experience, K136, who repeated the levels often, stated, "… I found it a bit boring." A student with high flow experience level K1 noted that "Speed and time increase the feeling of competition." Similarly, S8 indicated, “When we made a mistake, our score dropped, and we became more careful. I felt excitement because of the time.” Studies have revealed that gamebased learning contributes positively to the flow experience (Chang et al., 2017; Kiili et al., 2012; Kiili, 2005b).

Students drew attention to the proximity of the scenario to reality and stated that it affected their game-playing processes. For example, S2 stated, “Regarding the scenario, we really tried doing tasks fast as if we would be hired.” Similarly, K3 said, “The tasks are excellent, I felt as if I was getting a job myself, which is what most of us will encounter in the future. I think the criteria sought are in line with the truth.” S5 indicated, “Levels were in cafe, restaurant, or market, and tasks were dependent on the places.” Flow experience, skill, and challenge levels affected the learning process. For example, K3 said, “Sometimes I got 2 stars, this made me ambitious and made me play for the second or third time. I think that having one star means that I did not learn well enough, let me try again.” Kiili et al. (2012)’s research revealed that students earned high scores in terms of flow experience dimensions such as skill-challenge level, clear goals, feedback, playability, control, reward, focus, loss of self-consciousness, and time. As such, flow experience and game performance have a positive relationship.

6  Conclusions and implication

This study revealed and documented variables of PA, FT, flow experience, cognitive load, achievement, elapsed time, skill level, and challenge level in DGBL with quantitative and qualitative data in classroom settings. As many prior studies have addressed these variables separately, the contribution of this study to the literature was its comparative examination and explanation of variables in DGBL. Instructional designers and instructors may consider these variables when designing DGBL as an innovation in the learning process.

7  Limitations and future work

This research considered DGBL within the scope of an IT course for university students. FT were explanatory and confirmatory in DGBL. Future studies can investigate the effects of different types of feedback. In addition, they can use up-to-date approaches such as adaptive or artificial intelligence technologies in feedback design. PA were with and without in the study. Future research can investigate the effect of different types of PA by employing features such as interaction, different facial expressions, and selection. Cognitive load measurement was subjectively considered. Future studies may reveal the effects of the DGBL environment and the display of the on-screen PA in different ways with the use of an eye tracking device. In the research, students played games individually on a computer. Future studies might design multi-user games, including competition compatible with different devices.
