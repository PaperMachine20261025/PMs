Existing research has widely examined the effects of AI-assisted speaking training on language performance and learning outcomes; however, the experiential mechanisms through which AI- assisted speaking training affordances (AISTA) shape learners' communication-oriented behav­ iors remain underexplored. In particular, limited empirical evidence explains how such affor­ dances are translated into learners’ willingness to communicate (WTC) via flow experience (FE). Drawing on ecological affordance theory and flow theory, the present study conceptualizes AISTA as a core construct and proposes a moderated mediation model in which AISTA influences WTC through FE, with learner agency (LA) capturing individual differences in AI-supported speaking contexts.

Survey data were collected from 247 Chinese EFL university students following a four-week AI- assisted speaking practice and analyzed using structural equation modeling (SEM) with latent main effects and interaction terms. Bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5000 resamples was employed to test mediation and moderation effects. The results showed that AISTA was positively associated with FE, and FE was positively associated with WTC. Flow experience emerged as a key mediator linking AISTA to WTC. Moreover, learner agency significantly moderated the experi­ ential pathway, yielding a pattern of conditional partial mediation: the indirect effect of AISTA on WTC via FE was significant for learners with moderate to high levels of agency but not for those with low agency.

By elucidating the affordance–experience–behavior mechanism underlying AI-assisted speaking training, this study extends ecological affordance theory and flow theory to AI- supported second language learning contexts and highlights learner agency as a critical condi­ tion for translating technological affordances into communicative motivation.

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) has profoundly transformed educational practices, and its integration into foreign language learning has attracted growing scholarly attention. In recent years, generative AI-based speaking tools have proliferated, offering learners more flexible opportunities for oral practice and immersive simulated communication environments. Recent systematic review (e.g., Albedah, 2025) indicates that the application of AI in language education has evolved beyond early computer-assisted instruction to encompass multidimensional functions such as generative feedback, semantic evaluation, and context-based conversational interaction. These technologies have helped alleviate long-standing challenges in traditional

However, AI technologies do not automatically translate into learning success. Prior studies have shown that when human-AI interactions are poorly aligned, learners may experience frustration or even motivational decline (Kim & Su, 2024). Consequently, the educational value of AI lies not in the technology itself but in how learners perceive and mobilize the opportunities it affords. In this regard, AI-assisted speaking training affordances (AISTA) emerge as a critical analytical construct, emphasizing learners’ perception and activation of action possibilities within AI-mediated environments rather than the technical functionalities per se (Song, 2021; Van Lier, 2004).

At the same time, research attention must also be directed to the dynamic psychological dimension of learning. Flow experience (FE), conceptualized by Csikszentmihalyi (1990) as a state of intense immersion, concentration, and enjoyment, has been extensively validated as a key psychological condition that fosters learning motivation and engagement (Egbert, 2004; Engeser & Rheinberg, 2008; Zuniga, 2023). Within AI-mediated interaction, an important yet underexplored question concerns whether AISTA can consistently elicit flow and, in turn, enhance learners' willingness to communicate (WTC) in a second language. WTC is widely regarded as the most immediate antecedent of language output, determined not only by linguistic competence and anxiety but also by the learner's situ­ ational experience (Dewaele & Dewaele, 2018; Kirkpatrick et al., 2024).

Recent findings further suggest that learner autonomy and positive emotional experiences play increasingly significant roles in promoting WTC (Sato, 2025). As Tarp (2025) argues, learners’ psychological experiences in AI-mediated contexts may foster WTC through the joint activation of positive emotions and agentic behaviors. Taken together, these findings indicate that flow experience likely serves as the crucial psychological pathway linking AISTA and WTC.

Moreover, individual differences among learners should not be overlooked. Prior research has demonstrated that learners exposed to the same AI environment may experience markedly different levels of engagement and learning outcomes (Lee & Lee, 2024; Peng & Liang, 2025). Learner agency (LA) has been identified as a key factor underlying such variability, as it shapes how individuals actively construct, regulate, and transform their learning experiences (Reeve & Tseng, 2011; Song, 2021). Yet, the precise role of LA within the technology-psychology-behavior chain remains insufficiently examined, leaving open the question of how learners perceive and mobilize technological resources in AI-mediated contexts. In other words, current research calls for a comprehensive framework that integrates environmental affordances, psychological experience, and learning behavior, thereby revealing how learners activate latent action possibilities in complex technology-enhanced environments.

Building on the above issues, this study adopts Affordance Theory as its overarching theoretical framework. Gibson (1979) first introduced the concept of affordances to refer to the action possibilities that the environment offers to an actor, emphasizing that affordances are not objective functions inherent in the environment but relational outcomes emerging from the interaction between an individual's capabilities and environmental properties (pp. 127–129). Later, Van Lier (2004) introduced this concept into the field of language learning, arguing that linguistic affordances do not stem from technology itself but from learners' ability to perceive, select, activate, and utilize the linguistic resources offered by their environment. In other words, the same educational technology may not afford identical action possibilities to all learners. This theoretical stance provides the rationale for positioning the AI-assisted speaking training affordances (AISTA) as the core construct of the present study.

Within the context of generative AI-based speaking training, the system's immediate feedback, adaptive adjustments, and authentic situational simulations constitute a set of potential affordances. Only when learners actively explore and activate these affordances can effective learning experiences occur (Kordt, 2016; Song, 2021). Integrating AISTA, FE, LA, and WTC within a unified ecological framework thus offers a coherent explanation for the dynamic technology → psychological experience → behavioral motivation chain. From this theoretical standpoint, the limitations of existing research also become more apparent.

2. Theoretical framework

2.1. Ecological affordance theory and the definition of AISTA

As noted above, according to Ecological Affordance Theory, learning opportunities are not automatically generated by technology itself but rather emerge from the dynamic interaction between environmental attributes and learners’ capabilities (Gibson, 1979). Van Lier (2004) further emphasized that language learning occurs only when learners notice, recognize, and mobilize the linguistic re­ sources available in their environment. Drawing on this perspective, the present study defines AI-assisted speaking training affor­ dances (AISTA) as the action possibilities that learners perceive and are able to activate when interacting with generative AI tools. This ecological view provides the theoretical rationale for positioning AISTA at the core of the proposed model.

2.2. Theoretical foundations and definitions of core constructs

Within the overarching ecological framework, the four core variables examined in this study play distinct yet interrelated theo­ retical roles in the proposed model. AISTA, as an external environmental input, does not merely refer to the technological properties of AI tools; rather, it reflects learners’ overall perception of features such as controllability, interactivity, immediacy of feedback, and adaptive adjustment. These perceived attributes collectively determine whether learners can effectively activate the available affor­ dances (Kearney et al., 2019; McMillan & Hwang, 2002).

Correspondingly, FE, derived from Csikszentmihalyi's (1990) Flow Theory, emphasizes immersion, clarity of goals, and the balance between challenge and skill. FE represents a critical psychological state that sustains learning engagement and motivation (Egbert, 2004; Zuniga, 2023). LA, grounded in Reeve and Tseng's (2011) concept of “agentic engagement,” refers to learners' capacity to actively shape their learning conditions through questioning, seeking feedback, and regulating strategies, thereby influencing the extent to which they can activate AISTA (Lai & Lee, 2024).

Finally, WTC, following the pyramid model proposed by MacIntyre et al. (1998), is regarded as the most immediate motivational antecedent of language output. It is jointly influenced by situational confidence, emotional experience, and perceived communicative safety.

2.3. The path mechanism of affordance, experience, and motivation

Integrating the above constructs, a clear “affordance-experience-motivation” chain can be established. Learners’ perception of AISTA constitutes the environmental foundation that triggers FE. When the AI environment is perceived as more controllable, responsive, and meaningful, learners are more likely to experience immersion and enjoyment during interaction. The immediacy of feedback, interactivity, and contextual authenticity offered by AISTA create a balanced challenge-skill environment that facilitates the emergence of flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990; Egbert, 2004). Such positive experiences not only enhance learning enjoyment and self-efficacy but also shift language practice from passive completion to active exploration, thereby stimulating sustained learning motivation and willingness to communicate (Kirkpatrick et al., 2024; Zuniga, 2023).

From the perspective of WTC theory, the core driving force of language output arises from the accumulation of positive situational experiences (MacIntyre et al., 1998). In AI-supported speaking environments, fluent, enjoyable, and psychologically safe interactions can substantially reduce communication anxiety and strengthen learners’ sense of security, consequently increasing their willingness to express themselves in authentic communicative contexts (Alrabai, 2022; Dewaele & Dewaele, 2018). The AISTA → FE → WTC pathway thus reveals the underlying psychological mechanism of technology-enhanced learning and offers a theoretical anchor for understanding how motivation emerges in human-AI symbiotic environments.

Within this process, LA plays a critical conditional role. Learners with high levels of agency are better able to notice and utilize the multiple affordances offered by AI, actively engage with challenges and supports, and are therefore more likely to achieve flow and develop stronger communicative motivation. In contrast, those with low agency may fall into an affordance blindness, failing to fully activate the potential of their learning environment. Overall, this logic chain aligns with the core propositions of positive psychology, technology-assisted learning, and WTC theory, forming the theoretical foundation of the proposed research model.

In summary, the “affordance-experience-motivation” framework elucidates the intrinsic relationships between technological context, psychological experience, and communicative motivation, while underscoring the pivotal role of learner agency in mediating these dynamics. Building on the theoretical framework outlined above, this study is situated in the context of AI-assisted speaking training and aims to examine how learners’ perceptions of technological affordances influence their communication-oriented behavioral tendencies through psychological experience mechanisms. In addition, the study explores the moderating role of learner agency in shaping these relationships. Specifically, the study seeks to address the following research questions:

RQ1: Are learners' perceived AI-assisted speaking training affordances (AISTA) significantly associated with their flow experience (FE) and willingness to communicate (WTC)? This study recruited 278 non-English-major undergraduates from a comprehensive university in China. A total of 261 students completed all experimental sessions and the final survey, resulting in 247 valid responses (valid response rate = 94.6 %). Among the valid sample, participants ranged in age from 18 to 22 years (M = 19.6, SD = 0.9), with females accounting for 67.2 %. All participants had completed at least three semesters of college English courses within the past year, had passed the College English Test Band 4 (CET- 4), and received training in AI-assisted speaking practice prior to the study. To control for external variables, all participants engaged in the experiment under comparable instructional arrangements and technological conditions.

The study adopted Tencent Yuanbao, a generative AI–based speaking practice tool developed by Tencent, as the technological platform. The system supports speech-enabled interaction and automated feedback by analyzing learners’ spoken input in real time. During interaction, the tool provides individualized feedback on grammar, vocabulary, and pronunciation, and adjusts task difficulty and feedback content dynamically to sustain learner engagement and interactional continuity.

To ensure consistency in human-AI interaction, participants were provided with standardized AI prompts specifying the dialogue setup, feedback frequency, difficulty adjustment, and feedback format. For example, students were instructed to initiate each session with a standardized command such as: “Please engage in a 10-min English conversation with me on the topic of ______. Provide light feedback every one to 2 min, and at the end, categorize your feedback into grammar, vocabulary, expression, pronunciation, and content logic.”

Following this, students processed AI feedback using a “record-organize-synthesize” approach: during interaction, they noted key points without interrupting the flow of conversation; after the session, they organized the feedback into categories and summarized frequent errors as well as strategies for improvement.

This training procedure preserved the pedagogical value of AI's immediate feedback while minimizing interference with fluency. It enabled learners to maintain a balance between accuracy and communicative flow and, through systematic reflection and strategy generation, to more effectively activate and utilize AISTA.

3.2. Instruments

Four core variables were measured in this study, all adapted from well-established scales with contextual modifications to fit the AI- assisted speaking practice setting. The items were designed to capture learners’ perceived action possibilities in interaction with AI environments rather than the technical functions of the tool itself (see Table 1). All items were translated into Chinese and refined through bilingual expert review and back-translation procedures.

The AISTA and FE constructs were each measured with six items, while WTC and LA were each measured with five items. All scales used a 7-point Likert format (1 = “strongly disagree,” 7 = “strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating higher levels of the construct. To ensure reliability and validity, the instruments were reviewed by experts and piloted with a small sample before the formal survey. Exploratory factor analysis confirmed the unidimensional structure of the constructs, with all item loadings exceeding 0.60. The Cronbach's alpha values for the four scales ranged from 0.71 to 0.84, indicating satisfactory internal consistency.

For flow experience (FE), the English version of the Flow Short Scale was used, based on the original Flow State Scale developed by Rheinberg et al. (2003). The scale was adapted to the AI-assisted speaking context to capture learners’ momentary immersion, perceived control, and altered sense of time during human-AI interaction.

3.3. Data collection procedure

The experimental period of this study was set to four weeks, based on two major considerations. First, existing research on AI- assisted speaking training commonly adopts short-term designs ranging from three to six weeks, aiming to capture the immediate psychological and behavioral effects of technology use (Tai & Chen, 2024; Yang & Li, 2024). Both FE and WTC are highly state-dependent variables whose accumulative effects can be effectively observed within shorter intervention cycles. Accordingly, a four-week duration was deemed sufficient to yield stable and representative psychological and behavioral indicators.

Second, participants engaged in two AI-assisted speaking sessions per week. While a regular class period lasted approximately 40 min, the AI-mediated speaking task used for the intervention and subsequent evaluation consisted of a standardized 10-min con­ versation, followed by brief note-taking and post-task organization of AI feedback. This design ensured sufficient interactional fre­ quency for learners to become familiar with the AI's feedback patterns and develop stable experiential trajectories, while avoiding fatigue effects or participant attrition that might arise from longer interventions.

With regard to extracurricular use, students were not required to conduct additional AI-based practice outside the scheduled sessions, thereby minimizing potential noise caused by variations in practice frequency. To ensure workload consistency, all partic­ ipants completed the eight assigned AI speaking tasks within designated time windows supervised by the research team. Although extracurricular use was not mandatory, students were permitted to engage in voluntary, low-stakes interactions with AI in their daily communication, maintaining a naturalistic learning ecology.

Regarding topic design, each session included one assigned topic and one open-ended topic. The assigned topics, developed by the research team, covered a range of communicative contexts such as campus life (e.g., “Differences in study habits at university”), inter­ personal communication (e.g., “Managing roommate conflicts”), future planning (e.g., “Career aspirations and expectations”), and social issues (e.g., “The impact of AI on future work”). These topics ensured moderate challenge in terms of linguistic load, cognitive complexity, and interactional depth. The open-ended topics allowed students to initiate conversations based on their personal interests and communicative needs, enhancing authenticity and autonomy. This balance between structured and self-directed tasks maintained consistency in training while preserving individual variation in interactional patterns. Although the speaking practice was imple­ mented over four weeks, the present analysis focuses on cross-sectional survey data collected after the intervention.

3.4. Data analysis

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to test the proposed model and hypotheses in two steps. First, the measurement model was validated to assess the reliability and validity of the latent constructs. Second, the structural model was analyzed to evaluate the path coefficients for H1-H7 and the overall model fit.

After the measurement model was validated, model parameters were estimated using the maximum likelihood (ML) method. Mediation and moderation effects were examined with bias-corrected bootstrapping (5000 resamples) to generate confidence

All continuous variables were mean-centered before constructing interaction terms to reduce the risk of multicollinearity. For significant interactions, simple slope analyses were conducted at high (+1 SD) and low (−1 SD) levels of LA, and slope graphs were generated to illustrate the interaction patterns.

In addition, mediation through FE in the “AISTA → WTC” pathway (H4) was tested, and the analysis further examined whether this indirect effect varied by levels of LA, constituting a moderated mediation effect.

3.5. Control of common method bias

Since all variables were derived from self-reported questionnaire data collected at a single time point, several procedural and statistical remedies were employed to minimize potential common method bias (CMB). Procedurally, participants completed the survey anonymously, with randomized item order and inclusion of reverse-coded items to reduce response pattern bias.

Statistically, an unrotated single-factor test indicated that the first principal factor accounted for 38.97 % of the total variance, which is below the commonly accepted threshold of 50 %. Furthermore, a single-method-factor confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and marker variable technique were conducted for cross-validation. The inclusion of a method factor did not significantly improve model fit (Δχ2 = ns), suggesting that common method bias was unlikely to threaten the validity of the main findings (Podsakoff et al., 2003).

Furthermore, all inter-construct correlations were lower than the square root of the corresponding AVE, providing evidence of discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

Rather than exerting uniform effects across learners, AI-assisted affordances appear to facilitate communicative motivation in a differentiated manner, becoming increasingly effective as learners’ agency enables deeper experiential engagement. This conditional pattern sets the stage for a closer examination of the moderating role of learner agency in the structural pathways, as illustrated in Fig. 2 and discussed in the following section.

4.5. Moderation and conditional mediation analysis

To further clarify how learner agency (LA) shapes the affordance–experience–communication mechanism, interaction terms in the full model (M3) were interpreted together with simple-slope visualizations (Fig. 2). High and low LA were probed at ±1 SD.

First, LA significantly moderated the AISTA → FE pathway (AISTA × LA → FE; Table 3), indicating that learners with higher agency were more effective in converting perceived AI-assisted affordances into flow-relevant engagement (Fig. 2a). Consistent with an ecological view of affordances, this pattern suggests that the same AI environment affords deeper immersion only when learners actively appropriate and mobilize available resources.

Second, the FE × LA interaction predicting WTC did not reach conventional significance in M3 (Table 3), although the simple slopes still showed a stronger positive FE → WTC association under high LA than under low LA (Fig. 2b). This suggests that agency may amplify the motivational consequences of flow, but this moderation should be interpreted cautiously given the statistical uncertainty of the interaction term.

Third, LA significantly moderated the AISTA → WTC pathway (AISTA × LA → WTC; Table 3). As shown in Fig. 2c, the direction of the direct affordance–WTC link differed across LA levels (positive under high LA and weakly negative under low LA). Importantly, this pattern is best interpreted as a conditional trend in the direct pathway, rather than decisive evidence that a direct effect consistently emerges for high-agency learners.

Taken together, the results indicate that LA primarily conditions the indirect pathway from AISTA to WTC by strengthening the affordance-to-flow link (and, descriptively, the flow-to-WTC link), yielding a pattern of conditional indirect effects (moderated mediation) rather than a uniform direct effect across learners.

5.1. The relationship between AISTA and WTC: the mediating role of FE

The findings indicate that the relationship between AISTA and WTC was primarily realized through the mediating role of FE. In the overall model, the direct effect of AISTA on WTC was not significant, whereas the indirect effect through the path “AISTA → FE → WTC” was significant (see Tables 4 and 5). This suggests that when learners engage with AI-assisted speaking resources, changes in their WTC are mainly achieved through the activation of flow. In other words, the interactivity, immediate feedback, and personalized support provided by AI environments are effectively transformed into higher levels of communicative motivation only when learners enter a state of immersion.

This finding underscores the pivotal role of FE in the “technology-psychology-behavior” chain. It is consistent with previous studies that have identified FE as a driving force of positive emotional experiences in language learning (Dewaele & Pavelescu, 2019; Dewaele et al., 2019; Mercer & MacIntyre, 2014; Shao et al., 2020). In this study, the mediating effect of FE was not only statistically significant but also of medium-to-large magnitude, aligning with the benchmarks suggested by Preacher and Kelley (2011). This demonstrates that FE is not a secondary psychological response but a core construct explaining the link between AISTA and WTC.

Taken together, the effect of AISTA on WTC was mainly achieved through the mediating pathway of FE. This finding reinforces the central role of FE in AI-supported language learning and provides new empirical evidence on how technological affordances are transformed into WTC through positive psychological experiences. Notably, the results resonate with prior work. For instance, Yang and Li (2024) found that challenge-skill balance and immediate feedback are critical conditions for fostering learner engagement in AI-assisted contexts, which is consistent with the mediating effect identified in this study. Importantly, the nonsignificant direct effect of AISTA on WTC (H3) should not be interpreted as a failure of AI-supported speaking environments. Rather, it provides theoretical evidence that technological affordances exert their influence on communicative motivation only when learners actively engage with and shape these affordances through their learning experience.

5.2. Multi-level moderation of LA: Why the key to Human-AI interaction lies in the “human”

After confirming the mediating role of FE, this study further examined the moderating role of LA. The results revealed that LA exerted significant effects on multiple pathways in the model, including “AISTA → FE,” “FE → WTC,” and the direct path “AISTA → WTC.” Overall, learners with higher levels of LA exhibited a more robust and stable pattern of experiential engagement in AI-assisted speaking training. This finding not only supports the interaction hypotheses but also aligns with the ecological linguistic view that environmental resources must be actively mobilized by agents in order to translate into learning outcomes (Kordt, 2016; Van Lier, 2004). This indicates that the true driving force behind AI-enabled learning does not lie in the algorithm or platform itself, but rather in learners’ ability to perceive, select, and reconstruct technological resources—that is, in their agentic empowerment rather than passive technologization.

First, on the “AISTA → FE” path, LA significantly moderated the relationship (see Table 3, Fig. 2a). Learners with high LA were better able to draw on the multidimensional resources of AISTA, creating a challenge-skill balance and thereby entering a state of flow more easily. In contrast, low-LA learners tended to fall into “affordance blindness,” failing to consistently achieve flow due to their lack of initiative. This finding supports van Lier's (2004) argument that affordances exist only when they are perceived and activated by learners. From an educational psychology standpoint, this divergence essentially reflects variations in perceived control and task value (Pekrun, 2006). High-agency learners, through proactive engagement with AI-mediated affordances, cultivate a heightened sense of controllability and meaningfulness, which in turn fosters sustained engagement and a more enduring state of enjoyment.

Second, on the “FE → WTC” path, learner agency appeared to condition the strength of the association, as reflected in the simple slope patterns shown in Fig. 2b. High-LA learners were more likely to translate the enjoyment and confidence they experienced during AI practice into stronger links between FE and WTC, actively seeking communicative opportunities both inside and outside the classroom. By contrast, low-LA learners might experience positive emotions temporarily during practice but often lacked sustained agency due to “learned passivity” (Seyri & Rezaee, 2024), which weakened the long-term relationship between FE and WTC. Previous research has also noted that not all positive emotional experiences translate into communicative behavior, with individual differences in LA serving as a critical conditional factor (Lee & Chiu, 2024; Shao et al., 2020). This finding further reveals that the activation of flow does not inherently lead to communicative action; rather, its effectiveness depends on whether learners possess an agentic mechanism that enables them to convert emotional experience into communicative motivation—that is, a self-regulatory process linking emotion and action within human-AI interaction.

Finally, the moderating effect of LA on the direct path “AISTA → WTC” proved particularly noteworthy. While this path was nonsignificant in the overall model (see Table 4), the simple slope patterns suggest that a positive AISTA → WTC association was more likely to emerge under high-LA conditions, although the conditional direct effects themselves did not reach statistical significance (see Table 5). Low-LA learners, however, showed slopes close to zero or even slightly negative. This indicates that AI environments with diverse functions and high degrees of freedom are not necessarily “friendly” to all learners. For those with low LA, the complexity of interactive options and feedback may trigger cognitive overload or choice overload, undermining their sense of control and selfefficacy, thereby suppressing WTC. This phenomenon echoes concerns about a “technological inequality effect,” where learners with high and low levels of LA experience markedly different benefits from the same AI environment (Liu et al., 2025). This suggests that the proliferation of AI technology does not automatically lead to equity; rather, it may amplify learning disparities through differences in learner agency. In sum, LA not only determines whether technological affordances can be effectively utilized in the learning process but also shapes the strength of the connection between positive experiences and genuine WTC. This finding reinforces the dynamic interpretation of the technology-psychology-behavior chain. In AI-empowered learning environments, the root of learning variation lies not in the technology itself but in whether learners actively mobilize and exercise their own agency (Guo & Li, 2024). Therefore, the future of human-AI collaboration should not be understood as AI replacing humans, but rather as AI amplifying humans—its true value lies in activating, rather than substituting, learners’ will to learn and their capacity for self-growth. This human-centered perspective offers both ethical and pedagogical implications for AI-empowered education and lays a foundational framework for future research on human-centered AI learning design.

6. Conclusion, limitations, and future directions

The findings of this study confirmed the dynamic assumptions proposed in the “Affordance-Experience-Motivation” chain model, demonstrating that the effectiveness of AI-assisted speaking training is not automatically determined by technology itself but depends on how learners perceive, activate, and transform ecological affordances (AISTA) into meaningful learning actions. Grounded in Affordance Theory and Flow Theory within the framework of WTC, this study systematically examined the interrelationships among AISTA, FE, LA, and WTC among Chinese EFL university students.

The results revealed that AISTA was significantly correlated with FE, and FE showed a significant positive relationship with WTC. Learners who actively utilized AI-provided feedback and contextual resources were more likely to experience a state of immersion characterized by a challenge-skill balance, which in turn fostered stronger communicative motivation. Moreover, FE served as a mediator between AISTA and WTC, suggesting that the pedagogical value of AI environments is primarily realized through learners’ psychological experiences. Notably, while the direct effect of AISTA on WTC was non-significant in the overall model (and remained statistically non-significant across probed LA levels), the indirect effect via flow strengthened as learner agency increased, indicating a pattern of conditional indirect effects (moderated mediation). This highlights learner agency as the pivotal link that connects tech­ nological potential with communicative behavior.

Further analysis showed that LA exerted significant moderating effects on all three core pathways: learners with high agency were more capable of activating multidimensional affordances, thereby strengthening the association between FE and WTC, and trans­ ferring AI-mediated experiences directly into real communicative contexts. This finding substantiates the principle that the educa­ tional value of AI depends on human agency, emphasizing the central role of individual differences in AI-empowered learning. It also provides empirical evidence for the development of learner-centered human-AI collaborative models in future intelligent learning environments.

Despite these contributions, the study has several limitations that call for further research. First, the scope of the sample limits the external validity of the findings. The participants were Chinese EFL undergraduates, a group with relatively homogeneous cultural and demographic characteristics. Caution is therefore warranted when generalizing the results to other populations and contexts. Future research should test the model across different cultural and learner groups, using measurement invariance testing and multilevel modeling to enhance generalizability and robustness (Vandenberg & Lance, 2000).

Second, the reliance on a single data source may have introduced common method bias. Although multiple procedural and sta­ tistical controls were employed in this study, the use of self-report questionnaires cannot fully eliminate the risk of confounding or underestimating effects (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Future research should adopt multi-source and mixed-method designs. For instance, classroom recordings or ethnographic observations could be used to capture behavioral evidence, AI system logs could be extracted to quantify interaction frequency and feedback patterns, and wearable sensors could monitor physiological indicators such as heart rate variability and skin conductance. These multimodal data sources would allow for triangulation of psychological and behavioral constructs, thereby reducing common method bias and enhancing reliability and explanatory power (Liu et al., 2025).

Third, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. As this study employed a cross-sectional survey design, it was unable to capture causal directionality or dynamic processes. The relationships among AISTA, FE, WTC, and LA may not be static or linear, but instead fluctuate over time and across contexts. Both FE and WTC are state-like variables, often shaped by immediate classroom conditions (Lee & Chiu, 2024). Future studies could employ longitudinal or experimental designs to uncover causal pathways and temporal dynamics. On the one hand, longitudinal tracking or experience sampling could be used to observe the developmental trajectories of AISTA, FE, and WTC over a semester or longer, thereby revealing the cumulative effects of sustained AI-assisted speaking practice on psychological and behavioral outcomes (Lee & Chiu, 2024; Mercer & MacIntyre, 2014). On the other hand, experimental manipulations of AISTA or LA (e.g., by providing different intensities of AI practice or implementing pedagogical interventions to foster LA) could help validate the causal links and boundary conditions of the core pathways, thereby strengthening causal expla­ nations (Hwang et al., 2025; Reeve & Tseng, 2011).

Fourth, the restricted range of variables limited the comprehensiveness of the theoretical framework. WTC is a complex construct influenced by multiple factors. For instance, motivation, language anxiety, and teacher support have all been shown to play important roles (Khajavy et al., 2018; Liu & Jackson, 2008), yet these factors were not included in the present model. Future research could expand the framework by incorporating additional variables. For example, it would be useful to examine how the interplay of positive emotions (e.g., enjoyment, engagement) and negative emotions (e.g., anxiety, frustration) affects WTC, or to consider environmental support factors such as teacher feedback styles and peer collaboration, and how these interact with the affordances provided by AI. Fifth, the reliance on a single platform may have limited the external validity of the findings. This study used Tencent Yuanbao, a relatively specific AI-based speaking practice application, whose functional features may have influenced the results. For instance, different AI systems vary in speech recognition accuracy, feedback modes, and conversational styles (Lo, 2025), and these differences may shape learners’ experiences and outcomes. Thus, the generalizability of the present findings to other AI platforms remains un­ certain. Future research should validate and compare the model across multiple platforms and tools. For example, applying the present measures and model to open-domain dialogue systems such as ChatGPT or other mainstream speaking practice applications could help determine whether the relationships among AISTA, FE, LA, and WTC hold consistently. If differences emerge across platforms, further analysis should examine how specific technological features contribute to those variations. Expanding data collection to broader samples and environments will improve the external validity of the findings and provide targeted insights for optimizing the educational applications of different AI tools.

In sum, the limitations of this study highlight that AI technology itself is not a decisive factor in educational effectiveness. Learner individual differences, cultural contexts, research methodologies, and technological choices can all profoundly shape the relationships among AISTA, FE, and WTC. Future studies should advance theoretical models and pedagogical practices within frameworks that are cross-cultural, multi-method, dynamic, and multi-platform. Such efforts will contribute to promoting both the equity and effectiveness of AI-enabled language learning.
