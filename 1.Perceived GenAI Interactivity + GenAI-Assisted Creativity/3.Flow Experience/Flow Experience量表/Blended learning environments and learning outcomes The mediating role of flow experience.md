Blended learning environments and learning outcomes: The mediating role of flow experience

Keywords: Blended learning Flexibility Flow experience Interaction Management education

Despite the growing importance of blended learning environments (BLEs) across disciplines, the extant research provides inconsistent results on their performance effects. While many studies find positive effects of BLEs on learning outcomes, others either find no significant performance differences between BLEs and traditional lectures or reveal that learners prefer face-to-face en­ counters. These contradictory findings suggest that differences in the effects of BLEs and other instruction formats on learning outcomes might be more nuanced than conceptualized in prior research. Specifically, research in the fields of management education and educational psychol­ ogy indicates that these inconsistent findings might be addressed by carefully analyzing specific design characteristics of BLEs and by considering indirect effects of psychological characteristics. Thus, we introduce flow theory, which describes a learner’s complete immersion in an activity, and we propose that experiencing flow mediates the positive effects of flexibility and inter­ action—two central characteristics of BLEs—on learning outcomes. The results of a quasiexperiment with 115 graduate-level management students support our hypotheses. We contribute to blended learning research by looking beyond the direct effects of instruction formats on learning outcomes and by investigating the mechanisms as well as the boundary conditions behind active knowledge construction in BLEs from a constructivist perspective. Our results suggest that instructors should ensure that they promote both flexibility and interaction in BLEs, and they should account for learners’ cognitive characteristics.

1. Introduction

With the rise of a constructivist perspective on teaching and learning (Webb et al., 2005; Wiechowski & Washburn, 2014), blended learning environments (BLEs) have gained in importance in various research fields such as educational psychology (Leutner, 2014), educational technology (Evans, 2008), higher education (Snowball, 2014), and management education (Arbaugh, 2014; Arbaugh et al., 2009). Constructivism understands learning as a mental activity that lets learners actively construct knowledge from available information in relation to their individual backgrounds (Al-Huneidi & Schreurs, 2012). Blended learning supports this active knowledge construction by the learners through “opportunities for enhanced learner control of the learning process and group cohesiveness and collaboration” (Arbaugh, 2014). Blended learning is defined as a combination of online and face-to-face instruction with reduced in-class time, as some course components are provided online (Graham et al., 2013). As such, blended learning combines two originally separate modes of teaching and learning: traditional, classroom-based, face-to-face teaching and distributed learning

Research into blended learning has mainly focused on examining the impact of BLEs and their characteristics on cognitive learning outcomes (e.g., exam performance), self-assessments of knowledge, and learners’ reactions (e.g., learner satisfaction). However, this stream of research offers inconsistent results. For instance, Dowling et al. (2003) and Webb et al. (2005) observe significantly improved exam and assignment results for BLEs when compared to traditional lectures. In contrast, Chen and Jones (2007) and Cosgrove and Olitsky (2015) find no significant effect of the instruction format on exam performance, while Bryant et al. (2003) find better exam results for traditional lectures.

These inconsistent findings suggest that indirect effects might influence learning outcomes in BLEs. Educational psychology research shows that cognitive characteristics play an important role in learning (Mayer, 2014; Moreno & Mayer, 2007). Surprisingly, such cognitive characteristics have largely been neglected in BLE research thus far. Flow theory, which stems from educational psychology research, indicates that flow experience might be particularly important in this context. Flow is defined as a positive psychological state that is challenging, rewarding, and enjoyable, and it describes a learner’s complete immersion in an activity (Bressler & Bodzin, 2013; Csikszentmihalyi, 1975). Flow positively influences learning outcomes through increased exploratory behavior and greater depth of information processing (Bressler & Bodzin, 2013; Finneran & Zhang, 2005). Thus, a positive effect of BLEs on learning outcomes might be conditional on achieving a flow experience.

Hence, we combine perspectives found in BLE research and educational psychology research to posit that flow experience might constitute a boundary condition that helps explain the inconsistent effects of BLEs on learning outcomes. More specifically, we hy­ pothesize that flow experience mediates the effects of the two central characteristics of BLEs—course flexibility and interaction—on learning outcomes measured on the basis of self-assessments of knowledge. A quasi-experiment with 115 graduate management students confirms our hypotheses.

Our paper makes two contributions to research on BLEs. First, it highlights the important role of indirect effects for learning outcomes in BLEs. As such, it provides an explanation for the inconsistent findings provided by the extant research, which may be conditional on cognitive mechanisms. Specifically, we build on and extend research by Klein et al. (2006) and Kintu et al. (2017) by analyzing the role of boundary conditions such as flow experience in explaining differences in learning outcomes in BLEs. Our results indicate that scholars in the field of BLE research might benefit from drawing from related disciplines, such as educational psychology, and from conducting research that goes beyond the direct effects of BLEs on learning outcomes in order to more comprehensively understand the cognitive mechanisms and boundary conditions behind knowledge construction. Second, our paper suggests that the design characteristics of BLEs directly and indirectly affect learning outcomes. Thus, our paper encourages researchers to take the heterogeneity into account that exists within BLEs and that drives knowledge construction in different ways (Hazari et al., 2013). Our paper also has implications for the practice of blended learning. Specifically, instructors should design BLEs in a way that promotes flow experiences as the positive effect of BLEs on learning outcomes is conditional on these experiences.

2. Literature review

2.1. Blended learning and its characteristics

Blended learning describes a combination of online and face-to-face instruction with reduced in-class seat time (Graham et al., 2013). It can be clearly distinguished from other formats within the spectrum of course-delivery formats, such as “traditional face-to-face learning,” which does not contain any online components; “technology-enhanced learning,” which does not reduce face time; “mostly online learning,” which entails solely supplemental or optional face-to-face contact; and “completely online learning,” which lacks face-to-face components (Graham et al., 2013). Although the balance between online and face-to-face activities is different for each course, blended learning seeks “to maximize the benefits of both face-to-face and online methods—using the web for what it does best, and using class time for what it does best” (Osguthorpe & Graham, 2003).

This definition leaves room for a range of different BLE designs. For example, in studies on blended learning, the reduction in faceto-face time in BLEs compared to traditional lectures ranges from 25 percent (i.e., three instead of four face-to-face hours per week) in studies by Cybinski and Selvanathan (2005) and Snowball (2014) to approximately 85 percent (i.e., four meetings per semester instead of two meetings per week) in studies by Chen and Jones (2007) and Jones and Chen (2008). Also, the type of assessment used in BLEs can differ. In many cases, learning outcomes of BLEs are solely assessed on the basis of final exams (Lim & Morris, 2009; Priluck, 2004; Wiechowski & Washburn, 2014). However, some BLE designs include a combination of exams and assignments (Olitsky & Cosgrove, 2014; Terry, 2007), case studies (Chen & Jones, 2007), group projects (Bryant et al., 2003; Klein et al., 2006), or mid-term tests (Cosgrove & Olitsky, 2015; Dowling et al., 2003; Snowball, 2014).

Despite this lack of standardization, BLEs generally share some common characteristics. Arbaugh (2000), for example, highlights technological and pedagogical characteristics, such as the perceived ease of use of the course software package, perceived flexibility of the online environment, and the interactivity of the learning environment, as central elements of a blended MBA course. Kintu et al. (2017) investigate technology quality, online tool design, and course interaction as important characteristics of BLEs. Chou and Chou (2011) conclude that while the online part of BLEs offers learners greater flexibility in the learning process, the face-to-face part tends to encourage personal interactions with peers and the instructor. As such, course flexibility and course interaction are generally viewed as the central characteristics of BLEs (Arbaugh, 2014; Riffell & Sibley, 2005).

Course flexibility refers to a pedagogical method that provides learners with the ability to study anytime and anywhere (Alavi & Gallupe, 2003). It enables learners to balance their studies of predefined content with personal commitments, such as work, family, and

Course interaction refers to communication and collaboration with other learners and the instructor. It has been identified as an important predictor of learning outcomes, especially at the graduate level (Arbaugh, 2014; Riffell & Sibley, 2005). Interaction en­ courages engagement with different views and opinions (Alavi, 1994). For example, Kintu et al. (2017) find that course interaction has positive effects on both intrinsic motivation and knowledge construction. Napier et al. (2011) and Riffell and Sibley (2005) point out that blended learners report more interactions as well as higher-quality interactions with their instructor and their peers than tradi­ tional learners. Interaction occurs in synchronous and asynchronous forms (Alavi & Gallupe, 2003). Thus, it is not limited to the face-to-face encounters of BLEs, as it can also be integrated into the online portions.

2.2. Blended learning and learning outcomes

Research on blended learning has been conducted in various disciplines. Most studies compare the learning outcomes of BLEs and traditional lectures, although some compare BLEs to purely online courses. Different taxonomies have been developed to differentiate among learning outcomes. For example, Kraiger et al. (1993) propose a taxonomy that distinguishes among cognitive, skill-based, and affective learning outcomes. Cognitive learning outcomes include verbal knowledge, knowledge organization, and cognitive strategies. While verbal knowledge refers to the acquisition and recognition of information, knowledge organization comprises the construction of new mental models from underlying information elements, and cognitive strategies are related to metacognitive skills, such as self-awareness or self-regulation. Cognitive learning outcomes are generally assessed through tests (i.e., recognition, recall, power, speed) and—particularly for cognitive strategies—via self-assessments of knowledge (Kraiger et al., 1993). Skill-based learning out­ comes refer to skill compilation and automaticity. While skill compilation addresses the composition and proceduralization of skills (e. g., speed, fluidity, error rates, discrimination), which are often assessed through behavioral observations, interviews, and testing, automaticity refers to automatic processing and tuning. It is examined through, for instance, secondary task performance and inter­ ference problems (Kraiger et al., 1993). Finally, affective learning outcomes include the attitudinal and motivational effects of learning efforts. While attitudinal outcomes refer to changes in the direction and strength of attitudes, motivational outcomes comprise such aspects as motivational disposition, self-efficacy, and goal setting. Affective learning outcomes are typically investigated using self-reported measures (Kraiger et al., 1993).

In more recent research, Sitzmann et al. (2008) differentiate between affective learning outcomes and cognitive learning outcomes, which also comprise skill-based outcomes. In addition, they analyze learners’ reactions—indicators that “capture the degree to which the training provided a satisfying, meaningful learning experience” (Sitzmann et al., 2008). Sitzmann et al. (2010) also hint at dif­ ferences between self-assessments of knowledge and cognitive learning outcomes measured using exam results.

Research shows that cognitive and affective learning outcomes as well as learners’ reactions and self-assessments of knowledge are distinct but related. Learners’ reactions, such as satisfaction, serve as predictors of affective learning outcomes and, to a lesser extent, of cognitive outcomes (Sitzmann et al., 2008). Cohen (1981) finds a strong relationship between cognitive learning outcomes and learners’ reactions. Klein et al. (2006) demonstrate that affective outcomes, such as learning goal orientation and motivation, are related to both cognitive learning outcomes and learners’ reactions. For self-assessments of knowledge, Sitzmann et al. (2010) report strong correlations with affective learning outcomes, especially the motivation to learn, and moderate correlations with cognitive learning outcomes. However, they find a strong relationship between self-assessments of knowledge and actual knowledge levels (i.e., cognitive learning outcomes) “when learners are given an opportunity to self-assess and receive feedback on their self-assessments” (Sitzmann et al., 2010), which is the case in both BLEs and traditional lectures. Thus, despite some weaknesses, self-assessments of knowledge might serve as a comprehensive measure of learning outcomes, especially in research on BLEs, which are associated with affective and cognitive learning outcomes.

2.3. Empirical research on blended learning and learning outcomes

Empirical research on the relationship between BLEs and learning outcomes is mostly grounded in a constructivist perspective on teaching and learning (Webb et al., 2005; Wiechowski & Washburn, 2014). The constructivist perspective stands in contrast to the objectivist perspective that has been the dominant perspective on learning in the past (Müller & Wulf, 2020). The objectivist per­ spectives is based on the assumption that an objective reality exists which can best be transferred to the learner through traditional lectures (Ertmer & Newby, 2013). The constructivist perspective, in contrast, proposes that better learning outcomes result if learners construct knowledge themselves, either individually or in collaboration with others, on the basis of active engagement with and sensemaking of existing information (Al-Huneidi & Schreurs, 2012). From a constructivist perspective, learners occupy a central role in the learning environment, while instructors become facilitators.

In line with its constructivist grounding, empirical research on BLEs has focused on comparing learning outcomes between blended courses and other instruction formats, such as traditional lectures or purely online courses. This research mainly examines cognitive learning outcomes, such as course grades (Alonso et al., 2011; Ashby et al., 2011; McKenzie et al., 2013) and self-assessments of learning (Lin, 2008; Moore & Gilmartin, 2010). Only a few studies investigate learners’ reactions, such as convenience (Owston et al., 2013) and learner satisfaction (McDonough et al., 2014; Utts et al., 2003), or affective learning outcomes, such as confidence (Chen & Jones, 2007; Priluck, 2004) or motivation (Kintu et al., 2017; Klein et al., 2006). As Table 1 shows, research on the learning outcomes of BLEs yields inconclusive results. One group of studies that compares BLEs with other formats of instruction does not find significant differences with regard to learning outcomes. For example, in an experiment involving undergraduate biology students, Bergtrom (2011) finds that BLEs do not affect cognitive learning outcomes (i.e., recall and retention performance). Similarly, in a blended undergraduate computing module with two instead of three meetings per week, Napier et al. (2011) find that course grades in traditional and blended courses are comparable, although blended learners report higher levels of interaction. Chen and Jones (2007) examine traditional lectures and blended learning in an MBA accounting course, and conclude that the students’ grades are comparable. However, in terms of learners’ reactions, the students in traditional lectures are more satisfied with the clarity of instruction, while the blended learners express greater appreciation of the learned concepts and experience an improvement in their analytical skills. Cosgrove and Olitsky (2015) compare traditional and blended formats in an undergraduate economics course, and do not find significant differences in cognitive learning outcomes, measured in terms of exam performance. Nevertheless, students in the traditional course retain the material better based on a comparison of pre-test and post-test results. Terry (2007) examines three instruction formats—traditional, blended, and online—and finds that cognitive learning outcomes measured on the basis of continuous assessments are similar for all three formats, while the purely online format leads to significantly worse results on the final exam. Wiechowski and Washburn (2014) find no differences in the effects of the three modes of instruction on exam performance, although blended (and online) courses lead to higher learner satisfaction.

A second group of studies that compares BLEs with other instruction formats finds blended learning to be significantly more effective than traditional lectures. For example, Baepler et al. (2014) show that cognitive learning outcomes (i.e., course grades) in a blended course in chemistry are at least as good as in traditional lectures or significantly better. In addition, they find that blended learning improves affective learning outcomes (i.e., confidence and enrichment). Similarly, Lovett et al. (2008) show that students in a blended statistics course with 16 instead of 60 meetings per term learn twice as fast as students in the traditional lecture format and perform equally well or better on cognitive assessments. Furthermore, Riffell and Sibley (2005) find that blended learners in a biology course with a two-thirds reduction in face-to-face time not only achieve higher cognitive performance than learners in a traditional course but they also report higher levels of interaction with content, instructor, and peers. McFarlin (2008) demonstrate that a transition from traditional lectures to a blended format enhances course grades by almost 10 percent among physiology students, presumably due to greater exposure to course content through online materials. Melton et al. (2009) find that both course grades and learner satisfaction are significantly higher in an undergraduate health course using a blended format. In contrast, Pereira et al. (2007) observe better test results in a BLE but no difference in learner satisfaction for anatomy students. Webb et al. (2005) examine different instruction formats in an MBA course on information systems. They find better assignment results for students in blended and online environments than for those in traditional formats. Dowling et al. (2003) investigate different course formats for undergraduate ac­ counting students, and conclude that midterm and final exam results are higher among students in a blended course. Deschacht and Goeman (2015) show that the use of a blended format for various management subjects leads not only to better exam performance but also to higher dropout rates. In a study of undergraduate students who demonstrate a higher degree of active learning practices in a blended business communication course, Sauers and Walker (2004) find that blended learners achieve significantly higher test results than students in traditional lectures.

A third group of studies that compares BLEs with other formats of instruction finds that BLE alternatives yield better learning outcomes. For example, Cybinski and Selvanathan (2005) show that blended learners in a statistics course experience more assessment anxiety and, consequently, less enjoyment, which also has a negative effect on their cognitive learning performance. Reasons et al. (2005) study cognitive learning outcomes, and show that psychology and healthcare students in internet-based courses outperform those in both traditional and blended courses, while they find no difference in results between traditional and blended courses. Bryant et al. (2003) show that the final grades of undergraduate management students taking a course in information systems are higher in traditional lectures. Although Priluck (2004) does not find significant differences in exam performance, her marketing students are more satisfied with the traditional course and prefer it to a blended format. They also believe that traditional lectures are more effective for developing skills and course knowledge.

These inconsistent findings suggest that differences in the effects of BLEs and other instruction formats on learning outcomes might be more nuanced than conceptualized in prior research. In particular, these findings give reason to believe that a constructivist approach to teaching and learning might only be advantageous to an objectivist approach if specific boundary conditions are met. Thus, a more fine-grained approach to understanding differences in learning outcomes seems appropriate. Specifically, research in the fields of management education and educational psychology indicates that these inconsistent findings might be addressed by carefully analyzing specific design characteristics of BLEs and by considering indirect effects of psychological characteristics as boundary conditions.

Course flexibility and course interaction have been identified as two central characteristics of BLEs that “may be distinguishing advantages of blended learning in management education” (Arbaugh, 2014). Initial research indicates that these characteristics positively affect learning outcomes (Kintu et al., 2017). In addition, educational psychology research has shown that the cognitive characteristics of learners might play an important role in explaining differences in learning outcomes (Mayer, 2014; Moreno & Mayer, 2007). The concept of flow might be particularly important in this regard. Recent research indicates that flow experiences might be helpful in explaining and improving learning outcomes (Bressler & Bodzin, 2013; Csikszentmihalyi, 1975).

Research has identified focused concentration, perceived control, time distortion, and enjoyment as important characteristics of flow experiences. These characteristics have, for example, been observed during foreign language acquisition (Egbert, 2004), when playing educational games (Kiili, 2005), and when using computer software for learning (Ghani, 1995). Flow experiences have been associated with positive effects in the learning context. Flow is generally characterized as a form of intrinsic motivation to learn (Liao, 2006), which makes the respective learning activity internally rewarding (Ro et al., 2018). Thus, it is not surprising that flow has been linked to affective learning outcomes, such as a higher intention to learn and explore (Raphael et al., 2012) and increased excitement about learning (Admiraal et al., 2011). Research also highlights effects of flow experiences on cognitive learning outcomes, such as an increase in in-depth reflective processes (Hou, 2015), better performance on quizzes, and more positive subjective assessments of learning performance (Ro et al., 2018). Thus, flow experience might be an important boundary condition for positive effects of BLEs on learning outcomes. Specifically, the integration of flow experiences as a mediator into research on the effects of BLEs might help advance research regarding learning outcomes in these environments.

3. Hypotheses development

3.1. Course flexibility and interaction as antecedents of learning outcomes

In view of the inconsistent findings in the extant research regarding the learning outcomes of BLEs, scholars in the fields of management education and educational psychology suggest analyzing learning outcomes on the basis of BLEs’ distinguishing char­ acteristics rather than by simply comparing different learning formats (Arbaugh et al., 2009). Course flexibility and interaction are generally regarded as distinguishing characteristics of blended learning (Chou & Chou, 2011). As Arbaugh (2014) indicates, research on BLEs associates flexibility and interaction with improved learning outcomes. For example, Kintu et al. (2017) find that interaction supports knowledge construction—one aspect of cognitive learning—and fosters intrinsic motivation among learners, an indicator of affective learning. Similarly, Al-Huneidi and Schreurs (2012) argue that flexible online phases foster cognitive learning outcomes by supporting individual knowledge construction, while interactive class sessions mainly enhance social knowledge construction. Thus, as baseline hypotheses, we posit:

H1a. Course flexibility has a positive effect on learning outcomes.

H1b. Course interaction has a positive effect on learning outcomes.

3.2. Flow experience as an antecedent of learning outcomes

Flow is associated with better affective and cognitive learning outcomes (Choi et al., 2007; Reychav & Wu, 2015). Learners who experience more flow report an increased sense of discovery, flashes of intensity and, eventually, a desire to improve performance (Bressler & Bodzin, 2013). These experiences lead to an increase in exploratory behavior, which triggers cognitive problem-solving activities (Finneran & Zhang, 2005). These activities are, in turn, beneficial for the depth of information processing (Reychav & Wu, 2015) and have been associated with better performance on quizzes and more positive subjective assessments of learning per­ formance (Ro et al., 2018), which determine learning outcomes. Based on these findings, we assume a positive effect of a learner’s flow experience on cognitive learning outcomes. Hence, we posit:

H2. Flow experience has a positive effect on learning outcomes.

3.3. Flow experience as a mediator of the effects of course flexibility and interaction

Research indicates that course flexibility and interaction—the distinguishing characteristics of BLEs—are associated with flow experience (Arbaugh, 2014; Bressler & Bodzin, 2013; Osguthorpe & Graham, 2003). Course flexibility is mainly related to the technology-supported distance phases that are typical of BLEs. The timeand place-independent character of the distance phases has been found to foster more intensive learning processes over a longer period of time and to allow for periodic pauses for reflection (Arbaugh, 2000). This not only enhances conceptual thinking, but also allows learners to better deal with conflicts between learning requirements and other personal commitments (Garrison & Kanuka, 2004). The resulting higher level of concentration, sense of control, and enjoyment are associated with higher levels of flow (Guo & Ro, 2008).

Based on these arguments, we expect a positive effect of both course flexibility and course interaction on flow experience. Thus, we posit:

H3a. Course flexibility has a positive effect on flow experience.

H3b. Course interaction has a positive effect on flow experience.

Synthesizing our prior arguments, we assume that flow experience mediates the relationship between the two central character­ istics of a BLE—flexibility and interaction—and learning outcomes. Hence, we posit:

H4a. Flow experience mediates the effect of course flexibility on learning outcomes.

H4b. Flow experience mediates the effect of course interaction on learning outcomes.

4. Methodology

4.1. Design and procedure

To test our hypotheses, we collected survey data from 115 graduate-level management students studying at a German university. Following established procedures in blended learning research, we conducted a quasi-experiment with different learning environments (Asarta & Schmidt, 2017). Students had the opportunity to choose between two strategy courses that were highly similar in terms of content and difficulty but taught on different weekdays. Student selection bias was not an issue, as the registration materials that were available prior to the courses did not contain any information about the instruction format (Asarta & Schmidt, 2020) and no com­ parable experiment had been conducted in the past. Both courses were taught by the same instructor at the same time of day to avoid any bias caused by having different instructors and to ensure that difficulties caused by mixing data from different instructors were not a concern (Asarta & Schmidt, 2013). We delivered one course using traditional lectures with four lecture hours per week. For the other course, we used a blended format in which we reduced the face time to two lecture hours per week (Asarta & Schmidt, 2015) and the group size was cut in half.

Both courses were guided by the objectives of conveying knowledge on strategic management and of constructing knowledge by applying the acquired knowledge to case examples and discussing solutions. While the traditional lectures attempted to achieve these objectives solely in the classroom, the blended course used online activities to facilitate knowledge acquisition and face time for knowledge transfer and discussion, i.e. knowledge construction.

In each week’s traditional lecture, the instructor explained different theories and concepts of strategic management in the class­ room that build on literature that the students had been asked to read before class. The theories and concepts were illustrated using case examples or short exercises in order to familiarize participants with the application of these theories and concepts. The lecture ended with a brief oral quiz to assess if learners had understood the lecture content.

The blended course, in contrast, included in each week a flexible distance phase designed to enable learners to individually acquire and construct knowledge at their own preferred time and pace as well as an interactive classroom session in which learners could construct and apply new knowledge together. As such, the course design facilitated two central characteristics of BLEs: flexibility and interaction. For the distance phases, we provided learners with vidcasts (i.e., short videos that introduce the same theories and concepts as in the traditional lecture), related articles, and videos to deepen their understanding. We also provided them with textbook and academic references for supplementary reading. In addition, a chat function for synchronous online interaction as well as a messenger function and a discussion forum for asynchronous online interaction were available to learners. Furthermore, we granted learners access to a weekly online quiz to allow them to self-assess their understanding of the materials. The classroom sessions included brief repetitions of the core concepts followed by case studies solved in small groups and subsequent discussions in plenary, in line with Asarta and Schmidt (2017). Overall, the traditional lecture and the blended course did not differ with regard to the contents, but they differed regarding the degree of flexibility of knowledge acquisition before (and in) class as well as the intensity of interaction in class.

At the end of the course, we asked all participants to complete a questionnaire covering their learning experiences. Based on Podsakoff et al. (2003), we attempted to minimize common method bias by testing our questionnaire beforehand and incorporating feedback from multiple rounds of testing.

4.2. Measures

The measures for our variables were adapted from peer-reviewed articles and are outlined below. In line with previous studies on technology-supported learning (McGill & Klobas, 2009; Solimeno et al., 2008) and flow experience (Finneran & Zhang, 2005), we relied on the original items and scales for all of our constructs to reduce potential biases in terms of consistency motifs or common scale anchors (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Methodological research has shown that varying the number of response alternatives in rating scales does not affect the validity of the results (Maydeu-Olivares et al., 2009). 4.2.1. Dependent variable We used perceived learning—the participant’s self-assessment of knowledge—as a measure of learning outcomes (Alavi, 1994). Perceived learning is a commonly used measure in blended learning research (Lim & Morris, 2009; Sauers & Walker, 2004). Sitzmann et al. (2010) highlight that almost one third of the 166 studies included in their meta-analysis use self-assessments of knowledge as a measure of learning outcomes. They find that such self-assessments show stronger relationships with affective learning outcomes, especially motivation, than with cognitive learning outcomes in general. Nevertheless, Sitzmann et al. (2010) show that when the learning environment of BLEs or traditional lectures supports feedback between learners and instructors, self-assessments of knowl­ edge are strongly related to actual knowledge levels (i.e., cognitive learning outcomes). Thus, in research on BLEs, it might be advisable to rely on self-assessments of knowledge, such as perceived learning, as these assessments comprehensively measure learning outcomes, and cover both affective and cognitive aspects. This also reflects the learning objectives of the courses in our experiment. As such, in line with Alavi (1994), we measured perceived learning using three items on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree). We calculated perceived learning as the mean of those three items. The Cronbach’s alpha (α) of 0.77 suggests that the construct is sufficiently reliable (Cronbach, 1951; Tavakol & Dennick, 2011). The detailed items and scales can be found in Appendix A.

4.2.2. Independent variables We measured course flexibility, an important element of course design and a central characteristic of BLEs, using six items developed by Arbaugh (2000). We utilized a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree) and calculated course flexibility as the mean of the six items (α = 0.71).

To measure course interaction, a second important element of course design and another central characteristic of BLEs, we used five items and a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree) following Volery and Lord (2000) and Eom et al. (2006). We calculated course interaction as the mean of the five items (α = 0.71). The detailed items and scales for the independent variables can be found in Appendix A.

In our robustness tests, we added instructional condition as an alternative independent variable to test whether the delivery format matters. To measure instructional condition, we created a dummy variable that took a value of 1 for the blended learning format and 0 for the traditional lecture format (Dowling et al., 2003).

4.2.3. Mediator variable In line with Webster et al. (1993), we measured flow experience using twelve items on a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree). Four items were reversely coded to eliminate common rater effects (Podsakoff et al., 2003). We calculated flow experience as the mean of the twelve items (α = 0.80). The detailed items and scales can be found in Appendix A.

4.2.4. Control variables In line with prior research, we included several control variables that might influence the relationships between flexibility or interaction and perceived learning. We used a binary variable (1 = female) to measure gender, as gender has been shown to affect learning outcomes in blended courses (Dowling et al., 2003). For the same reason, we asked respondents to indicate their age in years (Hwang & Arbaugh, 2006; Lim & Morris, 2009) and their level of prior knowledge (Asarta & Schmidt, 2017). We asked participants to indicate their level of prior knowledge of the course subject on a five-point Likert scale (1 = none).

In addition to these demographic variables, we controlled for the intrinsic motivation of the learners, as intrinsic motivation might trigger greater involvement and affect learning outcomes (Klein et al., 2006; L´opez-P´erez et al., 2011). We used four items and a seven-point Likert scale (1 = corresponds not at all) adopted from Guay et al. (2000) to measure intrinsic motivation (α = 0.76). Furthermore, we controlled for self-management of learning, which has been shown to moderate learning outcomes, using four items and a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; α = 0.85) (Huang, 2014). In addition, we controlled for resistance to change using four items and a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; α = 0.71). Huang (2014) shows that resistance to a change from the established learning mode can demotivate learners. Finally, we controlled for intrinsic cognitive load—the cognitive burden stemming from the perceived complexity of information that has to be processed by a learner when confronted with the learning material—to control for the comparability of the two courses (Chandler & Sweller, 1991; Sweller, 2005). In line with Woo (2014), we asked the participants “How difficult was the learning content for you?” Learners responded using a nine-point Likert scale (1 = extremely easy). The detailed items and scales for the control variables can be found in Appendix A.

In Models 3 to 5, we controlled for the three mediation conditions defined by Baron and Kenny (1986). In Model 3, we test whether variations in the presumed mediator significantly account for variations in the dependent variable. This condition is reflected in H2, which proposes a positive effect of flow experience on perceived learning. Model 3 provides support for this hypothesis (B = 0.40, p < .001). Flow experience explains an additional 15 percent of the variance in perceived learning.

In Model 4, we test whether variations in the independent variables significantly predict variations in the presumed mediator. This condition is reflected in H3a and H3b. H3a proposes that course flexibility has a positive effect on flow experience. Model 4a supports this hypothesis (B = 0.26, p < .001). In H3b, we argue that course interaction has a positive effect on flow experience. Model 4b reveals a significant and positive effect (B = 0.31, p < .001).

In Model 5, we test whether the direct relationships between the independent variables—course flexibility and course inter­ action—and perceived learning hold when controlling for the presumed mediator. This condition is reflected in H4a and H4b. H4a proposes that the effect of course flexibility on perceived learning is mediated by flow experience. Model 5a offers support for this hypothesis. It reveals that perceived learning remains significantly and positively related to flow experience (B = 0.35, p < .001), while the initial relationship between course flexibility and perceived learning is no longer significant (B = .09, p > .1). Similarly, in H4b, we posit that the effect of course interaction on perceived learning is mediated by flow experience. Model 5b supports this hypothesis. It indicates that perceived learning remains significantly and positively related to flow experience (B = 0.37, p < .001), while the relationship between course interaction and perceived learning is no longer significant (B = .09, p > .1). In addition, Model 5c includes both independent variables as well as the presumed mediator of flow experience. The model shows that course flexibility (B = 0.08, p > .05) and course interaction (B = 0.07, p > .05) are no longer significantly related to perceived learning, while flow experience has a significant and positive effect (B = 0.33, p < .01). The mediation effect explains approximately half of the total effect of course flexibility and course interaction on perceived learning. Thus, we find consistent evidence that flow experience mediates the positive relationships between course flexibility and perceived learning, and between course interaction and perceived learning. The results of a bootstrapping analysis based on Preacher and Hayes (2004) with 5000 samples and a 95 percent confidence interval support our results.

The results translate into the following effect sizes: When course flexibility and course interaction both increase by one standard deviation, perceived learning increases by 5.1 percent (i.e., from 4.16 to 4.37). If additionally flow experience increases by one standard deviation, perceived learning increases by 7.8 percent (i.e., from 4.16 to 4.48). Subsequent power analyses using G*Power reveal a high statistical power of 0.99 for the full mediation model.

5.2. Robustness tests

We ran a series of tests to ensure the robustness of our results with respect to alternative variable specifications and alternative approaches to data analysis. First, to dispel potential concerns regarding common method bias and reverse causality (Podsakoff et al., 2003), we re-ran our analyses using the learners’ allocations to the different instructional conditions (i.e., traditional lectures versus BLE) as an alternative independent variable. Specifically, we created a dummy variable that took a value of 1 for the blended learning format and 0 for the traditional lecture format (Dowling et al., 2003). The respective regression analyses show that the blended learning format is significantly and positively related to perceived learning (B = 0.19, p < .05) as well as to flow experience (B = 0.55, p < .001), and that the effect of the blended learning format on perceived learning is fully mediated by flow experience (B = 0.42, p < .001). Thus, our results remain unchanged for instructional condition instead of course flexibility and course interaction as the in­ dependent variable.

Second, as a further test of whether the effect of flow experience differs between instructional conditions, we estimated separate regression models for the sub-samples of the 64 students in the blended course and the 51 students in the traditional lecture. These regression models included our control variables as well as flow experience as the independent variable. Regression results showed a highly significant effect of flow experience on perceived learning in the sub-sample of blended learning participants (B = 0.49, p < .001), while the effect of flow experience on perceived learning remained insignificant in the sub-sample of traditional lecture par­ ticipants (B = 0.32, p > .05).

Third, to address concerns regarding a lack of discriminant validity between our variables, we calculated heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) ratios for those variables that were relatively highly correlated (Henseler et al., 2015). Specifically, we report HTMT ratios for our variables perceived learning, flow experience, course flexibility, course interaction, self-management, intrinsic motivation, and resistance to change in Appendix B. All HTMT values are well below the threshold of 0.85 (Henseler et al., 2015). Thus, discriminant validity can be regarded as established.

Fourth, in order to ensure that our results are not biased by control variables which were only measured after the treatment even though they have more of an antecedent character, we re-ran our analyses without the control variables prior knowledge and intrinsic motivation. Our results remain unchanged.

Sixth, to reduce concerns regarding the use of different rating scales (i.e., five-point, seven-point, and nine-point Likert scales), we transformed all scales to five-point scales and, in another test, to seven-point scales. We then re-ran our analyses. The results remained the same.

Finally, in order to test the robustness of our results with regard to alternative approaches to data analysis and hypotheses testing, we used structural equation modeling to simultaneously test our hypotheses. Specifically, we fitted a structural equation model with course flexibility and course interaction as antecedents of flow experience, which, in turn, serves as an antecedent of perceived learning. This model as well as the parameter estimates are shown in Appendix C. An insignificant chi-squared test, a CFI value of 0.972, a TLI value of 0.931, and an SRMR value of 0.05 indicate good model fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999; Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). Thus, this alternative approach to data analysis provides additional evidence that flow experience mediates the relationships between course flexibility and perceived learning as well as between course interaction and perceived learning. Overall, our results appear robust to alternative specifications, analyses, and variable choices.

6. Discussion

BLEs are an increasingly common discussion topic in the field of education across disciplines (Arbaugh, 2014). Thus far, researchers have examined the direct effects of BLEs and certain BLE characteristics on learning outcomes. Overall, the empirical evidence is inconsistent (Bryant et al., 2003; Priluck, 2004; Wiechowski & Washburn, 2014). As such, the conditions under which BLEs improve learning outcomes remain uncertain.

Our study draws from educational psychology research to propose that a learner’s flow experience, as defined by Csikszentmihalyi (1975), mediates the effects of BLEs on learning outcomes. Specifically, we provide theoretical arguments and empirical evidence that course interaction and course flexibility—two central characteristics of BLEs—foster learning outcomes if they positively affect flow experience. As such, we introduce flow experience as a boundary condition that might at least partly explain the inconsistent findings regarding the effect of BLEs on learning outcomes.

In contrast to the extant research on blended learning, our paper goes beyond solely examining the direct effects of BLEs and certain BLE characteristics on learning outcomes. Rather, our paper takes a step towards opening the black box behind active knowledge construction in BLEs by investigating its mechanisms as well as its boundary conditions from a constructivist perspective. As such, our paper builds on and extends the research by Klein et al. (2006) and Kintu et al. (2017), who account for the effects of motivation in blended learning. Furthermore, we add to the research undertaken by Lim and Morris (2009) and Asarta and Schmidt (2020), who consider the effects of prior blended learning experience on blended learning outcomes, as well as to research by scholars such as Cosgrove and Olitsky (2015) and Asarta and Schmidt (2017), who account for the influence of students’ prior academic performance on learning outcomes in BLEs. Consequently, our paper has several important implications for blended learning research and practice.

6.1. Theoretical implications

Our paper suggests two important implications for blended learning research. First, our paper indicates that future research should put more effort into understanding the boundary conditions under which active knowledge construction by learners—based on a constructivist perspective—leads to better learning outcomes compared to traditional knowledge transfer, which is grounded in an objectivist perspective. To identify these boundary conditions, research might draw on related disciplines, such as educational psy­ chology, and investigate cognitive mechanisms that foster active knowledge construction and improve learning outcomes. These ef­ forts may include additional research on the antecedents and effects of flow experience in a BLE, which can build on Finneran and Zhang (2005) and Chen (2006). In addition future studies could also examine the role of other cognitive and affective characteristics, such as motivation, metacognition, or emotions, as boundary conditions of the positive effect of active knowledge construction in BLEs on learning outcomes. Moreno and Mayer (2007) have already considered motivation and metacognition in interactive multimedia environments, and Plass et al. (2014) and Knoerzer et al. (2016) have begun to examine the role of emotions in the design of materials for online learning phases.

The second implication relates to the design of empirical studies on BLEs. Thus far, this stream of research has mainly focused on comparing traditional instruction formats with blended formats (Terry, 2007; Wiechowski & Washburn, 2014). Our study suggests that the positive effect of BLEs on learning outcomes depends at least partly on its design characteristics, particularly interaction and flexibility. Hence, future research should compare different BLE designs and their antecedents as predictors of learning outcomes. Different BLE designs, e.g., regarding interaction and flexibility, might have different effects on learning outcomes, but also on boundary conditions such as flow experience. For example, Hazari et al. (2013) propose that blogs can foster deeper engagement in the distance phases of blended management learning. Furthermore, future research should examine antecedents of BLE characteristics such as how instructors contribute to flexibility and interaction in blended management learning environments. Such research could lead to stronger standardization of BLE designs.

6.2. Practical implications

Our study also has two important practical implications. First, instructors should design BLEs in a way that promotes both flexibility

Second, instructors should account for learners’ cognitive characteristics, such as flow experience which constitute important boundary conditions for effective knowledge construction by the learners. Chen (2006) and Choi et al. (2007) indicate that these antecedents include not only interaction-related aspects such as direct and immediate feedback, but also clear and attainable goals and skills relevant for given challenges. As such, instructors should set course objectives that are not only attainable and, thereby, moti­ vational but also transparent to the learners. Furthermore, instructors should enable direct and immediate feedback in the online phases and in the classroom. While the level of feedback in the classroom is naturally high, online feedback can also be direct and immediate when, for example, triggered by self-assessments with predefined solutions. Moreover, to ensure that learners’ skills are sufficient to meet the challenges of a blended course, instructors should conduct an initial assessment at the beginning of the course and provide supplementary learning materials and guidance for students with identified skills gaps. Overall, instructors need to carefully consider the effects of their BLE designs on the learners’ cognitive characteristics.

6.3. Limitations

As with all research, our study suffers from several limitations, all of which highlight opportunities for future research. First, our empirical sample is limited to 115 graduate management students from a German university and the sample is characterized by little cultural diversity. Although our results might not be generalizable to other disciplines, study levels, or cultural backgrounds, we deliberately examined two strategy courses that can be considered representative of any graduate business or management program. In addition, we chose graduate students because we assumed that they made a more conscious choice to engage in this line of study than undergraduates. Furthermore, we used a sample size that is similar to those used in other studies (Chen & Jones, 2007). Nevertheless, future research should examine potential effects of cultural diversity on flow experience in different learning environments, as business classes are becoming increasingly international. Such research might, for example, build on Andrade (2006) and Lebcir et al. (2008), who examine adjustment challenges and experiences among international students.

Second, following Alavi (1994), we used “perceived learning” as our dependent variable. This variable is commonly used to assess learning outcomes in studies on BLEs (Lim & Morris, 2009; Sauers & Walker, 2004), and serves as a comprehensive measure of learning outcomes because it is related to both affective and cognitive learning outcomes. Moreover, in our robustness tests, we confirmed our results on the basis of a measure of actual learning. Nevertheless, perceived learning may not fully reflect what business school students in general and strategy students in particular should learn. Therefore, we suggest that future research on the learning outcomes of BLEs build on more comprehensive measures of learning that, for instance, also comprise skill-based learning outcomes (Kraiger et al., 1993). Ideally, such research should even use alternative measures of learning outcomes to account for the multidimensionality of learning.

Third, our study uses a cross-sectional research design, which might raise concerns regarding causalities within our results and which might bias results when antecedents are only measured after the treatment. Although previous blended learning research has used cross-sectional data (Jones & Chen, 2008), we also conducted robustness tests with alternative specifications of the dependent and independent variables and with less variables, which confirmed our results and reduced the risk of reverse causality. However, we suggest that future research should examine data from several points in time (e.g., pre-tests, post-tests, experience surveys, and learning analytics gathered from learning management systems).

Fourth, the blended format may have caused a novelty effect, especially as students attended traditional lectures in other courses in parallel. To minimize this novelty effect, we started our course with an extended introductory session, provided clear instructions and guidance, and conducted our quasi-experiment over a period of several weeks. Nevertheless, future research might benefit from the increasing normality of BLEs. In this regard, researchers can use blended environments across multiple courses at the same time in order to reduce novelty.

7. Conclusion

Many scholars and instructors believe that blended learning is the future of higher education. While some studies provide evidence that BLEs improve learning, others find no effect of BLEs or even a learner preference for traditional lectures. Thus, the conditions under which BLEs contribute to better outcomes remain unclear. We suggest that psychological characteristics play an important role in learning, and we show that flow experience mediates the effects of BLEs’ central characteristics—course flexibility and course interaction—on perceived learning. Our results draw attention to the need to consider the mechanisms as well as the boundary conditions behind active knowledge construction by the learners in BLEs. This will advance BLEs in research and practice in man­ agement education and beyond.

Measured using a seven-point Likert scale ranging from “1 = strongly disagree” to “7 = strongly agree.”

How difficult was the learning content for you? Measured using a nine-point Likert scale ranging from “1 = extremely easy” to “9 = extremely difficult.”
