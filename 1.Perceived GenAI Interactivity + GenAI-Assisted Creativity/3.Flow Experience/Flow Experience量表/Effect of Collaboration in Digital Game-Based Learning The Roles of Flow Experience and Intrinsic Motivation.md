ABSTRACT Background: Attention has been paid to collaboration in digital game-­based learning, as it fosters interaction and student engagement, thereby enhancing learning outcomes. Previous findings about the effect of collaboration in digital game-­based learning were inconsistent, and few studies have explored the underlying mechanisms, particularly proximal psychological processes such as motivation or emotion. Aims: This study aimed to provide evidence about the effectiveness of collaboration in digital game-­based learning and figure out the mediating roles of flow experience and intrinsic motivation in the relationship between collaboration and learning achievement. Sample: A total of 181 Chinese university students (40 males and 141 females) were recruited in our experiment. Method: 92 participants in the collaborative condition were required to be fully involved in face-­to-­face teamwork to solve the tasks in an educational game, while 89 participants in the control group completed the same game tasks individually. A multiple mediation model was applied to analyse the mediating role of flow experience and intrinsic motivation between the relationship of collaboration and learning achievement. Results: Results revealed that students in the collaborative group performed better on learning achievement than those who were in the individual group. Furthermore, multiple mediation analysis revealed that two main indirect pathways emerged: ‘collaboration → intrinsic motivation → learning achievement’ and ‘collaboration → flow experience → intrinsic motivation → learning achievement’. Conclusions: The findings supported the cognitive-­affective theory of learning with media and provided evidence about the important role of flow experience and intrinsic motivation.

2   |   Literature Review

2.1   |   Collaboration in Digital Game-­Based Learning

Collaboration was described as ‘a coordinated, synchronous activity that is the result of a continued attempt to construct and maintain a shared conception of a problem’ (Roschelle and Teasley 1995). Collaborative learning, grounded in the sociocultural perspective, refers to a situation in which two or more people learn or attempt to learn something together (Dillenbourg  1999), which emphasises interaction, knowledge construction, and reflection as key elements in the learning process (Feltovich et al. 1996). According to Vygotsky's (1978) social constructivism, human learning is a collaborative process that facilitates the transition from externalisation to internalisation and eventually affects cognitive development. From a social constructivist perspective, teammates could mediate each other's learning through effective communication, such as asking questions and conveying explanations (King 1990). In collaborative learning, students tend to engage in reflection, foster cognitive function (Gillies 2019), and eventually promote the development of cognitive abilities (Sins et al. 2008; Sills et al. 2016).

In DGBL, collaborative learning is a way to assist players in successfully completing game tasks through interaction among students (Ge and Land 2004). As for the effectiveness of collaboration on learning achievement in DGBL, previous empirical studies have shown inconsistent results. On the one hand, some studies have indicated that collaboration could improve students' learning achievement in DGBL (e.g., Chen and Law 2015; Chen and Hwang 2017; Sung and Hwang 2013). For example, Sung and Hwang (2013) found that a collaborative educational game with repertory grids led to better learning achievement than the individual version. Cheng et al.'s (2019) findings also indicated that repeated communication, negotiation, and off-­ topic chatting under collaborative conditions in DGBL could

Furthermore, many previous studies suggested that motivational or emotional factors would lead to differences in the cognitive processing of learning materials, which in turn lead to differences in learning results (Leutner  2014). Variables such as learning motivation (Liu et al. 2025), situational interest (Wong et al. 2024), positive emotion (Acosta-­Gonzaga and Ramirez-­Arellano 2021), confusion (D'Mello et al. 2014), flow experience (Müller and Wulf 2022) and attitudes toward learning media (Fan and Zhang 2024) have been shown to potentially play a mediating role in the learning process. Nevertheless, in DGBL, the mechanisms that account for the effect of collaboration on learning achievement have not received enough research attention.

2.2   |   Flow Experience as Mediator

According to Csikszentmihalyi (1990), flow experience refers to a state when learners can be absorbed in tasks, excel in their limited abilities, and reach better performances. In collaborative learning activities, individuals are required to be mutually involved in the shared activities and must coordinate their efforts to solve problems together (Hamalainen 2008). During the interactions in collaboration, students might have attempted to convey their emotions to their teammates and made a conscious effort to become qualified participants in their team so that the problem-­solving process would flow more smoothly (Brown and Cairns 2004; Wismath and Orr 2015). Furthermore, the more students are involved in an activity, the more immersed they will be in it, resulting in a state of flow.

Immersive characters are inherent in games (Groff  2018; Lee et al. 2016), which is a crucial factor in inducing flow experiences for college students engaging in computer game activities (Colombo et al. 2023). Some studies were conducted to investigate the effect of collaboration on flow experience in DGBL. For example, Plass et  al.  (2013) conducted an experimental study to differentiate learning among individual, competitive, and collaborative conditions in mathematics DGBL, and they found that participants who played cooperatively performed significantly better than those who played individually in game enjoyment, situational interest, re-­engagement intention, and recommendation intention. Zou et al. (2021) also provided evidence that collaborative conditions were superior to competitive and solo conditions in enhancing learning performance and flow experience. In addition, Garcia and Jung (2021) also found that the key features of immersion observed in digital games also appeared in online collaborative activities.

Further, flow experience could enable learners to ignore irrelevant cognitive processes and concentrate on perceiving and understanding relevant materials, thus leading to excellent learning performance (Chang et al. 2017). When learners reach a state of high flow, they would be more involved in learning activities and thus gain better learning outcomes (Sun et al. 2017). From a behavioural perspective, Hou (2015) found that learners with higher levels of flow experience demonstrated more reflective learning behaviours. This deeper engagement in reflection may serve as a pathway to improved academic achievement, as supported by previous studies (Lew and Schmidt 2011; Müller and Wulf 2022).

Thus, previous studies suggest that collaboration could promote flow experience, which in turn improves learning performance in DGBL. However, we are aware of few studies that have directly examined the mediating effect of flow experience in the relationship between collaboration and learning achievement in the game-­based learning environment. This study aims to address this issue by assuming flow experience as a mediator between collaborative learning and learning achievement in DGBL.

2.3   |   Intrinsic Motivation as Mediator

In the context of educational games, the concept of intrinsic motivation is essential (Mayer 2019). Intrinsic motivation is characterised by engaging in an activity for its inherent satisfaction rather than for external rewards or pressures. When individuals are intrinsically motivated, they are driven by the pleasure or challenge the activity provides, rather than by external stimuli, pressures, or rewards (Ryan and Deci 2000). In DGBL, intrinsic motivation has been widely recognised as a key factor that fosters engagement and persistence in learning (e.g., Chen and Huang 2024; Xiang et al. 2025; Zhou 2024).

Previous studies have shown that collaborative learning has a significant impact on students' intrinsic motivation in educational games (e.g., Chen  2018; Chon et  al.  2024; Fonseca et al. 2023; Liao et al. 2019). Liao et al. (2019) suggested that discussion with teammates could enable students to manage their cognitive resources more effectively, thereby improving learning motivation. Similarly, Chen (2018) reported that collaboration reduces learning pressure and fosters a sense of meaningful engagement, which further boosts students' intrinsic motivation. Furthermore, a systematic review by Fonseca et al. (2023) suggested that gamification and game-­based learning as cooperative learning tools can favour learners' motivation, whether in virtual or face-­to-­face mode. These findings suggest that collaborative learning environments provide social and cognitive support, which helps sustain motivation in game-­based learning contexts.

Intrinsic motivation, in turn, has been shown to lead to satisfaction and pleasure in the learning process, thus improving the skills or knowledge of learners (Elliot and Harackiewicz 1996). Numerous studies have shown that intrinsic motivation leads to more interest, excitement, and confidence, which in turn contribute to enhanced performance, persistence, creativity, vitality, self-­esteem, and general well-­being (Ryan and Deci 2000). In addition, compared with students driven by external motivation, students with intrinsic motivation are more likely to persist in the face of learning difficulties (Herlambang et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2024). In DGBL, intrinsic motivation has been recognised as an essential predictor in improving learning achievement (e.g., Baek et al. 2015; Chen and Tu 2021; Hong et al. 2017). For example, Chen and Tu (2021) pointed out that there is a positive relationship between motivation and learning performance in a DGBL environment; that is, the higher the motivation level, the better the learning performance of students. Hong et al. (2017) also found that intrinsic motivation was significantly positively correlated with learning performance in educational games.

2.4   |   Flow Experience and Intrinsic Motivation as Serial Mediators

Given that theories and empirical studies have suggested that flow experience and intrinsic motivation are both mediators in the relationship between collaboration and learning performance, they may serve as mediators in a parallel way. On the other hand, they may also mediate the relationship serially. The close relationship between intrinsic motivation and flow experience has been noted (Ryan and Deci 2000). According to Csikszentmihalyi  (1975, 1990), intrinsic motivation (autotelic) toward an activity is considered a concomitant of the flow experience. When people reach a state of flow, they act to achieve high inherent satisfaction (Csikszentmihalyi 1990), which may be one of the consequences of time perception distortion (e.g., Im and Varma 2018; Sackett et al. 2010). Time perception distortion is considered a dimension of the flow experience (Hancock et al. 2019). Thus, intrinsic motivation may be an outcome of flow experience (e.g., Weibel and Wissmath 2011; Nah et al. 2014). In DGBL, previous studies have shown that flow experience is positively correlated with intrinsic motivation (Hong et al. 2017; Khang et al. 2013). Also, some studies have demonstrated that flow experience is an important factor in maintaining motivation in DGBL and that flow experience can significantly predict the level of learning motivation of students (Hsieh et al. 2016; Yang and Quadir 2018).

Grounded in these findings, we propose that collaboration influences learning achievement through a sequential mediation pathway, where flow experience first enhances intrinsic motivation, which subsequently drives better learning outcomes. Specifically, collaboration may enhance the learning experience by fostering flow, a state of deep engagement characterised by optimal challenge, immersion, and intrinsic enjoyment (Csikszentmihalyi 1990). When learners experience flow, they are more likely to develop intrinsic motivation, as engaging tasks reinforce their sense of autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Ryan and Deci 2000). In turn, heightened intrinsic motivation sustains effort and deepens learning, ultimately improving academic achievement (Baek et al. 2015; Chen and Tu 2021).

2.6   |   The Present Study

Since the effect of collaboration on learning achievement in DGBL has remained a topic of continued debate among scholars according to the above inconsistent findings, this study aims to enhance understanding of the effects of collaboration in improving learning achievement in DGBL. While previous studies have highlighted the importance of motivational and emotional factors like motivation and flow experience in shaping learning processes and outcomes, little research has explored how these factors may account for the relationship between collaboration and learning achievement in DGBL.

To better uncover the underlying mechanisms, this study adopts a mediation approach rather than moderation. Moderation would test whether the effect of collaboration on learning achievement depends on intrinsic motivation or flow levels, whereas mediation allows us to investigate the underlying process through which collaboration influences achievement. Given that both intrinsic motivation and flow have been identified as key mechanisms linking instructional interventions to learning outcomes (Chen and Tu 2021; Liu et al. 2024; Müller and Wulf 2022), this study examines both parallel and serial mediation models. The parallel mediation model assumes that collaboration independently affects both flow and intrinsic motivation, while the serial mediation model posits that collaboration enhances flow, which subsequently fosters intrinsic motivation, ultimately leading to improved learning achievement. Thus, this study aims to further examine the possible mediation mechanism of motivation and flow experience in the impact of collaboration on learning achievement in DGBL.

Specifically, the research question in this study is whether collaboration has any effect on learning achievement in DGBL, and whether this effect is mediated by flow experience or

1.	 Collaboration could improve students' learning achievement in DGBL.

2.	 Students' flow experience and intrinsic motivation would mediate the relationship between collaboration and their learning achievement in DGBL in a parallel way.

3.	 Collaboration would also promote students' learning achievement in DGBL through flow experience and intrinsic motivation, serially.

To ensure the effectiveness and authenticity of the collaborative learning experience in this study, we adopted a face-­to-­face dyadic collaboration design involving participants who were familiar with each other. This decision was grounded in both theoretical and empirical evidence.

First, assigning familiar peers to collaborate is grounded in research on group knowledge sharing, which posits that students who have a shared knowledge structure or similar identity before collaborative learning might obtain better learning effects (He et al. 2014; Wagner et al. 2017). In addition, previous studies have suggested that familiar partners in collaborative groups could exhibit high-­quality communication behaviours, which in turn yield more positive learning outcomes (e.g., Zhang et al. 2022; Zhao 2022).

In this context, such arrangements can be considered a form of peer collaboration, which refers to ‘a pair or small group of learners working together on all parts of a task, engaging with one another's contributions, and maintaining a joint problem space’ (Storch  2002, as cited in Kos  2025). Moreover, according to Dillenbourg (1999), collaborative learning is a situation where ‘two or more people learn or attempt to learn something together.’ Therefore, the dyadic collaboration in our study aligns with the fundamental definitions of collaborative learning, while offering practical advantages such as reducing coordination complexity and fostering more balanced participation. Prior studies also support the advantage of dyadic collaboration in promoting student motivation and collaborative problem-­ solving (Zhan et al. 2022), and it has been widely adopted in previous research (e.g., Burns et al. 2024; Chen and Tu 2021; Ruipérez-­Valiente and Kim 2020).

Furthermore, face-­to-­face settings have been shown to promote deeper engagement and interaction compared to computer-­ mediated environments (Fahy et  al.  2001). In such contexts, students can share ideas more efficiently and engage in richer communication, which contributes to more meaningful collaborative learning. Empirical evidence also indicates that learners in face-­to-­face collaborative conditions tend to outperform those in virtual settings (Meyer 2019; Siampou et al. 2014).

Taken together, these considerations informed our decision to adopt face-­to-­face dyadic collaboration between familiar peers, providing a more authentic and effective simulation of real-­world peer collaboration, which is especially important for short-­term, task-­oriented educational interventions.

This study employed a quasi-­experimental design to examine the effects of collaboration on learning achievement in DGBL. Due to the requirement that participants in the collaborative condition be acquainted with each other, strict random assignment was not feasible.

In educational research, quasi-­experimental designs are commonly used due to practical constraints in experimental settings and participant availability (e.g., Brown et  al.  2023; Khattib and Alt  2024; Mohd Bakhir et  al.  2025). When appropriately controlled, quasi-­experimental designs allow for meaningful causal inferences while maintaining ecological validity (Cham et al. 2024; Gopalan et al. 2020; Shadish et al. 2002).

To reduce selection bias, a pretest was used to measure participants' prior knowledge, enabling us to compare learning gains across groups more accurately.

3.4.2   |   Intrinsic Motivation

Intrinsic motivation was assessed using the Intrinsic Motivation Scale (IMS) developed by McAuley et al. (1989) and Ryan (1982) and adapted by Lin et al. (2013). Three subscales (interest, competence, and autonomy) were used to measure intrinsic motivation. While competence and autonomy are key psychological needs in SDT, and interest is often considered a reflection of intrinsic motivation, this study adopts these three dimensions as operational indicators to assess students' intrinsic motivation in the learning task (Lin et al. 2013). Interest included three items, competence included two items, and autonomy included two items. Participants rated each item on a 5-­point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘not at all true’) to 5 (‘very true’). A typical item is ‘This game was fun to play.’ Negatively worded items were reversely coded so that higher scores reflected more positive motivation. In the current study, the Cronbach's α of this scale was 0.879.

3.4.3   |   Flow Experience

The Undergraduates' Flow Experience Questionnaire (UFEQ, Lin 2010) was used to measure flow experience. This questionnaire was divided into 5 dimensions: A sense of control, telepresence, distorted sense of time, enjoyable feelings, and unconsciousness of irrelevant surroundings. A typical item is: ‘I was completely immersed in the virtual world created by the game.’ The scale consists of 19 items, each rated on a 5-­point scale ranging from 1 (extremely not true) to 5 (extremely true), with higher scores indicating higher levels of flow experience. This questionnaire was found to have satisfactory reliability and validity (Lin 2010). In this study, Cronbach's α of the scale was 0.886.

To further ensure the discriminant validity of the measurement instruments, we conducted a correlation analysis and variance inflation factor (VIF) check between the subscales of flow experience and intrinsic motivation. The results (presented in Supporting Information: Appendix 1) showed that all VIF values were well below the commonly accepted threshold of 10 (and most under the more conservative threshold of 5), indicating no serious multicollinearity. In addition, composite reliability (CR) values were satisfactory (flow = 0.889; intrinsic motivation = 0.871), supporting the convergent validity of both constructs.

3.5   |   Procedure

At the beginning of the experiment, the participants first took a pretest to measure their prior knowledge of English vocabulary and their demographic information (e.g., gender, age, and grade level). In the second session, participants were given a 10-­minute introduction to the operations and tasks of the game. Thereafter, participants from the two conditions were divided into different laboratories and required to play a similar game of MOBC for 5 min to familiarise themselves with the game mechanism.

Two participants from each group in the collaborative condition worked together on the same computer and shared an account to complete tasks. They negotiated and cooperated to make decisions together to complete game tasks, while participants in the control group completed the game tasks individually. Participants were given 20 min to complete the game, with instructions to stop if they exceeded this limit. However, all participants finished within the allotted time, so no one had to stop early. Finally, all participants were asked to complete IMS, UFEQ, Knowledge Retention Test, and Knowledge Transfer Test.

3.6   |   Analytic Strategies

Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS 25.0. T statistics and correlations were calculated to test the group differences and bivariate associations of variables. The multiple mediation model was tested by using Model 6 of the PROCESS macro (Hayes 2012). Bootstrapped confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to evaluate the mediated effects (5000 bootstraps and 95% CI). We obtained standardised coefficients for all continuous variables. In addition, prior knowledge, gender, age, and grade level of all participants were included as control variables in the analyses.

Therefore, students' intrinsic motivation was enhanced.

5.2   |   Serial Mediators

Our results confirmed that intrinsic motivation mediates the association between collaboration and learning achievement. That is, collaborative learning could enhance learners' intrinsic motivation and thereby improve their learning achievement. Our study provided evidence that collaboration in DGBL could provide a stimulating and entertaining environment to be a powerful support for enhancing participants' intrinsic motivation, which is the same as the result of Bawa et al.'s (2018) study. Previous studies have also shown that collaborative learning has a positive impact on students' interest, engagement and motivation (Shernoff et al. 2014). Collaborative learning, as an active learning method, makes it easier for students to handle tasks, perceive control and experience a sense of accomplishment with the help of their teammates, which in turn stimulates learners' intrinsic motivation. In addition, collaborative learning could improve students' self-­efficacy and intrinsic motivation, thus improving learning effectiveness (Shen et al. 2013; Khan 2024; Yadav et al. 2021).

The current study also found that the association of collaboration and learning achievement in DGBL is mediated by flow experience, followed by intrinsic motivation. Specifically, collaborative learning helps learners reach a flow state, which positively predicts learners' intrinsic motivation, and ultimately improves learning achievement. According to Csikszentmihalyi  (1997), challenge-­skill balance maintains flow experience, and if the challenge is beyond the student's ability, anxiety will occur. If the challenge fails to attract the player, the player will soon lose interest and want to leave the game (Admiraal et al. 2011; Hefkaluk et al. 2024). Considering the participants' limited prior knowledge, the challenge of the game is possibly beyond their ability. With help from teammates, they could cope with the challenges together, and participants' abilities would be improved gradually, which would lead to a challenge-­skill balance. This balance increases the likelihood of experiencing a flow state in the collaborative condition. When individuals reach a flow state, they are more willing to learn, repeat learning activities, persevere in difficulties (Deater-­Deckard et al. 2013), and sustain their attention to the learning task (Marty-­Dugas et al. 2021). Therefore, flow experience is a positive predictor of intrinsic motivation. In summary, collaborative learning is positively related to learning achievement through the integrated mediation of flow experience and intrinsic motivation in DGBL.

5.3   |   Implications

In sum, the current study broadens the horizon about the influencing mechanism of collaboration on learning achievement in DGBL. Collaboration affected learning achievement through two indirect paths: the mediating role of intrinsic motivation

Practically, the findings offer several insights for educators and instructional designers. First, educators can incorporate collaborative elements into DGBL to enhance students' flow experiences and intrinsic motivation. Structuring tasks that require meaningful peer interaction and joint problem-­solving may help students achieve a better balance between challenge and skill, thereby improving engagement and learning outcomes. Second, game designers can consider embedding features that facilitate collaboration and promote flow, such as adjustable difficulty levels, real-­time feedback, and team-­based goals. Moreover, these insights can inform the design of future curriculum interventions that aim to leverage the motivational and emotional benefits of collaborative gameplay to enhance student learning in digital contexts.

5.4   |   Limitations and Future Directions

Some limitations of this study should be noted, which need to be further improved in future research. First, based on the purpose of this research, only a comparison of the collaborative condition and individual condition in DGBL was investigated. For probing boundary conditions of collaboration, future research could further extend from the perspectives of online collaborative learning, learning content of other subjects, replication studies using different games, or a comparison of traditional classroom and other technology-­supported collaborative learning.

Second, most data were collected via self-­report methods (except knowledge tests), including measures of immersion and engagement. Advanced research methodologies should be employed to examine the learning strategies and processes of learners to investigate the benefits and mechanisms of collaborative learning in depth (Shih et  al.  2010). For example, techniques such as eye tracking, physiological measurements, electroencephalography (EEG), and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) could be used to investigate the cognitive and motivational processes underlying game-­based learning in the future, offering a more nuanced understanding of how DGBL fosters immersive experiences. Moreover, we could also leverage log data from digital tools in future research, utilising learning analytics or educational data mining techniques to identify potential behavioural indicators of flow and intrinsic motivation, and to further validate learners' immersive experiences.

Third, this study did not employ random assignment in the collaborative condition, as students were grouped with individuals they already knew. This decision was based on the research on group knowledge sharing, which suggests that shared prior

Fourth, participants with high prior knowledge were excluded to avoid potential ceiling effects and ensure the effectiveness of the DGBL intervention. While this decision allowed for more accurate detection of learning gains, it may limit the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, prior knowledge is only one of several learner characteristics that may influence flow experience and intrinsic motivation. Other unmeasured individual differences—such as learners' baseline interest in English learning, prior gaming experience, or self-­regulation ability—might also have acted as confounding variables, independently affecting both psychological responses and learning outcomes. Although we attempted to reduce variability through pre-­screening and controlling for prior knowledge, future research should consider controlling for these factors more systematically to better isolate the mechanisms linking collaboration, flow, motivation, and academic achievement. Alternatively, future studies could include participants with more diverse knowledge backgrounds and examine how learners at different knowledge levels benefit from collaborative DGBL.

6   |   Conclusion

Overall, this empirical study makes a valuable contribution to enhancing our understanding of the effectiveness and mechanism of collaboration affecting learning achievement in DGBL. We conclude that collaboration is an effective way of improving learning achievement, flow experience, and intrinsic motivation, which provides more evidence about the efficacy of collaboration in DGBL. We also found that flow experience and intrinsic motivation play an important role in the relationships between collaboration and learning achievement in DGBL. Based on interaction, knowledge co-­construction, and reflection (Feltovich et al. 1996), collaboration may increase the positive experience (e.g., flow experience) in DGBL. Along with the increasing positive experience, students would be more willing to participate in the learning process. Therefore, motivational or emotional factors may be the key features of collaborative learning, which should be considered in future research or practice.
