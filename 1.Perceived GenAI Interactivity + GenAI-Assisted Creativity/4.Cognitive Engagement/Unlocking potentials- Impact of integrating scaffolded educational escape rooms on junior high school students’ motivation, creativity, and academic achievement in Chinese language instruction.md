Unlocking potentials: Impact of integrating scaffolded educational escape rooms on junior high school students’ motivation, creativity, and academic achievement in Chinese language instruction

A B S T R A C T

Keywords: Scaffolded educational escape room Academic achievement Language instruction Motivation Creativity Game-based learning

Learning motivation and creative thinking are widely acknowledged as key competencies underpinning learners’ future competitiveness in rapidly evolving social systems. Yet, conventional Chinese language instruction in secondary schools remains predominantly characterized by rote memorization and literal comprehension, which may suppress motivation and restrict opportunities for higher-order cognitive engagement. To address such gap, this study designed and implemented an instructional intervention that integrates a scaffolded educational escape room (SEER) framework into Chinese language education. The intervention was theoretically grounded in Pintrich and Schunk’s motivational theory and Rhodes’ 4 P model of creativity. The ten-week program was structured into four instructional phases and implemented with a group of 38 seventh-grade students in a junior high school in Taiwan. Adopting a one-group pretest-posttest design with a mixed-methods approach, the study examined the effects of the intervention on students' learning motivation, academic achievement, and creativity. Results indicated significant improvements across all subcomponents of learning motivation and creativity. Although qualitative data reflected students’ perceived gains in comprehension and engagement, quantitative results showed a significant decline in posttest academic performance. Based on these findings, the study offers practical and research-oriented recommendations for future curriculum design and implementation.

1. Introduction

The World Economic Forum (WEF)’s Future of Jobs Report (2023) identified intrinsic motivation, curiosity, and creative thinking among the ten key skills for future competitiveness. In the educational context, a recent nationwide survey by the Child Welfare League Foundation (2023) in Taiwan found that approximately 30% of students lack interest in their studies. Similarly, Huang (2020) pointed out that despite the full implementation of Taiwan’s 12-Year Basic Education policy, many students still exhibit low learning motivation and poor academic performance, which may create a vicious cycle that hinders the development of higher-order thinking skills.

In Chinese language education, there is a growing consensus that instructional practices require innovative reform to better engage learners and cultivate higher-order thinking skills. Traditional curricula have largely emphasized rote memorization of texts and vocabulary, relying heavily on lecture-based teaching and paper-and-pencil assessments (Huang, 2025; Lin & Wang, 2013). Such approaches often neglect students’ active knowledge construction and provide limited opportunities for authentic language use, thereby hindering their capability to connect classroom content with real-life experiences (Yao & Chen, 2023; Chen et al., 2018). As a result, students frequently exhibit low level of engagement and a lack of critical engagement. According to Bloom’s taxonomy of cognitive objectives, Chinese language education has been criticized for remaining at the level of memorization, with insufficient attention to higher-order processes such as evaluation and creation (Chen et al., 2018; Lin, 2025). This lack of cognitively demanding tasks limits students’ opportunity to develop their creativity, problem-solving, or critical thinking skills in language learning, ultimately hindering the development of intrinsic motivation and a sense of academic achievement.

To address these challenges, game-based learning (GBL) has emerged as a promising instructional approach. Play constitute a natural mode of engagement for children, and game-based learning environments that featuring contextual tasks, interactive challenges, and immediate feedback can effectively enhance students’ interest in learning (Nadeem et al., 2023; Taraldsen et al., 2022) while fostering problem-solving skills and creativity (Singer et al., 2006). Among various GBL approaches, escape room pedagogy offers an immersive learning experience that combines teamwork, logical reasoning, and creative problem-solving. Evidence suggests that such activities promote in-depth knowledge application and understanding while also exerting a positive impact on students’ learning motivation, creativity, and academic performance (Rajendran et al., 2025; Taraldsen et al., 2022).

To bridge this gap, the present study designed and implemented an eight-week instructional intervention integrating a Scaffolded Educational Escape Room (SEER) framework into junior high Chinese language education. Adopting game-based learning principles, the SEER model leverages contextual tasks, interactive challenges, and immediate feedback to enhance engagement. Furthermore, the intervention is theoretically grounded in Vygotsky’s sociocultural theory of scaffolding, Pintrich and Schunk’s (1996) motivational theory, and Rhodes’ (1961) 4 P model of creativity. By integrating these frameworks, the SEER model provides a structured approach to cultivating motivation, creativity, and deeper knowledge application—areas that are typically underserved by traditional pedagogy. More specifically, the present study examines the effectiveness of integrating escape room pedagogy into junior high school Chinese language instruction on three dimensions of student learning outcomes: learning motivation, academic performance, and creativity. The instructional design is structured around a four-phase model: Experience, Construct, Design, and Practice. In the Experience phase, students engage in teacher-designed escape room activity that establish a meaningful learning context with clearly defined goals. Next, students use mind mapping to construct knowledge and collaboratively or individually develop puzzles based on course content. Finally, the teacher integrates the student-designed puzzles into a student-created escape room, which students then practice by completing.

This study aims to integrate escape room pedagogy into Chinese language instruction (SEER) and examine its effects on learning motivation and creative thinking. This study can contribute to the field of knowledge by addressing the gaps: the lack of research on escape rooms in Chinese language instruction, the limited exploration of physical and student-generated escape rooms, and the scarcity of empirical evidence linking escape room scaffolding to creativity and language performance. By applying the SEER model, this research provides a theoretically informed and empirically tested instructional design that can inform innovative curriculum development in junior high Chinese education.

2. Literature review

2.1. Game-based learning and escape room pedagogy

Escape rooms are immersive, game-based experiences that combine narrative, puzzle-solving, logical reasoning, and teamwork. Originating in Japan in 2007, escape rooms have gradually expanded beyond entertainment to applications in education, business, and cultural promotion (Nicholson, 2015). In educational context, escape rooms typically feature contextual narratives, puzzle-based challenges, and phased reward mechanisms, to create a problem-driven and collaborative learning environment with strong pedagogical potential (Wiemker et al., 2015). Research has shown that game-based learning not only enhances students’ learning motivation but also promotes comprehension, the development of higher-order thinking skills, and improved strategic regulation (Plass et al., 2015; Qian & Clark, 2016; Tobias et al., 2014). These benefits are exemplified in escape rooms, where inherent challenges and engagement foster learners’ active participation, knowledge construction, and social interaction (Pivec et al., 2003). Social support has been identified as a key factor influencing students’ motivation and creativity (Burnard & Kuo, 2016) and is also recognized as a crucial element in game-based learning.

While early educational escape room research indeed focused heavily on digital platforms, recent literature has increasingly documented non-digital implementations, including pen-and-paper, classroom-based, and hybrid physical escape rooms. Prior studies have demonstrated that such formats can effectively support collaboration, problem-solving, and subject-matter learning across domains (e.g., Karageorgiou et al., 2020; Botturi & Babazadeh, 2021). Moreover, a small but growing body of research has explored learning through game design, in which students assume the role of escape room designers for their peers, thereby engaging in deeper cognitive processing and creative production.

The present study does not claim that physical or student-designed escape rooms are absent in the literature. Rather, it identifies three specific limitations in prior research. First, student-designed escape rooms remain comparatively rare, with only limited empirical investigation in junior high language education. Second, existing studies tend to emphasize design phases or engagement outcomes, while offering limited theoretical integration with motivation theory, scaffolding, and creativity frameworks, and their combination. Third, few studies explicitly examine how scaffolded student design processes and the influence of pedagogical alignment (or misalignment) between motivation, creativity, and academic performance. Therefore, this study positions SEER not as a replacement for existing approaches but as a theoretically integrated extension that systematically embeds student-designed physical escape rooms within a scaffolded instructional approach grounded in motivational theory, creativity research, and sociocultural learning principles.

2.2. Language learning outcomes and related research on escape room

Learning outcomes encompass the progress learners achieved in knowledge, skills, and attitudes. Chang (2007) defines outcomes as the actual competence demonstrated by individuals in a specific domain, while Tanah (2009) views them as the degree of mastery over instructional content. As an immersive instructional tool, escape room pedagogy has gained empirical support for enhancing learning outcomes. Shafiee Rad and Alipour (2024) found that escape room activities increase learner engagement and motivation, thereby improving language acquisition. Similarly, Voreopoulou et al. (2024) reported that such activities help students develop vocabulary use and language expression skills. However, some studies also highlighted potential limitations. L´ opez (2019) noted that some students may become overly focused on puzzle-solving, neglecting the language learning objectives and resulting in gaps in knowledge acquisition. Other research has found that students’ perceived learning abilities may not align with their actual performance, and that time constraints and teamwork issues can arise (Bjork & Bjork, 2011; Dunning et al., 2004; Veldkamp et al., 2020). Therefore, the impact of escape room pedagogy is not uniformly positive effects; its effectiveness depends on instructional design, task difficulty, and student characteristics. Moreover, current literature offering empirical support for language learning outcomes in the context of escape rooms remains limited. To address this gap, present study integrates escape room pedagogy with Chinese language instruction to examine its impact on students’ language learning outcomes, aiming to validate both its practical value and potential challenges within language education.

2.3. Learning motivation and related research on escape room

Learning motivation is the driving forces that initiate and sustain learners’ engagement in the learning process. Pintrich and Schunk (1996) categorize learning motivation into three major components: affective (emotional experiences during learning), expectancy (self-efficacy, control beliefs, and expectations of success), and value (the importance and interest of the learning task) (Eccles, 1983; Eysenck & Keane, 2000; Linnenbrink & Pintrich, 2002). With increasing attention to knowledge transformation and action, some scholars have emphasized the importance of volition (Corno, 1993; Kuhl, 1985; Kuhl, 1987). Based on these perspectives, later frameworks often integrated volitional control as a fourth component to highlight the significance of action (Liu et al., 2010; Chang, 2016; Schunk et al., 2008), which is adopted in this study. Empirical studies demonstrated the potential of escape rooms to enhance learning motivation. From a neuroscience perspective, challenging tasks can stimulate the release of dopamine and endorphins in the brain (Cozolino, 2013; Wimmer et al., 2014). Vidergor (2021) conducted a study with 528 elementary students and found that their game experience with digital escape rooms was significantly more engaging than with other digital games. Huang et al. (2020) reported increasing students’ learning motivation and problem-solving skills with digital escape rooms in elementary science instruction.

In secondary and higher education, escape rooms enhance students’ engagement and satisfaction, and become an effective tool for fostering teamwork and learning motivation (Fuentes-Cabrera et al., 2020; Gonz´ alez-Yubero et al., 2023). For instance, Bell´es-Calvera and Martínez-Hern´ andez (2022) found that, in the language education context, incorporating escape rooms into Content and Language Integrated Learning (CLIL) instruction significantly improves students’ motivation for English learning, participation attitudes, language expression, and collaborative skills.

2.4. Creativity and related research on escape room

Creativity is widely considered as an essential 21st century competency. Since Guilford (1956) presidential address acknowledge the significance of creativity in human development, creativity has gradually developed into a core topic across education, psychology, and neuroscience (Puryear & Lamb, 2020). From an individual development perspective, Torrance (1963) conceptualized creativity as a process-oriented psychological activity encompassing steps such as problem recognition, hypothesis formulation, verification, and revision, operationalized through four key dimensions of creativity, i.e. fluency, flexibility, originality, and elaboration in the Torrance Test of Creative Thinking (TTCT). Wu et al. (1998) adapted this test and then developed the Chinese version of the Creative Thinking Test (CVCTT) (Wu et al., 1998), which serves as the basis for evaluating creativity in this study.

From the context of creativity education, Rhodes (1961) integrated existing literature and propose the 4 P framework—Person, Process, Product, and Press (environment), which remains a fundamental theoretical reference for constructing creativity-supportive learning environment. This study adopts the 4 P framework to structure the experimental curriculum. Building on this model, Kuo et al. (2017) and Kuo et al. (2019) pointed out that effective creativity education requires an ecosystem comprising creativity-oriented teachers, learners, a supportive classroom atmosphere, and sufficient instructional resources. In line with this view, recent trends in creativity education increasingly integrate gamification strategies, and empirical studies reported that such scenario-based approaches enhance students’ higher-order thinking skills (Chang et al., 2025; Chang & Kuo, 2024; Pan et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025).

Escape rooms synthesize these advantages and offer immersive scenarios, clearly defined tasks, and teamwork, aligning closely with Rhodes’ concepts of creative process and creative environment (Press). Research indicates that escape room pedagogy, whether delivered online or in-person, has a positive impact on creativity enhancement (Kuo et al., 2022; Taraldsen et al., 2022; Veldkamp et al., 2020). Grounded in these empirical insights, the current study integrates escape room pedagogy into junior high school Chinese language instruction through a curriculum that combines situational tasks and a student-driven dual-question creation mechanism. It not only provides stimulating learning experiences and interactive scenarios but also has the potential to inspire students’ creative expression and active participation. The effectiveness of this model on students’ creative thinking is examined with “New Creativity Thinking Test”, allowing the evaluation of its impacts on student creativity development and its overall feasibility within the language learning contexts.

Creativity literature, linked with Rhodes’ 4 P framework, offers this study a theoretical foundation, indicating that educational escape rooms’ immersive, problem-based environments can support creative expression. Meanwhile, scaffolding theory explains how structured supports can guide cognitive and motivational development. Building on these foundations, the SEER model integrates motivational, conceptual, strategic, and metacognitive scaffolding to explain how escape-room–based instruction can promote learning motivation, creativity, and academic performance in Chinese language learning. By filling key gaps in the existing literature and proposing a structured model for instructional implementation, this study advances both theoretical understanding and practical applications of escape room pedagogy in language education.

Further comparing SEER with other models cited in the literature, including escaped (Clarke et al., 2017), the Star Model (Botturi & Babazadeh, 2020), and Room2Educ8 (Fotaris & Mastoras, 2022), it is found that while these models primarily function as procedural design frameworks for constructing effective escape room experiences, SEER differs in scope and purpose. SEER conceptualizes escape room pedagogy as a developmental instructional system, emphasizing learners’ motivational regulation, scaffolded cognitive engagement, and creative development across time rather than focusing solely on the mechanics of escape room design. It is important to note that neither the teacher nor the students in this study were instructed to strictly follow escapED, the Star Model, or Room2- Educ8 as formal design templates. Instead, the teacher-designed escape room drew on shared principles across these models, such as clear learning objectives, content–puzzle alignment, and collaborative problem-solving, while remaining pedagogically flexible. Similarly, during the student design phase, learners were not required to apply a formal escape room design model. This decision was intentional, aiming to reduce cognitive overload and ensure developmental appropriateness for junior high students. Rather than procedural fidelity to existing models, the SEER framework prioritized scaffolded learning processes, guiding students to focus on content accuracy, conceptual clarity, creative transformation, and peer usability. In this sense, SEER complements existing escape room design models by shifting the analytical lens from how escape rooms are built to how escape room pedagogy supports learner development over time.

3.1. Study design

This study adopted a one-group pretest-posttest design to compare changes in students’ creativity, learning outcomes, and learning motivation before and after the instructional intervention. The primary goal was to evaluate the effectiveness of integrating escape room pedagogy into Chinese language instruction. To maintain internal validity, participants did not receive any other escape roomrelated courses or similar instructional interventions during the experimental period. 3.1.1. Participants The participants included 38 seventh-grade students from a junior high school in Hsinchu County, Taiwan. Since the number of participants exceeded the instructional setting's capacity, the class was divided into three subgroups for the SEER intervention, with 12–13 students assigned to each group. To ensure equivalence across groups at baseline, a heterogeneous grouping strategy was implemented by assigning students to groups based on their scores from the first formal Chinese language assessment of the semester. Participants were evenly distributed by proficiency level, with each group consisting of three high-achieving students, six to seven intermediate students, and three low-achieving students.

3.2. Course design

Prior research has highlighted potential limitations of escape room pedagogy, such as students overemphasizing puzzle completion at the expense of learning objectives (L´ opez, 2019), time pressure, and misalignment between perceived and actual learning (Bjork & Bjork, 2011; Veldkamp et al., 2020). Therefore, the present study explicitly incorporated these concerns into the SEER design. First, to prevent the neglect of learning objectives, all escape room tasks were explicitly aligned with predefined Chinese language learning goals, including vocabulary application, reading comprehension, rhetorical analysis, and text interpretation. Thus, each puzzle required direct engagement with core instructional content rather than peripheral game mechanics, ensuring that successful task completion depended on accurate application of knowledge rather than trial-and-error guessing. Second, the SEER integrates multi-layered scaffolding (motivational, conceptual, strategic, and metacognitive) to regulate students’ cognitive focus throughout the learning process. Conceptual scaffolding, such as guided mind mapping and structured knowledge-construction activities, was implemented prior to puzzle design to ensure students had adequate content understanding before engaging in game-based tasks. Strategic and metacognitive scaffolding further guided students to reflect on the correctness, logic, and linguistic accuracy of their designs, reducing the risk of superficial engagement.

Specifically, this study designed an eight-week Chinese language curriculum integrating escape room elements, comprising a 50- minute session per week. The course design combined language knowledge, reading comprehension, and rhetorical application from the teaching materials. The materials covered three texts: (1) Selected Analects (《論語選》), focusing on idiom meanings and solved puzzles related to the text; (2) The Silent Group (《那默默的一群》), emphasizing application of key vocabulary through studentdesigned puzzles and escape room activities, and Childhood Memories (《兒時記趣》), developing imagery association and rhetorical applications to cultivate students’ skills in image-text translation and creativity. Throughout the course, students collaborated in groups to complete tasks requiring text interpretation, creative thinking, and problem solving, aiming to deepen their language learning outcomes.

To further ensure domain alignment and instructional transparency, text-specific language learning objectives were explicitly defined for each of the three instructional units. For Selected Analects (《論語選》), the learning goals emphasized accurate interpretation of classical Chinese vocabulary, analysis of character structure, and the ability to infer the moral and philosophical meanings embedded in the text. For The Silent Group (《那默默的一群》), the objectives centered on identifying key ideas, analyzing characterization and descriptive techniques, and applying vocabulary knowledge to contextual interpretation tasks. For Childhood Recollections (《兒時記趣》), instruction targeted the recognition and application of rhetorical devices (e.g., exaggeration and metaphor), understanding their effects on textual imagery, and articulating evaluative judgments through written reflection. Grounding the escape-room activities in these text-aligned objectives ensured that they remained rooted in disciplinary language competencies rather than devolving into generic problem-solving exercises.

The instructional process followed four stages: Experience, Construct, Design, and Practice. During the Experience phase, students engaged with teacher-designed escape room activities to familiarize themselves with the game mechanics and knowledge-puzzle integration. In the Construct phase, students transformed relevant knowledge into mind maps as a foundation for puzzle design. During the Design phase, guided by the teacher, students developed puzzles based on key components from the language materials (reading comprehension, idiom use, classical Chinese analysis, Chinese cultural knowledge, etc.). It should be noted that students engaged with a variety of escape room tasks, including both their own-designed puzzles and those created by other groups. This kind of design has two benefits: First, it preserves the authenticity of the escape experience for players, requiring genuine problem-solving rather than recall. While playing other groups’ escape rooms, students encountered puzzles without prior knowledge of the solutions, ensuring authentic problem-solving experiences. This mixed implementation allowed students to alternate between designer and player roles, supporting deeper understanding through immediate peer feedback on puzzle clarity, difficulty, and logical coherence. Secondly, during reflective and revision activities, it provided designers with cross-group immediate peer feedback on puzzle clarity, difficulty, and logical coherence, extending learning beyond gameplay to include evaluative judgment and iterative refinement. In the Practice phase, student-designed puzzles were integrated into formal escape-room scenarios across two full cycles of student design and implementation.

Finally, to enhance instructional effectiveness, structured debriefing sessions were conducted after both the teacher-designed and student-designed escape room activities. Following the teacher-designed escape room, the teacher led whole-class discussions connecting puzzle-solving processes to targeted Chinese language competencies, addressing misconceptions, and consolidating key concepts. 3.3. Research instruments and analysis

This study employed an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, in which quantitative pretest-posttest analyses were first conducted to identify changes in students’ learning motivation, creativity, and academic achievement following the SEER intervention. A multifaceted assessment strategy was employed, including a learning motivation scale, a creative thinking test, and standardized course assessments, along with multiple qualitative data sources, to ensure the validity and reliability of the data. Semistructured interviews were subsequently adopted and analyzed to explain and contextualise the quantitative results, with particular attention to the observed decline in students’ summative assessment performance. Mixed-methods integration was implemented throughout the study. Quantitative results first guided the purposive sampling of interview participants and shaped the focus of interview prompts. Interview questions were then refined to triangulate quantitative findings. The themes derived from thematic analysis were used to explain specific quantitative patterns and to triangulate evidence across datasets. The following sections detail the quantitative and qualitative research instruments and analysis methods.

3.3.1. Quantitative research instruments The quantitative component utilized psychometric tests and school summative assessments. After conducting descriptive analyses to understand variable distributions, the data were analyzed with SPSS 17 software to perform descriptive statistics and paired-sample t-tests, examining significant differences in the three variables, i.e. learning motivation, creativity and academic achievement, before and after the intervention.

3.3.1.1. Learning motivation. This study adopted the "Junior High School Chinese Learning Motivation Scale" adapted by Chang (2016). This scale specifically measures learning motivation in the Chinese language domain and is theoretically based on Liu’s (2009) revision of Pintrich’s motivation theory, which includes four dimensions: value, expectancy, affect, and volition. The scale uses a five-point Likert format, comprising 36 items. The Cronbach’s alpha values for each dimension range from.80 to.95, with an overall scale reliability of.95, indicating good reliability and validity.

3.3.1.2. Creativity. The creativity assessment used in this study was the “New Test of Creative Thinking Test” developed by Wu et al. (1998), which includes both verbal and figural forms of tests. In the figural test, participants are asked to design as many figures as possible based on the Chinese character “人” (person), measuring four dimensions: fluency, flexibility, originality, and elaboration. Each dimension’s Cronbach’s alpha ranges from.86 to.96, demonstrating good reliability. In the verbal test, participants consider multiple uses for chopsticks, measuring fluency, flexibility, and originality. The Cronbach’s alpha values for these dimensions range from.91 to.99, indicating excellent reliability and validity overall. Regarding rater training and inter-rater reliability, the creativity test was scored according to the standardized scoring manual of Wu et al. (1998). Two raters underwent calibration sessions using sample protocols before scoring, and then the trained raters independently scored all responses. Inter-rater reliability was computed using ICC (two-way random, absolute agreement). The agreement was strong, across dimensions (ICC range =.84–.93), indicating satisfactory scoring consistency.

3.3.1.3. Chinese language learning achievement. Regarding academic achievement, the study employed two official school summative assessments administered at the beginning and end of the semester, covering vocabulary explanation, text comprehension, reading tests, and short-answer writing. These assessments were designed and reviewed by the teaching team to ensure content validity. Additionally, to allow for pretest-posttest comparisons and to control for potential variations in test difficulty, raw scores were converted to T-scores, thereby enhancing the reliability of the results.

3.3.2. Qualitative research instrument Semi-structured interviews with teachers and students were conducted in this study to obtain an in-depth understanding of the learning processes and to triangulate the quantitative results. While quantitative data reveals what occurred, qualitative investigation can explain how and why certain outcomes have emerged as a result of the SEER intervention. A protocol derived from the research objectives was used to guide the interviews, providing structure while also allowing for flexibility to explore participants’ perceptions. Purposive sampling was employed to recruit both teachers and students from the course, ensuring that the selected participants could provide rich, diverse, and contextually relevant insights into the intervention.

The analysis of the interview data followed an inductive approach (Matthews & Ross, 2011) to explore primary themes and derive first-hand insights directly from participant accounts (Thomas, 2006). The procedure adhered to Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-phase thematic analysis. To strengthen the trustworthiness and validity of these findings, data and methodological triangulation were employed. Multiple data sources, including student and teacher interview transcripts and classroom observation notes, were analyzed to provide an interpretively rich account of the learning processes.

3.3.3. Interview procedures Semi-structured interviews were conducted after the experimental teaching to gain deeper insights into students’ learning experiences, perceptions of learning outcomes, and responses to the SEER. A purposive sampling strategy was employed to ensure representation of students with diverse academic achievement levels (high, medium, and low) and both genders. A total of 18 students were recruited for the interviews. To encourage open discussion and reduce power distance, students were interviewed in small groups of three to four, resulting in five groups, each lasting approximately 60 min. In addition, two Chinese language teachers involved in the course implementation were interviewed individually, each lasting approximately 45–60 min. All interviews followed a semistructured protocol aligned with the research objectives, allowing flexibility to probe emerging themes. Sample interview questions for the students’ interviews are as follows: “How did the escape room activities influence your understanding of the Chinese texts?” “Do you feel your Chinese learning has improved through this course? Why or why not?” and “Which parts of the escape room activities were most challenging or meaningful to Chinese learning and your creative thinking?” With a different focus, teacher interviews centered on instructional observations and assessment issues, with questions such as: “What kinds of learning outcomes do you think were enhanced or constrained by SEER?” and

“How well do current assessments reflect students’ learning in this course?”

4. Results: analysis of student outcomes

4.1. Analysis and interpretation of Chinese language learning performance

This study used the school's midterm exam as the pretest and the final exam as the posttest to evaluate the impact of incorporating SEER activities into Chinese language instruction on students' academic performance. The results showed that students had an average pretest score of 53.87 (SD = 8.55), while the average posttest score was 48.74 (SD = 10.94), indicating a declining trend in learning performance. The paired sample t-test reached statistical significance (t(37) = 3.800, p = .001), with an effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.617, indicating a moderate effect. This result indicates a statistically significant decline in students’ performance on traditional summative assessments following the intervention period. See Table 4-1 for the paired sample t-test results of learning performance. This decline was treated as an explanatory case for the semi-structured interviews, and the resulting themes were used to account for this specific quantitative pattern (see Table 4-1-1).

However, the qualitative data revealed a discrepancy with the quantitative results. Most students reported that their Chinese language learning had improved. They expressed that this teaching method made it easier for them to absorb knowledge and helped them improve their performance in the subject, as solving puzzles often required applying what they had learned in Chinese class, which naturally contributed to their learning.

“Learning while playing makes it easier to absorb knowledge, even more than in a regular class,” “I think it was helpful because the escape room made me understand Chinese better.”

“In order to escape successfully, I had to study the text thoroughly and understand parts I didn’t get before.”

“Using games for learning leaves a deeper impression. It helped me quickly become familiar with word pronunciation, forms, and rhetorical techniques.”

However, in the students’ feedback, some also pointed out that incorporating the escape room made the course more intense and fast-paced, making it hard to keep up. Some content even had to be self-studied. Teachers also made related observations and speculations following implementation of the course.

“Because the pace becomes really fast, it’s hard to keep up, and some things have to be self-studied.”

“It seems students’ test scores didn’t improve much—more like stayed the same. Maybe that’s because compared to the escape room class, going back to the traditional Chinese lessons felt painful, which might actually hinder memory-based learning.”

5. Discussion

The present study investigated the impact of a SEER-based intervention on students' learning motivation, creativity, and academic achievement within the context of traditional Chinese language education.

5.1. Chinese academic learning

It should be noted that in interpreting the academic performance results, an important methodological and interpretive consideration concerns the ecological validity of using school-wide summative assessments (midterm and final exams) to evaluate a bounded instructional intervention such as SEER. Educational escape rooms cannot address the full breadth of content assessed in end-of-term examinations. In this study, the midterm and final exams covered cumulative curriculum content, including materials only partially or indirectly addressed during the SEER intervention. As a result, these assessments served as contextually authentic indicators of learning outcomes, while future studies may employ more targeted measures to capture a broader range of instructional effects associated with the SEER intervention.

In terms of quantitative analyses, there was a significant decline in students’ learning performance, whereas qualitative data indicated students’ subjective perception of improved Chinese language comprehension, suggesting a discrepancy between perceived and demonstrated learning achievement. One influential factor contributing to the academic decline was the system-level stress imposed by the curriculum structure. Compressing eight instructional sessions into an already tight semester schedule drastically reduced the time allocated for systematic memorization and review. This acceleration forced students to engage in unplanned selfstudy, fragmenting their cognitive resources and increasing cognitive load (Kang & Kuo, 2024). The problem was exacerbated by a profound construct misalignment between instruction and assessment. Furthermore, the SEER pedagogy deliberately targeted higher-order learning objectives, culminating in analysis and creation. Conversely, the school’s final exam focused predominantly on low-order recall and literal comprehension. Importantly, qualitative evidence suggests that students’ cognitive engagement was not shallow but differently allocated. Learners invested substantial effort in meaning-making, structural analysis, and application during puzzle design and solving, while engaging less frequently in deliberate memorization aligned with exam formats. Thus, the learning paradox reflects not a lack of cognition, but a misalignment between higher-order learning processes and traditional assessment visibility.

The negative effect may also reflect inadequate allocation of time and resources to test-specific, low-level objectives, rather than a failure of the innovative pedagogy to deliver its intended high-level outcomes. This structural tension also contributed to the "motivational crash" observed by teachers (T-04), in which the contrast between engaging SEER sessions and mandated rote review made the latter seem painful, hindering necessary final efforts. Furthermore, enjoyable and fluent learning experiences may also cause an illusion of competence, making learners overestimate mastery, particularly when feedback is implicit rather than explicit (Bjork & Bjork, 2011; Dunning et al., 2004). In game-based contexts, students may perceive gains in engagement and application while underestimating deficits in retention and recall.

Research suggests several explanations may account for the decline in performance. First, as L´ opez (2019) observed, students may have been overly focused on puzzle-solving and task completion, neglecting the core learning objectives. Despite improved engagement, ineffective regulation of learning strategies can result no enhanced improvement in academic performance (Weinstein & Mayer, 1983). Motivation enhancement needs to be accompanied by effective cognitive and review techniques to foster academic achievement. Future instructional designs should incorporate structured strategy training and diverse supplemental resources to accommodate different learning preferences.

Beyond the influences of learning strategies and styles, this study also identified a gap between students’ cognitive awareness and their actual learning outcomes. Consistent with prior literature, highly interactive and immersive learning activities can foster a "learning illusion," where active participation and positive self-perception lead students to overestimate their mastery of the knowledge. However, without subsequent consolidation and practice, the knowledge often fails to be firmly internalized and thereby retrieve information accurately during assessments (Bjork & Bjork, 2011; Dunning et al., 2004; Veldkamp et al., 2020). Therefore, it is recommended that courses integrate reflective activities, such as learning summaries and self-assessments, to promote cognitive awareness and knowledge reinforcement. Furthermore, both teachers and students reported that the end-of-term intervention during the period coincided with intensive course pace, expanded testing scope, and numerous school activities. These competing demands constrained the time available for preparation and fragmented students’ cognitive resources (Sweller, 1988; Kanfer & Ackerman, 1989). 5.2. Learning motivation

The findings of this study indicate that significant improvements were observed across the four measured dimensions of students’ learning motivation following the SEER curriculum. These results align with the learning motivation theory proposed by Pintrich and Schunk (1996), who highlighted that meaningful learning experiences, coupled with enhanced students’ self-efficacy and interest in the curriculum, can effectively foster motivation and drive learners to take action and produce outcomes.

The key to the successful enhancement of intrinsic Value was the shift in student agency. In contrast to previous game-based learning studies where Value and Expectancy sometimes failed to improve, the requirement for students to become active creators of the curriculum (puzzle designers) transformed their perception of content. When students actively use rhetorical devices or complex textual comprehension to construct a functioning puzzle, the learning content is immediately vested with utility. This transition from passive consumer to active creator provided an intrinsic anchor for sustained motivation, demonstrating that content production is a superior mechanism for elevating the Value dimension compared to mere consumption or extrinsic rewards.

Furthermore, the social nature of the tasks, where success required contribution from all members, even low-achievers (B-32), acted as a powerful social scaffold, reinforcing “Expectancy” beliefs. The group environment provided external accountability, translating positive feelings (Affect) into sustained, observable effort (Volitional Control). This pattern of motivational change suggests the potential efficacy of the SEER model in junior high Chinese language learning, addressing a critical gap identified in the literature.

The findings are also consistent with a growing body of empirical studies conducted in domestic and international contexts (Huang et al., 2020; Gonz´ alez-Yubero et al., 2023; Vidergor, 2021). Specifically, this study resonates to prior research on gamification in language acquisition (Bell´es-Calvera & Martínez-Hern´ andez, 2022) while addressing a significant gap in the context of Chinese language education. It provides empirical confirmation that integrating SEER activities into Chinese language education instruction not only boosts student curiosity and participation but also substantially fosters a multifaceted spectrum of learning motivation.

5.3. Creativity

The quantitative results from the creativity scale revealed significant improvements across all four dimensions: fluency, flexibility, originality, and elaboration, demonstrating the positive impact of the SEER intervention on creativity development. This finding can be interpreted by Rhodes’ (1961) 4 P framework, which suggests that the curriculum effectively stimulates students’ creative thought process (process) and tangible outcomes (product) through engaging students in situation-oriented tasks and problem-solving processes. More specifically, the SEER intervention successfully engineered a nurturing, creative Press (environment) by establishing a culture that sanctioned "wild and free" thinking (T-08) and minimized the stress of seeking single, correct answers (B-29). This environment was crucial for unlocking the cognitive Process. The puzzle design tasks demanded complex divergent thinking, requiring students to rapidly generate multiple concepts (Fluency) and transform textual knowledge into diverse formats (Flexibility) (G-39). These cognitive demands directly addressed weaknesses in ideation noted by international assessments like PISA.

The creation of the functional escape room challenges served as the Product dimension. The quality of these products improved because the Metacognitive Scaffolding ensured refinement (Elaboration). Students internalized the value of precision when they realized their puzzles had to work for their peers (T-07). Crucially, the recognition received for these functional creative outputs solidified the student's identity as a creative Person (G-42). The SEER model thus provided not only a creative task but also a validated creative identity, offering strong evidence for its utility as a tool for creativity development in the humanities.

The results further reflect current trends in creativity education research, which emphasize the significance of constructing a supportive pedagogical environment through teacher facilitation, a creative climate, and adequate resources (Kuo et al., 2017; Kuo et al., 2025). Specifically, this study provides empirical evidence for the assertions regarding the effectiveness of gamified teaching in cultivating higher-order thinking (Chang et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2023; and Kuo et al., 2024). It also covers broadly with findings from previous studies on escape room-based curricula (Kuo et al., 2022; Taraldsen et al., 2022; Veldkamp et al., 2020). Therefore, the present study demonstrates the potential of integrating escape room activities into creativity education, offering a valuable model for future creativity-oriented curriculum design. However, it should also be cautious that the large effect sizes observed in figural creativity may reflect both genuine cognitive engagement through iterative design tasks and possible test familiarity. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously in the absence of a control group.

Overall, the evidence indicated that SEER was an effective approach for nurturing motivation, Chinese language achievement, and creativity. However, the impact should be interpreted as a collective effect, whereas the motivational and affective benefits observed in this study are consistent with prior research on educational escape rooms and game-based learning more broadly, the contribution of SEER lies not in novelty or gameplay alone. Rather, SEER distinguishes itself by systematically integrating motivational theory, multilevel scaffolding, and the 4 P model of creativity into a coherent instructional framework. In other words, the observed gains in motivation and creativity should not be attributed solely to the presence of an escape room or gamified activity. Educational escape rooms can vary widely in pedagogical depth, and without intentional scaffolding, they risk remaining engaging yet instructionally superficial. 6. Educational implications and research limitations

6.1. Implications for practices

Drawing on feedback from teachers and students, this study proposes several practical recommendations for implementing SEER pedagogy in junior high Chinese language education. Firstly, curriculum design requires a collaborative effort, leveraging professional learning communities to facilitate resource sharing and reflective practice across teaching departments. “Learning paradox” was identified in this study, where students reported substantial perceived learning gains despite declining performance on traditional assessments. This highlights the need for greater alignment between instructional design, learner perception, and assessment practices. Furthermore, curriculum compression raises legitimate concerns about SEER’s viability in exam-driven contexts. The SEER should function as a strategic complement rather than a replacement for comprehensive curriculum coverage. Without sufficient time for consolidation, retrieval practice, and exam-oriented review, immersive interventions may displace necessary rehearsal of foundational knowledge, indicating the need for hybrid scheduling models.

Secondly, regarding the course delivery, in addition to the phases outlined in the SEER (see Table 3–1), retrieval practice and brief individual reflections after escape-room activities can help students calibrate perceived understanding against actual knowledge retention, reducing learning illusions through metacognitive monitoring and timely correction of misconceptions. Thirdly, to enhance student engagement and mitigate the marginalization of low-achieving students, a task-leveling system should be introduced to implement differentiated instruction effectively, thereby mitigating motivational decline during transitions to more routine learning tasks. Fourthly, the implementation frequency needs to balance pedagogical innovation and the prescribed instructional pace, while reserving adequate time for knowledge consolidation and reflective practice to ensure learning effectiveness. Finally, formative assessment mechanisms should be systematically embedded within SEER-based instruction. Teachers suggested adopting an iterative design approach and regularly collecting student feedback to make timely curriculum adjustments, thereby improving instructional quality and relevance.

6.2. Limitations and suggestions for future research

The present study offers initial yet strong evidence on the positive effects of integrating SEER activities on students’ learning achievement, motivation, and creativity, the conclusions should be read in light of potential limitations. First, the single-group pretestposttest design provided a valuable foundation for detecting initial changes in learning outcomes, however, including a control would further strengthen internal validity for causal inference. Second, assessment design should be diversified to better capture higher-order learning outcomes fostered by SEER pedagogy. Performance-based assessments, such as rubric-based evaluation of puzzle design, analytical writing tasks, or learning portfolios, can complement traditional paper-based exams and more accurately reflect students’ application, analysis, and creative thinking abilities. Thirdly, the sample from a single junior high school in Taiwan enabled an indepth examination within a specific context; subsequent research across diverse contexts and populations will be essential to explore the generalizability of the findings.

The one-group pretest–posttest design constitutes a significant limitation of the present study and necessitates careful interpretation of the findings. Without a control or comparison group, it is difficult to rule out all possible alternative influences. Thus, the observed changes in motivation and creativity should be interpreted as associations temporally aligned with the intervention, rather than as definitive causal effects. Evidently, the study is case-sensitive, as statistical results revealed significant pre–post increases with medium to large effect sizes, particularly in creativity and motivation. However, these results must be contextualized within the study’s relatively small sample size, which also limits strong population-level generalizations. Furthermore, although the TTCT-based instrument is designed to measure divergent thinking and is relatively robust to short-term repetition effects (Wu et al., 1998; Kuo et al., 2022), the absence of a parallel form or a control group prevents a full disentangling of intervention effects from potential practice effects. The findings should be regarded as exploratory and theory-building rather than as confirmation of causality for the SEER.

Post hoc analysis with statistical power suggests that the detected effects were sufficiently large to reach significance despite the limited sample, particularly for creativity outcomes. Therefore, caution is warranted when extending these findings to other student populations, grade levels, or educational systems. To address these limitations, future research may consider including a control group or adopting a randomized controlled design with larger, more diverse samples. Explicit comparison groups receiving traditional instruction would also allow stronger causal inference regarding the unique contributions of SEER or relevant innovative pedagogical practices. Learning achievement was measured primarily through traditional standardized tests focused on memory-level outcomes, a deliberate choice to align with core curricular objectives; future studies would benefit from complementing these measures with innovative process-based assessments to capture a broader spectrum of higher-order thinking and learning outcomes. Furthermore, six students were absent during either the pretest or posttest creativity assessment due to school activities or sickness, resulting in a reduced matched sample.

Creativity assessments required matched pre–post data; six students were absent for either the pretest or posttest and were excluded from paired analyses. To examine whether missingness was plausibly random, we compared students with complete creativity data

Further examination of the score distributions indicated that the six students with missing creativity data were not concentrated/ aggregated in either the highest or lowest performance ranges; rather, their Chinese achievement and learning motivation scores were evenly distributed across both higher and lower levels of the baseline. This pattern provides additional warrant that the missing data was not systematically associated with initial baseline performance or motivation. Nevertheless, the reduced sample size, still, lowers statistical precision. Though, the current study additionally collected and analyzed qualitative data to triangulate the findings, the interpretation of results, especially the causal relationship, need to be cautious.

Lastly, the eight-week intervention was sufficient in mapping the implementation of SEER pedagogy and short-term effects, creating a foundation for longitudinal studies to investigate its long-term effects and control for external variables. It should be noted that although the TTCT-based instrument is designed to measure divergent thinking and is relatively robust to short-term repetition effects, the absence of a parallel form or control group makes it impossible to disentangle intervention effects from potential practice effects fully.

Therefore, future research is recommended to consider the following: (1) employ control groups with expanded sampling to enhance validity; (2) extend the research period to conduct longitudinal follow-ups for assessing sustained impact; (3) incorporate moderating variables such as individual difference in learning to explore differentiated impacts on individuals varying characteristics; and (4) adopt diverse assessment approaches such as learning portfolios and performance-based evaluations to comprehensively reflect the impact on students’ learning processes.

7. Conclusion

This study examined the effects of integrating scaffolded educational escape room activities into Chinese language education on junior high school students’ learning achievement, motivation, and creativity. The results indicate that while students generally expressed enthusiasm and positive feedback toward the course and showed significant improvements across all dimensions of learning motivation and creativity, paradoxically, the positive outcomes were accompanied by a decline in measured learning achievement. This decline may be attributed to mismatches between game-based learning and traditional examination demands, a condensed course schedule, and insufficient reflection time. In essence, SEER pedagogy may serve as a catalyst for fostering learning motivation and creativity by stimulating students’ willingness to participate, emotional engagement, and creative expression. Future implementations should incorporate strategic instructional design and assessment adjustments to balance curricular innovation with learning objectives.

Appendix A. Sample Escape-Room Puzzles

A1. Selected Analects One of the primary language learning objectives of the Selected Analects unit was to develop students’ understanding of classical Chinese vocabulary and sentence meaning. Accordingly, the escape-room password design was directly grounded in textual interpretation.

Students were first provided with a password chart containing several four-character Chinese idioms. These idioms included both positive and negative connotations. They were then given the textual prompt: “Select the good and follow it; correct what is not good” (擇其善者而從之，其不善者而改之). This sentence conveys the idea that positive or virtuous elements should be retained and learned from, whereas negative elements should be corrected or avoided.

Based on this textual clue, students were required to identify and retain idioms with positive meanings while eliminating those with negative connotations. After marking all appropriate idioms, the highlighted entries revealed the numerical code required to unlock the box (7610).

A2. The Silent Group In The Silent Group unit, one of the key language learning objectives was to help students recognize the characteristics of different occupations and make semantic judgments based on key vocabulary. Accordingly, the puzzle design required students to interpret clusters of four-character idioms representing distinct professional traits.

Students were first presented with three sets of idiomatic clues, each corresponding to a different occupation:

Set 1: Go through fire and water (赴湯蹈火), Extremely urgent (十萬火急), A disastrous fire (祝融之災) Set 2: Travel a thousand miles in a single day (日行千里), An experienced horse knows the way (老馬識途), Familiar with the route (輕車熟路)

Set 3: Create something out of nothing (無中生有), Sweat pouring down like rain (汗如雨下), A towering building rises from the ground (萬丈高樓平地起)

Through semantic interpretation of the idioms, students inferred that the three occupations represented were: (1) firefighter, (2) driver, and (3) construction worker.

Students then located the corresponding occupation numbers from the provided occupational list, which together formed the password required to unlock the box (437).
