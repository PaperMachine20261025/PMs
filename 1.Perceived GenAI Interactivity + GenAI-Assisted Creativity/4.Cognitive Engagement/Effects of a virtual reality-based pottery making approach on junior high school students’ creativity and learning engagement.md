Jue-Qi Guan a, Liang-Hui Wang a, Qu Chen b, Kai Jinc and Gwo-Jen Hwang d

aDepartment of Educational Technology, Zhejiang Normal University, Jinhua, People’s Republic of China; bCollege of Geography and Environmental Sciences, Zhejiang Normal University, Jinhua, People’s Republic of China; cDepartment of Education Information Technology, East China Normal University, Shanghai, People’s Republic of China; dGraduate Institute of Digital Learning and Education, National Taiwan University of Science and Technology, Taipei, Taiwan

ABSTRACT Pottery making in technology education aims to foster students’ practical and creative ability. In a typical pottery making class, students receive the teacher’s instruction on the fundamental techniques used in pottery making and have opportunities to practice. It is meaningful to construct an observation-doing-reﬂection process for students in a pottery making class to promote their learning experience and performance. Therefore, in this study, a Virtual Reality (VR)-based pottery making approach was applied to technology education in a junior high school to examine its eﬀects on students’ creativity and learning engagement. A total of 97 seventh graders from three classes participated in pottery making in class. One class of 34 students was assigned to be the experimental group learning with the VR-based approach, while the other two classes were assigned to be the control groups, with 30 students learning with the paper-and-pencil approach and 33 students with the clay-based approach. The results showed that the students learning with the VR-based approach were not only more creative in terms of their products, but also had higher cognitive engagement than those in the control groups. Moreover, the VR-based approach improved the students’ behavioral, emotional and social engagement compared to the paper-and-pencil approach.

Introduction

Technology education integrates technology with crafts, arts, natural science and so on, with a focus on providing students with practical and exploratory experience and developing creativity and associated skills among students (Williams, 2009). Diﬀerent countries use diﬀerent terms to describe technology education (Rasinen, 2003). In China, a Labor and Technology course is generally on the curriculum in primary schools and junior high schools. Pottery making is one important topic of the course, aimed to foster students’ practical and creative ability.

In a typical pottery making class, a teacher provides tutorials on the fundamental techniques used in pottery making, and learners are given the opportunity to design and practice. The clay used as the basic material for making pottery is processed through several stages. Instruction does seem to signiﬁcantly increase students’ knowledge of ceramic terms and techniques (Brewer & Colbert, 1992), but students may be unable to completely observe each of the small steps taken in the pottery making process (Köhler, 2012). Among the focuses of technology education, developing students’ creativity in the classroom is an international priority (Craft, 2011). On the other hand, learning engagement is a strong predictor of academic performance, including knowledge retention and critical thinking (Hughes et al., 2008; Pascarella et al., 2010). Moreover, they are fundamentally and reciprocally linked in the view of ﬂow theory (Gardiner, 2017). It remains a challenging issue to facilitate and support students’ creativity and active engagement in learning (Henrie et al., 2015; Shneiderman et al., 2006). Scholars have shared the assumption that an individual or a group’s creative potential would be enhanced if taught in a reliable way, and the beneﬁts of support tools have been validated (Shneiderman et al., 2006). Wang and Nickerson (2017) pointed out that individuals and groups need the assistance of technological tools to generate innovative designs in various domains. The design of pottery has developed gradually from manual to computer-aided (Cao et al., 2020). Meanwhile, technologymediated learning has a signiﬁcant, direct eﬀect on learning engagement (Hu & Hui, 2012).

Among the emerging technologies, Virtual Reality (VR) technology has been frequently discussed and used in education and training. Recently, VR has been adopted in the teaching of geometry (Hwang & Hu, 2013), geography (Lisichenko, 2015), history (Yildirim et al., 2018), music (Innocenti et al., 2019), STEM (Chen et al., 2020), and so on in K-12 classrooms. It is indicated that the application of VR technology provides students with active learning opportunities (Bridge et al., 2007) and could help active, project-based and scenario-based learning (Chandramouli et al., 2014), which are meaningful for students’ creativity and learning engagement. For instance, Chen et al. (2020) applied VR-based active learning in STEM education, and found that 10th-grade students’ understanding of abstract scientiﬁc concepts and other learning performance were enhanced. Yang et al. (2018) oﬀered VR-based activities in a design challenge, and found that the participants in the immersive VR environment outperformed those in the paperand-pencil condition in terms of creativity.

In the pottery making course, allowing students to interact with virtual equipment would be a good choice. The mature VR has enabled the application of virtual pottery to be extended to education (Gaitatzes et al., 2001). Although the issue of virtual pottery making has been addressed (Chiang et al., 2018), there are few recent studies providing the eﬀects and implications of VR in a pottery making course. Therefore, a VR-based pottery making approach that emphasizes experiencing and creating is proposed in a junior high school Labor and Technology course to increase students’ creativity and learning engagement. Based on the above research objectives, the following research questions were addressed:

(1) Does the VR-based approach help students be more creative with their ceramic products as com-

pared to the conventional learning approaches? (2) Does the VR-based approach better encourage students’ active engagement as compared to the

conventional learning approaches?

Active learning

Active learning is “anything that involves students in doing things and thinking about the things they are doing” (Bonwell & Eison, 1991). Active learning is achieved through experiences and reﬂection (Fink, 2003). Experience is an essential component of the educational process, and learners’ experience in active learning takes two forms, observing and doing (Fink, 2003). Active learning is always related to other instructional strategies, including experiential learning, learning-by-doing, and problem-based learning. Using active learning means that students actively engage in complex tasks and have more time devoted to experience and reﬂection that otherwise would be used for teachers’ lecturing. Active learning pedagogy has been shown to be a more eﬀective teaching and learning strategy than traditional lectures, especially in terms of developing students’ problem-solving skills and ability to apply knowledge in various situations (De Freitas, 2006).

Many scholars have attempted to apply active learning in various courses to increase students’ learning outcomes. Schiller et al. (2013) used active learning in undergraduate marketing courses. It was found that when “doing” was incorporated into a learning activity, enjoyment and learning outcomes improved signiﬁcantly. Vinay (2018) used the active learning strategy of collaborative learning through the method of role-play to develop students’ higher order thinking skills. The results showed that the students who had taken a Psychology course experienced a marked increase in their performance. Chen et al. (2020) proposed a cyclical active learning pattern using a VR-based approach in STEM education, which alternated a starting group discussion with solving problems and developing a product. It was found that the students’ learning performances and hands-on abilities were enhanced.

However, scholars have also pointed out that some obstacles might prevent teachers from using active learning approaches in a course. These obstacles include, ﬁrst, a lack of conﬁdence on the part of teachers who feel unfamiliar with this form of pedagogy and have diﬃculty redesigning traditional instruction which comprised good lectures (Bonwell & Eison, 1991; Niemi, 2002; Pundak & Rozner, 2008; Snyder, 2003); second, a lack of materials or equipment to support active learning approaches (Bonwell & Eison, 1991); and third, students with low willingness and ability feeling uncomfortable with this learning process (Watkins et al., 2007). Besides, providing students with active learning methods to construct their knowledge and develop their skills is a recurring theme. Therefore, developing an eﬀective active learning activity at the class level has become an important and challenging issue.

Virtual reality (VR)

VR is a computer environment in which the users are immersed and can experience simulated visual, auditory, and force sensations (Ostler, 1994), which gives them a sense of presence. This sense of presence through immersion is a main motivation for using VR in education (Jensen & Konradsen, 2018). In the timeline of technology development, from the desktop-based VR systems, fully immersed VR systems to distributed VR systems, VR has been giving participants an increasing amount of space and improving their sensory experiences.

VR can simulate physical settings and facilitate access to limited resources in education, providing the possibility to participate in virtual spaces where learners could immerse themselves to have realistic experiences (Wei et al., 2015). Additionally, the application of VR to teaching has great prospects for new styles (Kartiko et al., 2010), and the participants are expected to transform the way they learn (Lau & Lee, 2015; Merchant et al., 2014). Minocha and Reeves (2010) found that a 3D virtual environment could support active learning by doing.

Previous studies showed some encouraging results regarding the use of VR in education (Dalgarno & Lee, 2010). Merchant et al. (2014) examined the eﬀects of VR in K-12 or higher education

A virtual reality-based pottery making approach

In this study, we used the active learning pedagogy to guide the design and development of the VR- based pottery-making approach in a Labor and Technology course. Figure 1 shows the structure of this design, which consists of the teacher’s role, the students’ “observation-doing-reﬂection” activities, and the VR-based learning environment.

In contrast to the conventional teaching of pottery making, the course introduced various types of porcelain ware to oﬀer more potential for designing ceramics. Considering that explicit technological knowledge would involve speciﬁc tasks, a Smart Home was introduced to create a thematic situation for designing ceramics and inspiring students’ creativity. The teacher organized two activities, which were adapted for the course.

Learning involves personal construction resulting from an experiential process (Bruner, 1986). In this study, the students’ “observation” activity involved the exploration of the Longquan celadon making process in the virtual environment, and reﬂection on such experiences. It aimed to assist the students in understanding the process and undertakings they will engage in as potters. In the “doing” activities, the students thought about and designed ceramic products in the context of a Smart Home by individually using the VR system, and collaborated with group members to reﬂect on their design works. The “learning-by-doing” activities can stimulate students to create their own products.

The VR learning environment consisted of a teaching area, and operation and discussion areas (shown in Figure 2). The teaching area consisted of LED displays, an electronic whiteboard and seats for students, which was mainly used for teaching knowledge, the arrangement of learning

The operation and discussion areas were equipped with VR apparatus and discussion tables, between which there was plenty of space for the students to use the VR devices. The discussion tables were used for group discussion and for drawing up the speciﬁ- cations of individual works.

There were eight sets of VR apparatus in the classroom, each consisting of a Head Mounted Display (HMD), two wireless controllers, a computer mainframe and a LED display. The HMDs had a 2.89 inch LED display, with a resolution of 1440*1440 pixels and a visual angle of 110°. Unlike mobile VR systems, the HMDs needed to be linked with external hosts, providing a more immersive experience. First, the apparatus allowed the participants to move and interact freely when carrying out the task. The headset weighed only 380 grams and featured an ergonomic design, which made it comfortable to wear. The user interacted with the objects in the learning scenario via optically positioned controllers in both hands (shown in Figure 3).

Second, the students were provided with a system to make ceramics in a virtual environment. The system was based on the Unity 3D game engine and an editor, which reproduced the technological process of Longquan celadon. The students were allowed to fully experience each processing step of

Third, the students were free to design their own products. The system provided a variety of digital tools to support them in designing diﬀerent product models, for example, the function of height, width and smoothness. In addition, pot cover, handle, spout, leaves and other tools were oﬀered by the software to inspire creative ideas and help the students make diﬀerent types of ceramics with various styles (shown in Figure 4).

The above VR-based approach is compared with conventional learning approaches, including a clay-based approach and a paper-and-pencil approach. The three approaches had the same learning ﬂow and the same opportunities to design, but diﬀerent ways of experiencing the pottery making and design. The clay-based approach used clay as the material to design or make pottery works in the class. Paper-and-pencil tools are frequently used when designing as they allow designers to draw more easily and freely than with digital tools (Bueno & Turkienicz, 2014). In this study, the paper-andpencil approach was adopted for students to design pottery works with color pencils. For the creative work, the clay or digital VR approach were 3D, while the paper-and-pencil approach was 2D.

Experiment design

To evaluate the eﬀects of the VR-based pottery-making approach, an experiment was conducted on the junior high school Labor and Technology class to perform a comparative study on the students’ creativity and learning engagement.

Participants

The participants of the experiment were 97 seventh graders from three classes of a junior high school in China. The students were equally distributed to the three classes according to their academic performance when they became seventh graders. In this study, the three classes were randomly assigned to the three groups: the VR experimental group, in which 34 students learned with the VR-based approach; the clay control group, in which 33 students learned with the conventional clay-based approach; and the paper-and-pencil control group, which consisted of students from the third class who learned with the paper-and-pencil approach. The students in all groups were randomly assigned to small teams of four to ﬁve students. Each group had the same teacher who had taught related courses for more than 5 years to organize the learning process, and the experimental group had two technicians to help the students use the VR system.

Discussion and conclusions

The potential of the VR-based approach for facilitating creativity

Using the VR-based approach here, students were able to experience the “observation-doing-reﬂection” process in the pottery making course, which was beneﬁcial for the quality of their individual creative products in all dimensions. The results of this study were consistent with the previous indication of technological potentials for facilitating creativity (Gabriel et al., 2016) and the ﬁndings that the 3D tools were more helpful in terms of providing a rich stimulus for creativity (Tano et al., 2003), and participants in the VR-based activity had higher quality products (Yang et al., 2018).

The potential of the “observation-doing-reﬂection” process for facilitating students’ product creativity in this study could be attributed to the immersive learning environment and the creative scaﬀolding for students made available via VR tools. On one hand, VR enables learners to better observe the whole ceramic technological process through full-body involvement and visualization (Freina & Ott, 2015; Gong et al., 2017). This would enhance students’ understanding, and their reﬂection on the experiences. On the other hand, the immersive environment and VR tools could be used to facilitate doing in a course. The immersive environment could be used to facilitate experiential learning tasks (Dalgarno & Lee, 2010), and participants in the more immersive system voluntarily spent more time on the learning tasks (Alhalabi, 2016). According to the students’ interview records, one student mentioned that “I was totally immersed in the activity, and I always felt that time passed quickly. I was very active and creative while having this kind of experience.” Such an immersive experience would help students generate product design ideas. The VR tools also provided scaﬀolding for the students’ design work. The students were able to easily design their products and make multiple attempts to redesign them. One student mentioned that “the function of adjusting width provided by the VR software allowed me to design a slim tube as an accessory of an intelligent humidiﬁer. So, with the adjustment my design made it more convenient to use without a reﬁll of water for a certain period of time. I think VR can inspire and broaden my mind.” In addition, with the help of the VR tools, it is easy to modify a design repeatedly, present a very realistic eﬀect, and promote students’ reﬂection while learning (Zhu & Li, 2013). By reﬂecting on prior experiences, students might come up with a new design idea and put it into action. The diﬃculty of redesigning and presenting the ideal appearance of their works with clay is one of the reasons why some students using clay presented very similar works and even works of lower quality compared to those using paper and pencil.

The potential of the VR-based approach for stimulating engagement

Engagement in learning stems from active, physical involvement (Stephen et al., 2008). The “observation-doing-reﬂection” active learning process would encourage students’ engagement. On the one hand, through the comparison of the three groups, we found that the VR-based approach was more beneﬁcial for cognitive engagement. Tano et al. (2003) showed that the 3D point of view and digital tools could help facilitate cognitive reﬂection. On the other hand, the students using the VR-based approach had signiﬁcantly higher engagement in emotional, behavioral and social dimensions than those using the paper-and-pencil approach. The ﬁndings supported the previous ﬁndings regarding VR technology, and this was attributed to its eﬀective learning environment and properties (Bulu, 2012; Dalgarno & Lee, 2010; Makransky & Lilleholt, 2018).

Existing research has shown that students tend to look favorably on VR solutions (Huang et al., 2010). VR oﬀers opportunities to increase intrinsic motivation and emotional engagement through students’ physical participation and natural interaction. Positive emotional engagement may positively inﬂuence cognitive resource allocation (Fredrickson & Branigan, 2005) and enhance learning outcomes (Plass et al., 2014), which is also the reason why a VR-based approach is more beneﬁcial for cognitive engagement. However, according to the Rosenthal Eﬀect, a new technology might be eﬀective in terms of stimulating students’ motivation at ﬁrst, but there would be a declining trend during the process. We therefore need more evidence by continuing to track the eﬀects of VR-based learning on students’ emotional engagement and its link to other learning performance.

The ﬁnding regarding behavioral engagement in this study indicated that students in the VR or clay condition could participate actively (Topu & Goktas, 2019). Colbert (1991) found that if materials facilitated expression and were developmentally appropriate, tasks signiﬁcantly enhanced children’s performance. Watkins et al. (2007) suggested that engaging pupils behaviorally involves them in actively using and creating materials. This may be one of the reasons why the students who had used VR or clay while “doing” showed more involvement in the learning activities. Regarding social engagement, the students with the paper-and-pencil approach engaged in less discussion based on the expression by materials than those using the other approaches. That is one of the reasons why the students learning with the paper-and-pencil approach had lower social engagement.

Conclusion and future studies

In this study, a VR-based pottery making approach was proposed for a Labor and Technology course. The most important ﬁnding was that the VR-based approach, which encompassed the “observationdoing-reﬂection” process, contributed to the students’ product creativity performance and cognitive engagement, which is attributed to the immersive learning environment and the usable VR tools. These ﬁndings provide more in-depth exploration of creativity and engagement of VR in K-12 classrooms.

However, there are several limitations of this study that should be noted. First, we asked students to design smart ceramics for a creativity task. Although signiﬁcant diﬀerences in all dimensions of creativity among the three groups were observed, multiple tasks could have helped better understand the creativity of the students who learned with the VR-based approach. Second, we analyzed the students’ engagement with the data from their self-reports. For this, we need the integration of multiple methods, including classroom observations to assess engagement (Wang & Degol, 2014). Fredricks et al. (2004) suggested that the interaction among cognitive, behavioral and emotional engagement had positive eﬀects on learning output. Therefore, more in-depth exploration of the relationship between the four dimensions of engagement in the VR-based approach is to be carried out in subsequent studies. Third, we provided VR software to help students design smart ceramics. Although the usable VR tools scaﬀolded the creative design process and gave students more chances to keep trying, more ﬂexible and diverse functions are needed to provide more room for creation.
