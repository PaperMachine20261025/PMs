Abstract

This study explored how pre-service TESOL teachers integrated GenAI tools into a Digital Multimodal Composing (DMC) project, composing a website, at a Hong Kong university. Speciﬁcally, it explored their decision-making and cognitive engagement in the DMC process when they had autonomy in the use of GenAI tools. Drawing on a social practice perspective of literacy, the research explores how GenAI supports or complicates multimodal composing and the professional learning outcomes for TESOL pre-service teachers. Data were collected through group and individual interviews with stimulated recall and analysis of group project websites. Findings reveal that all ten participating groups used GenAI tools, though in selective and strategic ways inﬂuenced by perceptions of tool efﬁciency, capabilities, authenticity, meaning-making, and desired degree of involvement. GenAI was employed both for creating multimodal content (e.g., images, videos, and summaries) and as a partner in the composing process (e.g., idea generation and feedback). The DMC project fostered creativity, digital literacy, and critical reﬂection, with participants reporting increased conﬁdence in multimodal and AI-assisted pedagogies. However, concerns about AI reliability and contextual constraints in future teaching environments were identiﬁed. The study highlights the importance of experiential learning, explicit guidance, and ethical awareness for integrating AI

INTRODUCTION T

he development of GenAI tools is disrupting contemporary composing processes (Godwin-Jones, 2024). A range of GenAI- supported literacy practices is emerging, compelling language teachers and learners to rethink composing in general and multimodal composing speciﬁcally. Consequently, it is increasingly important for language learners to develop AI, digital, and multimodal literacies to engage successfully in these new literacy practices (Jiang & Lai, 2025). However, to meet these needs and facilitate students’ development of these literacies, language teachers must develop expertise in GenAI- assisted, multimodal practices (Kohnke, Zou, Ou, & Gu, 2025). The integration of Digital Multimodal Composing (DMC) projects into TESOL teacher education programs, with their emphasis on combining multiple semiotic modes to create a meaningful multimodal text for a speciﬁc audience (Jiang, 2017), has been suggested as a way to facilitate language teachers’ literacy development (Jiang & Hafner, 2024).

GenAI tools both complicate and open new opportunities for DMC. GenAI-assisted DMC may reduce some barriers to implementing DMC by automating aspects of text composing (Jiang, 2024). At the same time, GenAI tools require speciﬁc competencies, for example, prompting skills, and learners need to be able to use the tools strategically (Moorhouse, 2024). Equally, depending on how they are used, GenAI tools may expand or constrain opportunities for learning and meaning-making within composing processes (Liu, Zhang, & Biebricher, 2024). Given the complex context, there is a need to understand how pre-service teachers use GenAI as part of the multimodal composing processes and the implications of this use for their professional learning. In particular, while there is a growing body of research examining teachers’ and learners’ GenAI use in TESOL (e.g., Kohnke et al., 2025; Warschauer et al., 2023), there is a need to better understand how pre-service teachers make agentive decisions about when, how, and for what purposes to integrate (or deliberately not integrate) GenAI when they have autonomy during a substantive DMC task.

Digital and Multimodal Literacies

In this study, we adopt a social practice approach to language and literacy education, one that aligns with the theoretical perspectives of the new literacy studies (New London Group, 1996; Street, 1984). According to this perspective, literacy is more than a cognitive process of encoding and decoding texts (Gee, 1989). Rather, literacy is a social practice enacted in various literacy events and involves the interpersonal and social processes that contribute to making meaning, building relationships, constructing identities, and critically understanding how power operates through positioning (Jones & Hafner, 2021). The concept of digital literacies aligns with this sociocultural view of literacy practices, focusing on the ways technological innovations and digital media create new contexts for social literacy practices (Liu, 2025; Weninger, 2023).

Scholars of language and literacy education have long argued that L2 learners need to develop multimodal and digital literacies in order to be able to engage with digital media through the interpretation and utilization of diverse semiotic resources (i.e., textual, aural, linguistic, spatial, and visual) to communicate and make meaning in their target language (Kress, 2003). AI-Assisted DMC

GenAI tools can generate a wide range of monomodal and multimodal content (e.g., images, videos, code, and audio) and perform human-like tasks, such as text summarization, translation, and sentiment analysis (Barrot, 2023; Moorhouse, 2024). In addition, “AI-assisted” features are being embedded in many digital tools utilized by language teachers and learners for multimodal composing (e.g., Canva). These developments have led many educators to speculate about their utility in language teaching (Pack & Maloney, 2024). Recently, there has been an increase in empirical studies exploring the integration of AI into TESOL (e.g., Kohnke et al., 2025; Warschauer et al., 2023). Most studies exploring the integration of GenAI tools offer promising results with reservations.

Regarding AI-assisted DMC in TESOL, there has been scant research. Scholars have speculated that multimodal GenAI can offer writers opportunities to engage more effectively in multimodal literacy practices (Kang & Kim, 2023). One study conducted by Jiang and Lai (2025) compared the composition processes and products of 14 GenAI-assisted groups and 10 non-GenAI-assisted groups that participated in a one-semester-long DMC project at a Hong Kong secondary school, in which L2 learners composed video projects. The researchers found that the groups that utilized GenAI within their DMC processes scored signiﬁcantly higher in the development of purpose, outline/ navigation skills (organizing in genre-appropriate stages) and their overall DMC outcomes, but had a similar performance to the non-AI group in the expression of base units (e.g., through linguistic or visual modes), layouts and rhetorical relationships. Jiang and Lai (2025) devised a taxonomy of four types of affordances of GenAI-assisted DMC over non-GenAI-assisted DMC: (1) generating and composing purpose-related ideas/topics; (2) constructing and composing outlines/navigation ﬂows; (3) generating scripts and feedback for customized writing; (4) offering a range of customized resources/ templates for special effects and personalization in DMC. Lin and colleagues, also in Hong Kong (Lin, Zhou, Li, & Sun, 2025), explored the use of GenAI as part of a 2-week-long DMC project in a Chineseas-a-second-language classroom. They found students used GenAI in ﬁve key areas: content generation, feedback and revision, multilingual support, critical thinking and visual representation.

AI-Assisted DMC and TESOL Teacher Education

There is a clear understanding that teachers need to recognize the beneﬁts of DMC and feel conﬁdent and competent to implement it in their classrooms (Jiang & Hafner, 2024). Therefore, effective incorporation of multimodal pedagogies into teacher education is essential (Li, 2020). It is likely that to get TESOL teachers “on board” with AI-assisted DMC, they should participate in a DMC project as part of their teacher preparation.

While there do not appear to be any studies conducted in TESOL teacher education on AI-assisted DMC, Smith, Shimizu, Burriss, Hundley, and Pendergrass (2025) examined the processes and perspectives of 21 pre-service teachers from a diversity of majors as they composed multimodal texts in a literacy education course. During a 3-hour class session in two different literacy educator courses, the students were tasked with retelling or remixing a text (e.g., a novel, play, or article) into a multimodal composition using GenAI. They found that, as well as using GenAI tools to create multimodal texts (songs, images), they also used them as “thought partners” that spurred new ideas. The pre-service teachers had mixed views on whether GenAI should be incorporated into the classroom. Those who had negative perceptions seemed concerned that GenAI might reduce opportunities for meaningful learning experiences. Smith and colleagues’ study offers valuable insights. Theoretical framing: GenAI as a mediational resource in social literacy practices. Consistent with the social practice view of literacy (New London Group, 1996; Street, 1984), we conceptualize GenAI not simply as a productivity tool but as a mediational resource that can shape how writers make meaning, design for audiences, and negotiate authorship and responsibility within situated composing practices (Godwin- Jones, 2024; Moorhouse, Wan, Wu, Wu, & Ho, 2025). In DMC, such mediation is evident in how participants orchestrate GenAI alongside other digital tools and semiotic resources to produce multimodal texts (e.g., websites) for particular audiences. In this study, we therefore focus on patterns of agentive decision-making about GenAI use (including selective uptake and deliberate restraint), the rationales that shape these decisions, and how participation in GenAI-assisted DMC contributes to TESOL pre-service teachers’ development of AI–multimodal–digital literacies and pedagogical reasoning for future practice.

In addition, central to this framing is the dual identity of many TESOL pre-service teachers, including the participants in our study (see Methodology section), as L2 users of English who are simultaneously preparing to become English-language teachers. This positioning means that, in addition to technical and compositional considerations, their decisions regarding the use of GenAI could be inﬂuenced by language and identity. For example, they may evaluate AI outputs as language (e.g., register, ﬂuency, accuracy, and intelligibility), negotiate questions of linguistic authority and ownership of English as L2 users themselves (Warschauer et al., 2023), and reason about the multimodal meaning-making practices they may later ask their own language learners to undertake.

Participants

During the DMC workshop, the pre-service teachers were given information about the research project and provided with a link to an instant messaging group where they could indicate their interest in joining the project. Of the 137 students in the course, 22 agreed to participate, representing ten group projects (see Supplementary Material S5 for the group project topics and list of group members who participated in each interview).

The participants included 19 females and 3 males. They ranged in age from 22 to 28. All participants originated from Mainland China. Most had no or limited teaching experience before joining the program. The vast majority planned to work as English-language teachers in Mainland China, Hong Kong, or Macau upon graduating. All participants were provided with an information sheet outlining the project details and their rights as participants. They all provided informed consent to participate.

Data Collection Tools

The study deployed two data collection methods: group / individual interviews with stimulated recall of the website composing processes, which were the primary method, and the collection of the ten groups’ websites, used as a secondary method. This combination allowed us to anchor participants’ accounts in concrete artifacts (their websites) while also eliciting rationales for tool-related decisions that are not directly observable from the ﬁnal product alone.

To facilitate the interviews, a protocol was developed. The ﬁrst author reviewed the relevant literature and developed a draft protocol, which was then shared with and discussed by the two other researchers

The stimulated recall component was split into two sections. In the ﬁrst instance, the participants were invited to use the screen-sharing function to show different aspects of their websites and talk through their composing process. After students shared their self-selected aspects of the website, the interviewer shared sections of the website identiﬁed as being of interest (e.g., including possible use of GenAI). This part allowed the researchers to understand better the decision-making that went into the composing processes (Gass & MacKey, 2017). These interviewer-selected prompts focused particularly on (a) multimodal elements where GenAI use was plausible (e.g., images, charts, summaries, scripts), and (b) genreand audience-facing design choices (e.g., information chunking, navigation, interactivity).

While participants were encouraged to interview in their project groups, due to availability, some groups were represented by one to four members. The participants were given the choice of English, Cantonese, or Mandarin for the interviews, with all selecting Mandarin. The interviews were conducted via video-conferencing software and lasted between 50 and 65 minutes. The interviews were conducted by the third author, who neither taught the course nor had prior relationships with the participants. We translated and transcribed the interview data (using machine translation and transcribing tools with human oversight, cross-checking, and veriﬁcation).

Website collection and pre-interview artifact review. The collected websites were analyzed prior to the interviews to facilitate stimulated recall. Speciﬁc aspects of the websites that relate to the interviews were extracted for analysis and to provide illustrative examples. Speciﬁcally, prior to each interview, the research team reviewed the group’s website and identiﬁed sections for potential follow-up during stimulated recall. These sections were selected because they (a) contained prominent multimodal elements (e.g., visuals, charts, embedded videos), (b) appeared to involve design choices that required explanation (e.g., navigation structure, interactive elements), or (c) plausibly involved GenAI-supported transformation (e.g., condensed summaries). During the interview, these excerpts were screen-shared to elicit participants’ accounts of the composing decisions that led to them.

Reasons for using and not using GenAI. To understand how TESOL pre-service teachers made decisions about GenAI use as part of the DMC process, it is important to examine their rationale for using or not using GenAI. Four reasons were identiﬁed: (1) Efﬁciency; (2) Functionality and capabilities; (3) Authenticity and creativity; (4) Degree of involvement.

Reason 1: Efﬁciency. Efﬁciency was a clear driver of the participants’ decision to use or not use GenAI tools for speciﬁc tasks. They selected tools and processes that were more convenient to them and cost less time to achieve their intended outcomes. This was especially evident in language-focused tasks. DISCUSSION

Situated within the social practice view of literacy (e.g., New London Group, 1996) that sees literacy as a set of socially situated practices, the study set out to explore TESOL pre-service teachers’ utilization of GenAI as they engaged in a DMC project when they were given the choice to use these tools as part of their DMC process. The study was limited to one teacher education program in a Hong Kong university and relied primarily on participants’ self-reported data through interviews. Therefore, the ﬁndings should be interpreted accordingly. However, the study provides useful insights despite these limitations. Below, we discuss the ﬁndings from the perspectives of agentive decision-making and professional learning, using cognitive

Agentive Decision-Making and Orchestration of GenAI in DMC

The ﬁndings show that GenAI tools can be integrated into various aspects of the DMC process (see Table 3 for a summary). GenAI tools were used to create and generate various kinds of multimodal representations that formed parts of the website (e.g., images, videos, graphs, and summaries), and consulted as partners that the teachers used to seek ideas, advice and recommendations, and feedback and improvement suggestions on content they had created. Similar GenAI uses have been found in other studies. However, in previous studies, participants were required to use GenAI (e.g., Smith et al., 2025), or researchers employed an experimental design (e.g., Jiang & Lai, 2025). Our study shows that in an authentic, naturalistic setting where TESOL pre-service teachers were given agency to use GenAI or not, they chose to use GenAI tools for speciﬁc tasks. This extends prior work by showing how the use of GenAI emerges as selective orchestration within a teacher-facing DMC website genre, rather than as a uniform or mandated practice.

By exploring individual groups’ reported uses of GenAI, we can better understand the factors that shaped the decisions to use GenAI. We can see that some groups integrated GenAI tools across various aspects of the process and product, while others used them more narrowly. Using GenAI tools as a partner was common across the groups. These ﬁndings support previous studies that found that GenAI is integrated into learners’ composing processes (e.g., Kang & Kim, 2023; Moorhouse et al., 2025). In terms of creation, GenAI tools were used for image generation, text creation and text summarization. However, video generation and coding were less common. The decision to use these tools was inﬂuenced by their perceived efﬁciency and ability to support the website’s composition. For example, the pre-service teachers reported that these tools were more effective and easier to use for text-based written tasks than for video generation. The ability of these tools to generate, revise, and summarize written text was considered a more mature capability than video generation. Importantly, these decisions were not just about evaluations of “better” or “worse” tools. From a social practice perspective, participants’ rationales (efﬁ- ciency, capability, authenticity, and degree of involvement) reﬂect how they negotiated the division of composing responsibilities, audience

Central to their decision to use tools was the DMC project itself. Rather than using these tools to automate every process, the preservice teachers prioritized meaning-making, actively assessing whether using the tools would help them achieve their intended aims. For example, participants understood that websites should avoid large blocks of text and that information should be presented in a multimodal, reader-friendly way. This led them to use GenAI tools to create graphs and charts and to assist them in summarizing the information for a more accessible presentation on their sites. At the same time, they recognized the need for the “human touch” and aimed to ensure human presence on the site. For this reason, they deliberately chose not to use AI for certain aspects of their sites (e.g., videos, voice-overs, and letter to the reader). As with other studies that have explored DMC, the emphasis on combining semiotic modes to create meaningful multimodal texts for a speciﬁc audience (Jiang, 2017; Jiang & Hafner, 2024) stimulated the participants enough for them to use GenAI selectively and strategically. This highlights a TESOL-relevant point: participants’ GenAI decisions were mediated by the communicative purposes and genre demands of producing an accessible teacherfacing resource.

Building on work on cognitive engagement in GenAI-supported learning (Lo et al., 2024), our ﬁndings suggest that autonomy to orchestrate GenAI within a meaningful DMC task can create conditions for sustained engagement. The task required them to explore different tools, research how to use them, and engage in trial and error before integrating the tool into their DMC process. They were also continuously comparing the efﬁcacy and performance of the tools

Providing choice does not seem to lead to an overreliance on GenAI but rather to a more conscious and reﬂective use of GenAI, making agentive decisions based on the affordances, constraints, and social meanings of these technologies in their educational context (Godwin-Jones, 2024). Furthermore, AI reduced some of the technical challenges of DMC, allowing them to focus on meaning-making (Jiang, 2024) and maintain high involvement within the DMC process. The ﬁndings support calls for a more open stance toward the use of GenAI (Tan, Wang, & Xu, 2025). However, this should be combined with training in the use of these tools, as well as raising awareness of the complex issues involved in using GenAI as part of text-composing processes, e.g., audience perceptions, learning opportunities, and ethical issues (bias, copyright) (Moorhouse et al., 2025). For teacher education, this implies that scaffolding should not only introduce tools but also provide guidance on orchestration practices (e.g., when to delegate, how to verify, and how to preserve voice and audience connection) and on ethical decision-making.

Professional Learning and Future Practice

The pre-service TESOL teachers in the study had never engaged in a DMC project before, nor had they composed a website. They initially conceptualized website building as a complex task that language teachers could not do. However, their experience of the project changed this concept, with the teachers reporting that they learned transferable skills that they could use in their future teaching practice. As Li (2020) mentions, it is unlikely that TESOL teachers will integrate multimodal pedagogies into their teaching unless they have experienced it themselves. The opportunity to work in groups and review other groups’ websites allowed them to learn collaboratively. We found that the participants took an experiential approach to using GenAI tools. They also used external resources to learn more about GenAI and DMC. Given the wide range of GenAI tools and their distinct affordances and interfaces, there is a clear need for users to trial different tools and become familiar with their functionalities (Kohnke et al., 2025; Moorhouse & Wong, 2025). Similarly, engaging external resources, particularly social media, can provide TESOL teachers with useful advice to assist them in using GenAI tools. Through their self-exploration, they became more aware of potential accuracy and performance issues with GenAI (e.g., bias, inaccuracies, and spelling errors). This led the participants to adopt a more cautious approach, affecting the tools they used and the tasks they worked on with GenAI. By engaging in critical evaluation and ethical use of GenAI during their DMC process, the participants demonstrated awareness of the issues inherent in AI tools, questioned the authority of AI-generated content to ensure accuracy, and reﬂected on the implications for authenticity and audience engagement (Darvin, 2025). This suggests that GenAI-assisted DMC can function as a situated context for developing critical AI literacy alongside multimodal composing competence, particularly when the output is a teacher-facing resource that requires credibility and accessibility.

We were encouraged to ﬁnd that the participants showed positive intentions to integrate AI and DMC into their future teaching practices. They saw utility in both AI and DMC in providing greater opportunities for language learning and increasing their future students’ AI and multimodal literacies—skills they identiﬁed as essential for modern society. Notably, their reasoning was oriented speciﬁcally toward language-learning outcomes, such as developing learners’ oral expression and integrating language skills into meaningful multimodal tasks. This reﬂects the language-pedagogical lens through which they appraised both GenAI and DMC. While most felt that composing websites might be too large a project for their students, digital storytelling, poster design, and video creation were all suggested as possibilities. These positive ﬁndings support calls for greater attention to AI and DMC in TESOL teacher education, especially if we wish for GenAI- assisted DMC and other multimodal pedagogies to be integrated into language teaching (Jiang & Hafner, 2024; Jiang & Lai, 2025).

CONCLUSION

The study provided valuable insights into how pre-service TESOL teachers integrate GenAI into the DMC processes in an authentic, naturalistic environment. The study showed that the decision to use AI was mediated by a range of factors, with a commitment to creating a socially relevant website being key. In summary, the study showed that GenAI does not replace the social dimensions of literacy; rather, it is a site for the negotiation of meaning and agency. The pre-service teachers in our study appropriated GenAI as a resource within a broader ecology of social, technological, and pedagogical practices— thereby showing that the social practice view of literacy also encompasses the complexities and opportunities of the GenAI era.

By focusing on decision-making patterns under conditions of autonomy, the study extends TESOL research beyond tool adoption to demonstrate how pre-service teachers selectively orchestrate GenAI in relation to audience design, perceived tool capabilities, authenticity, and responsibility. We believe that by integrating GenAI-assisted DMC into teaching and learning for pre-service TESOL teachers, teacher education programs can enrich their curricula while providing their students with awareness of multimodal pedagogies well-suited to the GenAI age (Jiang & Lai, 2025). The project described in this article can serve as a reference for other TESOL education programs. Future work could examine how such orchestration practices develop over time and how they translate into classroom enactment. However, the present study demonstrates the value of embedding GenAI within substantive, audience-oriented DMC tasks to support the development of AI–multimodal–digital literacies and pedagogical reasoning in TESOL teacher education.
