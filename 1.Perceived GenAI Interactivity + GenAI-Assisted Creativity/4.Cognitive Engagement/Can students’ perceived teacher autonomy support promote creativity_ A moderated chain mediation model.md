(2021) assert that the potency of creativity is both evident and irrefutable, serving as a fun­ damental force propelling civilisation ahead, with humans capable of possessing and expressing it to varying extents at all levels. However, students’ independent thinking skills remain underdeveloped, and their creativity remains latent due to the influence of exam-oriented education and tradi­ tional teaching models, which excessively emphasise rote memorization (Wu et al., 2014). Additionally, teachers, fac­ ing intense instructional pressures, often prioritize knowl­ edge transmission while paying insufficient attention to supporting students’ autonomy, further hindering the devel­ opment of creativity (Wang & Greenwood, 2013). Abstract The objective of this study is to examine the mechanism by which teachers’ autonomy support influences student creativ­ ity. A relationship model comprising five variables—teacher autonomy support, positive emotion, creativity self-efficacy, academic engagement, and creativity—was developed based on the ecosystem theory, self-determination theory, and social cognitive theory. A survey was administered to 637 undergraduate students in China. The study found that teacher autonomy support significantly enhances creativity, with creativity self-efficacy and academic engagement partially medi­ ating the relationship between teacher autonomy support and creativity and producing a chain mediation effect between the two variables. Positive emotions, as a moderating variable, favourably influenced all mediation interactions. The find­ ings extend the explanatory power of three theories on the relationship between teacher autonomy support and creativity, offering fresh insights and theoretical references to foster students’ creative development.

The effect of teacher autonomy support on creativity

Teacher autonomy support denotes a student-centred meth­ odology wherein educators facilitate teaching and learn­ ing activities and offer knowledge on resources and varied options while concurrently reducing their control and pres­ sure on students (Deci & Ryan, 2000). Self-determination theory (SDT) is an empirically derived framework that explains human motivation and personality within social contexts, emphasising the influence of environmental fac­ tors on individuals’ motivation, behaviour, and personal­ ity development(Deci & Ryan, 2012). SDT posits that an autonomy-supportive environment provided by a teacher fulfils students’ demands for autonomy, competence, and relevance (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Specifically, when teach­ ers support students’ autonomous learning through three distinct forms of guidance—providing relevant informa­ tion, assisting in goal-setting and strategy selection, and engaging in the evaluation of learning outcomes—they cre­ ate a more conducive environment for fostering creativity (Morris, 2020). Individuals in autonomy-supportive situa­ tions enjoy greater freedom of expression and action, make decisions that are more likely to be validated, and experi­ ence enhanced autonomy (Deci, 1991; Reeve et al., 1999). SDT has consistently acknowledged that autonomy support inside the classroom can effectively improve student learn­ ing, resulting in elevated success and skill development (Deci & Ryan, 2013; Reeve et al., 2002). Sheldon (1995) asserted that persons capable of making self-determined decisions may genuinely articulate their instincts and pos­ sess enhanced cognitive resources for fostering creativity. In conclusion, teacher autonomy support likely enhances cre­ ativity. The internal mechanism by which teacher autonomy support influences creativity remains unclear, representing a significant topic to be addressed in this study.

Educators provide several supportive actions to students, such as facilitating independent learning, establishing high expectations, delivering immediate feedback, and present­ ing complex, engaging, and significant learning activities (Fredricks et al., 2016). Effective autonomy support in the classroom depends on both teachers’ efforts and stu­ dents’ perception because a mismatch between autonomy and perceived support may undermine student engagement and participatory behaviour (Engels et al., 2019; Hagger et al., 2007). Ma (2021) explains that teachers’ autonomy support fulfils students’ needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness, thereby fostering greater academic engage­ ment. Crucially, students’ autonomous learning behaviours enhance their creativity and foster more positive emotions and greater academic effort (Reeve, 2006; Reeve & Jang, 2006). Furthermore, the impact of teacher autonomy support on students’ academic engagement is influenced by individ­ ual factors, particularly self-efficacy (Gong & Xu, 2024). Notably, creative self-efficacy is a derivative construct of self-efficacy. In light of this, educators and researchers have increasingly recognised the importance of creative selfefficacy in maximising the educational environment’s role in fostering creativity (Liu et al., 2021). Therefore, in addi­ tion to examining the impact of teacher autonomy support on creativity, this study incorporates creative self-efficacy, academic engagement, and students’ emotions into the research framework. By expanding the scope of inquiry into the interplay among creativity-related variables, this study aims to offer more targeted insights and practically relevant educational recommendations.

The mediating role of creativity self-efficacy

Creativity self-efficacy denotes the conviction in an indi­ vidual’s capacity to produce innovative thoughts or behav­ iours (Tierney & Farmer, 2002). With the depth of research related to creativity, creativity self-efficacy is considered a derivative concept of self-efficacy, which has been dem­ onstrated to enhance persistence, effort, and creative per­ formance in creative endeavours (Liu et al., 2021; Tierney & Farmer, 2004). Individuals with elevated creativity selfefficacy view challenges as opportunities and demonstrate perseverance (Newman et al., 2018). Chain mediation of creativity self-efficacy and academic engagement

Creativity self-efficacy is typically linked to educational research since it indicates students’ confidence in execut­ ing creative behaviours during the learning process (Hung, 2018; Smith, 2022). Engagement in the creative process is regarded as a creative activity that affects creative selfefficacy and personal creativity (Zhang & Bartol, 2010). Preliminary studies indicate that individuals exhibiting elevated creative self-efficacy demonstrate greater motiva­ tion to persist academically in their disciplines and are more inclined to participate in academic endeavours (Beghetto, 2006). Empirical evidence suggests that enhancing stu­ dents’ creative self-efficacy through effective teaching strat­ egies and learning experiences can significantly promote their academic development and engagement (Green & Riz­ wan, 2024). Currently, there is a paucity of understanding regarding the individual-level positive effects of creativity self-efficacy, and scant evidence exists to support a positive correlation between creativity self-efficacy and creativity (Li et al., 2021; Ng & Lucianetti, 2016; Tierney & Farmer, 2011). A study grounded in the ecological theory perspec­ tive suggests that the impact of students’ perceived teacher autonomy support on their learning engagement may be conditional and is likely to be moderated by the key fac­ tor of self-efficacy (Gong & Xu, 2024). In conclusion, for students, creativity self-efficacy could be a crucial predictor of academic engagement; however, a more comprehensive examination of creativity self-efficacy antecedents (teacher autonomy support) and consequences (academic engage­ ment) is required in this study.

The mediating role of academic engagement

Academic engagement refers to the extent of students’ invest­ ment in the learning environment and their motivation to learn (González & Paoloni, 2015). Engagement is regarded as an appealing term because of its significant responsive­ ness and adaptability to alterations in teaching practices (Fredricks et al., 2016). A primary aspect of enhancing stu­ dent engagement within a learning environment is teacher autonomy support (Yang et al., 2022). According to SDT, when the social environment meets students’ basic psycho­ logical needs, their intrinsic motivational foundation for growth is then established, and naturally, students’ academic engagement is enhanced (Reeve, 2012; Ryan & Deci, 2000). A study based on SDT revealed that instructors’ autonomy support facilitates the connection between motivation and psychological needs, hence enhancing academic engage­ ment (Dewey, 2024; Wang et al., 2017). Simultaneously, educators who offer autonomous support to students might assist them in developing internal motivational resources, enhancing their motivation to engage in learning (Reeve, 2009; Yang et al., 2022). Active student engagement in ben­ eficial learning methods, coupled with a willingness to con­ front new knowledge and implement changes, indicates the initiation of creative activity (Beghetto & Schreiber, 2017). Current research

This study explores the potential mechanisms through which teacher autonomy support influences creativity, revealing that teacher autonomy support serves as a key antecedent of creative self-efficacy, academic engagement, and creativ­ ity, underscoring its critical role in fostering these variables. Although numerous studies have explored the anteced­ ents and consequences of creativity among undergraduate students, however, existing research lacks an integrated framework that examines the mechanism by which teacher autonomy support influences students’ creativity, incorpo­ rating creative self-efficacy and academic engagement as mediating variables and positive emotions as a moderating factor. Therefore, this study is grounded in Ecological Sys­ tems Theory, Self-Determination Theory, and Social Cogni­ tive Theory to establish a comprehensive understanding of the relationship between teacher autonomy support and cre­ ativity. Participants and procedures

The Ethics Committee and the university president approved this study before the formal data collection, and all partici­ pants provided informed consent. This study employed ran­ dom sampling, yielding 698 questionnaires from Chinese university students, facilitated by pertinent researchers. Due to response patterns, incomplete background information, and omitted questions, 56 questionnaires were excluded, resulting in 637 valid questionnaires (91.26%). Out of 637 surveys, 271 respondents were male (42.54%), and 366 were female (57.46%), with ages ranging from 17 to 24 years.

Measures

Teacher autonomy support

The assessment of individuals’ perceived support for teacher autonomy utilised a scale created by Williams and Deci (1996), comprising six questions rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Elevated total scores on the six questions signify the more teacher autonomy support an individual receives. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.91, CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.98, and AGFI = 0.96.

Positive emotions

The assessment of people’s positive emotions was conducted using the emotion scale created by Watson et al. (1988), spe­ cifically employing the Positive Emotions subscale as the measurement tool for this investigation. The measure has 10 questions rated from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree), with a higher cumulative score indicating the more positive emotions an individual has. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in this study was 0.95, CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.96, AGFI = 0.92.

Creativity self-efficacy

The creativity self-efficacy of individuals was assessed using a scale modified by Carmeli and Schaubroeck (2007), comprising eight items rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). A higher cumulative score across the eight items signifies greater creativity and self-efficacy in the individual. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in this study was 0.91, CFI = 0.98,TLI = 0.97,AGFI = 0.95.

Academic engagement of individuals was assessed using a scale created by Schreiner and Louis (2011), with 10 questions rated from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). The statements ‘It is hard to pay attention in many of my classes’ and ‘In the last week, I’ve been bored in class a lot of the time’ require reverse scoring to calculate a total score, where higher scores signify greater academic engage­ ment. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in this study was 0.94, CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, AGFI = 0.94.

Creativity

The creativity of individuals was assessed using a scale devised by Miller and Dumford (2016), comprising seven questions rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). A higher cumulative score on these questions sig­ nifies greater creative ability in the individual. The Cron­ bach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in this study was 0.89, CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, AGFI = 0.95.

Test for common method bias

To mitigate frequent method bias, coded anonymous evalu­ ations were employed throughout the administration to sys­ tematically regulate sources of such bias. All test items were analysed via Harman’s one-way analysis of variance. The findings indicated five variables with eigenvalues exceeding 1, and the total variance of the most explanatory component was 39.26%, which is below 40% (Harman, 1976). Conse­ quently, it is acceptable that this study suffers from common methodological bias.

Discussion

The relationship between teacher autonomy support and creativity

The study revealed that teacher autonomy support consider­ ably and positively influenced the growth of students’ cre­ ativity, hence validating research hypothesis 1. This finding aligns with prior studies regarding the influence of teacher support on student creativity (Amabile, 2018; Soh, 2017), although it is distinct in that the current study focused spe­ cifically on the type of support that teachers provide to stu­ dents that is more autonomous. This discovery enhances the explanatory capacity of Self-Determination Theory concerning the influence of teacher autonomy support on creativity. By granting students autonomy in expression and decision-making, educators enable children to articulate their intrinsic motivations in more independent settings and use more profound cognitive resources to foster creativity (Deci, 1991; Reeve et al., 1999; Sheldon, 1995). The under­ lying rationale is that students who perceive support from teacher autonomy gain from their needs for autonomy, com­ petence, and relevance in the educational process (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Consequently, educators should prioritise cre­ ating a secure and nurturing environment, enhancing diverse pedagogical approaches, and genuinely transforming their instructional methods into a vital avenue for cultivating stu­ dent creativity (Mostafavi et al., 2020; Salmanpour et al., 2024).

The results demonstrate that creative self-efficacy and academic engagement partially mediate the relationship between teacher autonomy support and creativity, thereby confirming hypotheses H2 and H3. This suggests that teacher autonomy support influences students’ creativity development via their creative self-efficacy and enhances creativity through increased academic engagement. The aforementioned results substantiate the explanatory capac­ ity of the ecosystem theory, positing that the cultivation and evolution of students’ creativity depend on their learning experiences through interactions with the environment and symbols that integrate individual and environmental factors (Bronfenbrenner, 1994). Teacher autonomy support initi­ ates a sequence of learning feedback from pupils that fosters creativity. The findings of this study indicate that students’ perception of teacher autonomy support influences their creative growth via the mediating effects of creativity selfefficacy and academic engagement.

According to the SCT interpretation, educators influ­ ence students’ learning behaviours by offering autonomy support, while self-efficacy serves as a crucial determinant of individual decision-making and actions (Schunk, 1985). Creative self-efficacy, a subset of self-efficacy (Tierney & Farmer, 2004), empowers individuals to surmount chal­ lenges without trepidation, cultivate intrinsic motivational resources, and engage in autonomous learning with support from educators (Reeve, 2009; Yang et al., 2022). Research indicates that students in classroom settings demonstrate self-efficacy, a significant internal resource, enhancing their confidence in managing problems, hence fostering learning engagement (Martin & Rimm-Kaufman, 2015). The afore­ mentioned findings implicitly corroborate research hypoth­ esis 4, which posits that students with heightened creative self-efficacy exhibit more academic engagement, a conclu­ sion endorsed by Beghetto (2006).

Moreover, elevated creative self-efficacy and engage­ ment facilitate the attainment of favourable creative out­ comes; conversely, diminished creative self-efficacy hampers an individual’s ability to derive pleasure from the creative process and reduces their willingness to invest the necessary time and effort to cultivate creativity (Li et al., 2021). Similarly, individuals must perceive creative engage­ ment as a type of creative effort to comprehend the creative process (Zhang & Bartol, 2010). Active and robust aca­ demic engagement is a crucial determinant in fostering and actualising creativity (Yuan et al., 2019). The moderating effect of positive emotions

The study found that positive emotions played a moderating role between teacher autonomy support and creativity selfefficacy, thus validating the research hypothesis H5. Spe­ cifically, an increase in positive emotions among students correlates with a larger positive prediction of teacher auton­ omy support on creativity self-efficacy, aligning with prior research findings. When students recognise teacher auton­ omy support, their positive feelings foster enjoyment of the learning experience and enhance their beliefs in reaching educational objectives (Oriol-Granado et al., 2017a, b). Stu­ dents exhibit heightened motivation in learning when they regard teacher autonomy support as a valuable resource, which enhances their self-efficacy and elevates their aca­ demic expectations (Chen et al., 2015; Sakiz et al., 2012; Yu & Singh, 2018).

Additionally, the study found that high levels of positive emotions were shown to more significantly moderate the chain mediation between creative self-efficacy and academic engagement in teacher autonomy support and creativity than low levels of positive emotions. This may be attributed to the fact that students experience more confidence in task completion when they have more positive emotions, while the presence of negative emotions correlates with a higher incidence of failures (Schutz & Lee, 2014). High levels of positive emotions enhance motivation, engagement, and persistence in students’ learning, whereas low levels may impede their efforts, particularly when they do not meet academic achievement expectations (Sadoughi & Hejazi, 2021). Consequently, emphasising the significance of posi­ tive emotions can yield a more thorough comprehension of the mechanisms through which teacher autonomy support influences creativity, thereby offering a more advantageous framework for fostering students’ creative development.

Conclusion

This study, grounded in ecological systems theory, selfdetermination theory, and social cognitive theory, substanti­ ates the chain-mediated role of creativity self-efficacy and academic engagement in the relationship between teacher autonomy support and creativity, representing a preliminary effort to connect these variables and enhance the existing literature. Examining the chain mediation model clarifies the influence of teacher autonomy support on students’ cre­ ativity and growth via creative self-efficacy and academic engagement. In practice, when educators seek to augment students’ creativity, they must cultivate a more autonomysupportive environment and motivate students to foster academic engagement by boosting their creativity selfefficacy. Moreover, while fostering autonomy-supportive learning environments, educators must focus on eliciting students’ positive emotions to facilitate their development and engagement in creativity-related curricular activities, thereby genuinely enhancing their creative growth.
