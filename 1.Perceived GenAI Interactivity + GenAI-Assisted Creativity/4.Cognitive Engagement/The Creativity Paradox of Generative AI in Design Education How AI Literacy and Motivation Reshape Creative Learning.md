ABSTRACT Generative artificial intelligence (GAI) is quickly changing the foundations of design education. It alters how creativity is under­ stood, created and judged in higher education. This issue is especially important in design fields, where creativity involves not just productivity and fast idea generation but also the ability to handle uncertainty, engage in reflective experimentation, and think independently. In this context, the current study examined the relationships among teachers' acceptance of AI, their AI lit­ eracy, students' motivation to learn, and creativity in AI-­supported design education at Chinese universities. The research gath­ ered data from randomly sampled teachers and students at 10 universities known for their art and design programmes, which include visual communication, industrial design, digital media, animation and environmental design. This study contributes to the growing research on AI in design education by shifting the focus from celebrating technological advances to a more critical perspective on how humans interact with AI in creative contexts. The findings suggest that AI can both enhance and restrict creativity, depending on how it affects students' independence, cognitive engagement, and motivation. Thus, the future of design education involving AI should not only focus on technological complexity or adoption rates, but also on its ability to maintain human originality, reflective thought, and the uncertainty that is vital to creativity.

However, the increasing research on AI acceptance and knowledge reveals several important gaps. First, many studies mainly focus on students' views and intentions regarding AI, while less attention has been given to how teachers perceive AI acceptance and knowledge within specific academic fields (Börekci and Çelik 2024; Khoso et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025). Second, while frameworks for technology acceptance, such as TAM, UTAUT and TPACK, have provided useful insights into AI adoption behaviours, many studies are overly focused on the technology's functionality. They often neglect broader educational, ethical and creative aspects that arise when in­ tegrating AI into higher education (Chen and Feng  2025; Zhu et al. 2025; Jin and Rui 2026). Third, most existing lit­ erature fails to explore the connection between teachers' AI skills and students' emotional and creative learning outcomes, even though there is growing evidence that factors such as classroom innovation climates, teacher beliefs and teaching methods significantly influence students' creative engage­ ment and motivation (Jiang et al. 2025; Ocado 2025; Zhou and Peng 2025).

For these reasons, this study aimed to assess teachers' percep­ tions of AI acceptance and their knowledge of AI. It also ex­ amined how these factors relate to students' motivation and creativity among design students in Chinese universities. By focusing on design education in the Chinese higher education context, this study addresses calls for more nuanced and crit­ ical investigations into how GAI is integrated into education. Importantly, it seeks to go beyond simplistic views of technology to examine whether teachers' AI skills are essential to shaping students' motivation and creative experiences in AI-­enhanced design learning environments.

The country's rapidly grow­ ing digital education landscape and focus on innovation have increased the use of GAI in creative industries and university programmes, especially in art, design and visual communica­ tion. The relationship between generative artificial intelligence (GAI) and design education is significant. Thus, the connection between GAI and design education is meaningful because both areas cen­ tre on creative generation, interpretive flexibility, and the transla­ tion of abstract ideas into tangible forms (Wilhelmsen et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2025; Chen and Hung 2026). Unlike fields that focus on fixed procedures, design education relies on imaginative explo­ ration, aesthetic interpretation, and conceptual ambiguity. This makes GAI particularly influential in creative learning settings where success is defined by multiple solutions instead of single an­ swers (Lee 2025; Liu et al. 2025). GAI's role in design education is linked to its ability to externalize imagination and speed up visual thinking. With text-­to-­image systems, generative visualization tools, and AI-­assisted conceptual modelling, students can quickly turn abstract design ideas into visual prototypes. 2.2   |   The Association Between Teacher AI-­Acceptance and AI-­Literacy

Research suggests that GAI can significantly improve student engagement, confidence, and exploratory learning by reduc­ ing creative anxiety and enhancing quick idea visualization. Students who struggle to express their conceptual ideas visually may find AI-­assisted tools useful in guiding their creative pro­ cesses without the immediate worry of technical shortcomings (Fleischmann 2024; Lee 2025). In fields such as architecture, interior design, and product design, generative systems enable learners to explore a range of structural designs, aesthetic op­ tions, and spatial ideas that may be beyond reach with traditional studio methods (Liu et  al.  2025; Alamasi et  al.  2026). GAI-­ supported design settings have also shown promise in strength­ ening collaborative discussions, interdisciplinary thinking, and sustainable innovation by enabling students to interact dynam­ ically with visual and conceptual alternatives (Fathoni 2023; Li et  al.  2024). However, integrating GAI into design education also raises significant epistemological and ethical issues that challenge the core assumptions of creative learning. A key con­ cern is that GAI may commercialize creativity by turning deep artistic processes into algorithm-­driven production tasks.

The connection between teachers' acceptance of AI and their AI literacy is crucial for successfully integrating generative ar­ tificial intelligence (GAI) in higher education. This is especially true in fields that focus on creativity, innovation and complex problem-­solving. Recent studies view AI literacy not just as a narrow skill set, but as a broad ability encompassing critical evaluation, ethical awareness, adaptability in teaching, under­ standing algorithms and thoughtful judgement about technol­ ogy. At the same time, AI acceptance goes beyond simply having a positive view of technology. It includes teachers' perception of AI's usefulness in teaching, their professional trust, emotional readiness and willingness to incorporate AI into their teach­ ing methods. Research shows that these factors support each other rather than exist alone. Higher AI literacy tends to lead to greater acceptance and more meaningful use of AI in teaching (Yim and Wegerif 2024; Jin and Rui 2026; Lin et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2026).

The quick rise of GAI in education has heightened the gap be­ tween what technology promises and what educators are ready for. Universities seek innovative teaching methods enhanced by AI, yet many teachers feel unprepared to effectively manage, evaluate, and use AI tools in real classrooms. As a result, a lack of AI literacy often leads to anxiety about technology, ethical concerns, reduced confidence, and resistance to using AI. In contrast, better AI literacy can boost teachers' confidence, flex­ ibility, and willingness to experiment with new methods (Yao and Wang  2024; Börekci and Çelik  2024; Bilbao-­Eraña and Arroyo-­Sagasta 2025). In this context, AI acceptance is funda­ mentally about understanding how AI can aid or limit teaching practices. Studies on technology acceptance show that AI liter­ acy greatly influences how teachers view AI's usefulness and ease of use, as well as their intentions to adopt it. Teachers with a strong understanding of AI are more likely to see GAI as a tool to enhance their teaching rather than as a threat to their inde­ pendence or creativity (Jin and Rui 2026; Luo et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2025). On the other hand, those with limited literacy often worry about issues such as originality, academic honesty, and reliance on algorithms, which can undermine their acceptance and hinder innovation in teaching (Bartlett and Camba 2024; Jasmer 2024; Zhu et al. 2025).

This concern becomes even more urgent because generative sys­ tems usually rely on predicting and recombining existing visual patterns rather than on genuine originality. Therefore, students might unconsciously adopt the standardized aesthetic styles pres­ ent in the training data, which could limit their divergent thinking and lead to repetitive creative outcomes (Bartlett and Camba 2024; Pektaş 2025). From a teaching perspective, this creates a conflict where tools meant to foster creativity may also restrict original thinking if students depend too much on machine-­generated in­ spiration. Thus, the challenge for design education is not whether GAI boosts productivity, but whether it nurtures true creative thinking or merely speeds up the process of creating visually ap­ pealing work. The role of teachers in AI-­driven design education is becoming increasingly complex and demanding. Traditional design teaching has relied on critique-­based approaches, mentor­ ship, discussions, and process-­focused evaluations. However, GAI complicates these structures as students can now produce sophis­ ticated visuals with little technical difficulty. This complicates the assessment of originality, skill development and authentic au­ thorship (Ratum 2025; Bartlett and Camba 2024). Educators find it increasingly challenging to distinguish between students' inde­ pendent contributions and AI-­assisted creations. This generates considerable uncertainty about the validity of assessments, aca­ demic integrity, and the measurement of genuine creative skills in AI-­supported design contexts. 3   |   Research Method

The current study used a quantitative cross-­sectional design to examine the relationship between teachers' acceptance of AI and students' creativity. It looked at how AI literacy and learn­ ing motivation influenced this relationship among students in design-­related fields at Chinese universities. A cross-­sectional

approach was appropriate, as the study aimed to examine the connections among psychological, teaching, and technology-­ related factors in a real educational setting at a single point in time. This design allowed for the evaluation of both direct and indirect pathways among the underlying variables using struc­ tural equation modelling (SEM). SEM is especially good for test­ ing intricate mediation mechanisms and well-­founded causal ideas in educational technology research.

3.1   |   Participants and Research Setting

The current study was conducted across 10 Chinese universi­ ties known for their strong art and design programmes at the bachelor's, master's and doctoral levels. These universities were chosen because of their focus on creative fields and their grow­ ing use of digital technologies, artificial intelligence (AI), and AI-­supported teaching practices. The selected institutions of­ fered programmes in visual communication, industrial design, digital media, animation, creative arts, environmental design, and other related areas. This variety provided a suitable setting for exploring AI-­related educational experiences in design ed­ ucation. The study used random sampling within the defined target population to ensure that all eligible participants had an equal chance of being chosen from the participating institu­ tions. The target population included students and teachers in design-­related programmes with experience in AI-­supported educational environments. Participants were selected from this population using a random selection process based on in­ stitutional records. This method ensured that sampling did not rely on the researcher's choice or voluntary self-­selection. After obtaining ethical approval from the Shenzhen university's re­ search ethics committee, sampled teachers and students were required to provide written informed consent.

Participants were contacted through institutional coordina­ tion channels and academic networks, which served as struc­ tured ways to reach the defined sampling frame, not as the sampling method itself. These channels made it easier to com­ municate with randomly selected eligible participants across various departments and institutions. Inclusion criteria were set to ensure contextual relevance: students had to be enrolled in design-­related programmes with previous exposure to AI-­ assisted learning tools or generative AI, while teachers needed experience in teaching design-­related subjects and familiarity with AI-­supported educational technologies. These criteria ensured that all selected participants had enough background to respond meaningfully to the study variables. By randomly selecting participants from multiple institutions, academic levels, and professional backgrounds, the study improved the representativeness and diversity of the sample. This approach enhanced the ability to explore the relationships among AI acceptance, AI literacy, learning motivation, and creativ­ ity within the context of Chinese higher education design programmes.

3.2   |   Measurements

3.2.1   |   Teachers' AI Acceptance Scale

Teachers' acceptance of AI was measured using a modified ver­ sion of the Technology Acceptance Model developed by Fred Davis. The scale included five areas: perceived ease of use, per­ ceived usefulness, perceived trust, AI literacy, and intelligent technological pedagogical content knowledge (iTPACK), along with behavioural intention toward GenAI acceptance. The tool contained items examining teachers' views on the usability, ef­ fectiveness, trustworthiness, pedagogical integration, and fu­ ture use of generative AI tools in teaching.

3.2.1.2   |   Perceived Usefulness (PU).  Perceived useful­ ness is defined as the extent to which teachers believe that using GenAI will enhance their job performance, teaching effective­ ness and productivity.

3.2.1.3   |   Perceived Trust.  Teachers' confidence in the reli­ ability, safety, ethical integrity and accuracy of GenAI systems.

3.2.1.4   |   Attitude Toward AI.  It refers to teachers' knowl­ edge, understanding, and ability to effectively interact with AI systems, including GenAI tools, which enables teachers to make informed decisions about integrating AI into pedagogy and reduces fear or misunderstanding of AI technologies.

3.2.1.5   |   Behavioural Intention Toward GenAI Accep­ tance.  In TAM, behavioural intention is the immediate antecedent of actual system use and is shaped by perceived use­ fulness and perceived ease of use (Davis 1989). It represents a psychological commitment to adopting the technology.

Sample items included ‘Learning how to use GenAI tools is easy for me’ and ‘I plan to use GenAI tools in my future teaching’. The instrument was adapted from earlier studies on technology acceptance and AI integration in education, drawing on the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis 1989) and the TPACK-­related AI integration literature (Mishra and Koehler 2006). The scale's validity was tested using confirma­ tory factor analysis. The results confirmed the scale's factor structure (x2 = 2.63, RMSEA = 0.078, NFI = 0.92, IFI = 0.95, CFI = 0.95). The overall Cronbach's alpha for the scale's inter­ nal consistency is 0.94. The internal consistency coefficients for its subscales range from 0.87 to 0.95. The scale calculates the total score. Higher scores suggest increased acceptance of AI.

3.2.2   |   AI Literacy Scale

Students' AI literacy was assessed using a modified AI literacy competency tool derived from previous educational technology studies on AI literacy and the TPACK framework. The scale contained four dimensions: content knowledge (CK), peda­ gogical content knowledge (PCK), technological pedagogical content knowledge (TPACK), and technological content knowl­ edge (TCK).

3.2.2.1   |   Content Knowledge (CK).  Refers to an individ­ ual's foundational understanding of AI-­related concepts, prin­ ciples and applications within a specific domain. In educational contexts, this includes knowledge of what AI is, how AI systems function, their capabilities and limitations, ethical consider­ ations, and their relevance to disciplinary learning (Mishra and Koehler 2006).

3.2.2.3   |   Technological Content Knowledge (TCK).  This dimension reflects the ability to recognize how AI tools can transform, support, or reshape content-­specific learning, inquiry and knowledge production. In educational settings, TCK involves understanding which AI technologies are most appropriate for particular subject areas, how AI can support content exploration and problem-­solving, and how technological tools influence the representation and con­ struction of knowledge within different disciplines (Mishra and Koehler 2006).

3.2.2.4   |   Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK).  This dimension involves understanding how to meaningfully incorporate AI into teaching and learning pro­ cesses while maintaining alignment with learning objectives, instructional strategies, and student needs (Koehler et al. 2013). Within AI literacy research, TPACK is increasingly viewed as a critical competency because it reflects the capacity to move beyond simple AI use toward informed, ethical, and pedagogi­ cally purposeful AI integration.

The instrument evaluated participants' understanding of AI concepts, computational thinking, teaching strategies for AI learning, and practical skills in using AI tools such as Teachable Machine, Scratch, and generative AI systems. Example items included ‘I have sufficient knowledge of AI’ and ‘I can design and teach lessons that effectively combine AI, technologies, and teaching methods’. The instrument was adapted from the TPACK theory proposed by Punya Mishra and Matthew J. Koehler, along with current AI liter­ acy frameworks in education. The scale's validity was tested using confirmatory factor analysis. The factor structure of the AI literacy scale was confirmed (x2 = 2.11, RMSEA = 0.067, IFI = 0.97, CFI = 0.97). The overall Cronbach's alpha for inter­ nal consistency of the scale is 0.97. The internal consistency of its subscales ranges from 0.87 to 0.96. The scale is used to calculate the total score. Higher scores indicate a greater ac­ ceptance level of AI literacy.

3.2.4   |   Creativity Scale

Students' creativity was evaluated using a modified product-­ based creativity scale. This scale was adapted from established frameworks created by Stein in 1953 and later expanded in de­ sign and product creativity research. Unlike self-­efficacy or self-­ perception measures, this tool specifically assesses the creative qualities of finished design outputs through structured semantic criteria.

Participants' design products were rated using a 15-­item se­ mantic differential scale. Trained raters assessed each product along bipolar adjective dimensions that capture key indicators of creative performance. These dimensions included originality, usefulness, elaboration, refinement, organization and innova­ tion, which are widely acknowledged in creativity research as key indicators of both novelty and appropriateness in creative work. Each item received a rating on a five-­point semantic dif­ ferential scale, featuring opposing descriptors (e.g., Ordinary-­ Original, Conventional-­Innovative, Basic-­Well-­crafted). Higher scores indicated greater perceived creativity, including original­ ity, technical refinement and design innovation. This measure was not designed to capture general self-­assessments of cre­ ativity. Instead, it aimed to define creativity as an observable product-­level construct. This approach is consistent with estab­ lished methods in design education research, where creative performance is typically assessed through structured ratings of artefacts based on novelty and usefulness criteria. Prior studies suggest that design creativity is best viewed as a multidimen­ sional product attribute rather than a purely subjective trait.

The scale's validity was tested using confirmatory factor anal­ ysis. The factor structure of the creativity scale was confirmed (x2 = 1.82, RMSEA = 0.04, GFI = 0.96, NFI = 0.95, CFI = 0.98). The overall Cronbach's alpha internal consistency coefficient of the scale is 0.85. The internal consistency coefficients of its subscales range from 0.72 to 0.76. The scale is used to calculate the total score. Higher scores on the scale indicate higher AI literacy.

A key consideration for this study is the dual-­source and un­ even sample structure. It included both teachers (for AI accep­ tance and AI literacy at the instructor level) and students (for learning motivation and innovativeness at the learner level). Since teachers and students represent different analytical levels, the data were integrated carefully before the SEM analysis to maintain conceptual and statistical coherence. To handle this structure, they first aggregated teacher responses at the instruc­ tional group or class level. Specifically, they averaged individual teacher scores for AI acceptance and AI literacy within each teaching unit (or institution/class grouping) to create group-­level indicators. Before aggregation, they examined inter-­rater agree­ ment statistics (e.g., ICC and within-­group consistency, when relevant) to justify the aggregation and confirm that teachers shared a common understanding within the same instructional context. Afterward, these aggregated teacher-­level constructs were combined with the student dataset using a common group identifier (e.g., class, programme, or institution code). This en­ sured that each student record matched the corresponding in­ structional environment defined by teacher-­level AI acceptance and AI literacy. This method allowed teacher variables to serve as contextual predictors of student-­level outcomes (learning motivation and innovativeness) within a single integrated SEM framework.

Consequently, the final SEM specification tested conceptual re­ lationships across levels in a single-­level covariance structure model, where:

•	 AI acceptance and AI literacy (aggregated teacher-­level constructs) were exogenous variables,

5   |   Discussion

The findings of this study add to the limited understanding of how AI acceptance and AI literacy relate to creativity and learning motivation in design education, especially in Chinese higher education. Here, creativity is not just a learning target but a job requirement. Previous research has often viewed gen­ erative AI as a tool that inherently boosts creativity in design fields. However, this study challenges that view by showing that the connections between AI, motivation, and creativity are much more complex and context-­dependent than typically be­ lieved. This understanding is particularly important in design education, where originality, experimentation, tolerance for ambiguity, and independent thinking are essential to creative work rather than straightforward outputs generated by algo­ rithms. (Bartlett and Camba 2024; Wilhelmsen et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2025; Han and Piaw 2025).

With this background, the difference between AI acceptance and AI literacy, as seen through teaching experience, is easier to interpret. Teaching experience deepens AI literacy, particularly PCK and TPACK, through ongoing involvement in instructional design and classroom practice. However, this does not automati­ cally lead to stronger AI acceptance, which might be influenced more by institutional discussions or general attitudes toward new technologies. In this regard, the results confirm that H2 was also supported, as AI acceptance positively affected students' learning motivation. This suggests that favourable views of AI boost learners' willingness to engage in their studies, even if this motivation does not always lead to deeper cognitive or creative results. This separation is important because much of the ex­ isting literature confuses acceptance with competence. It over­ looks that educators and students can have a positive attitude toward AI while lacking the deeper analytical and pedagogical skills needed for effective integration (Börekci and Çelik 2024; Yao and Wang 2024; Salhab and Aboushi 2025). Moreover, mul­ tivariate patterns that show variation in motivation and creativ­ ity by gender and age indicate that involvement in AI-­supported design learning is not equal across learner groups. These differ­ ences make sense when viewed through the lens of AI literacy dimensions. For example, stronger CK and TCK may foster crit­ ical engagement with AI systems, increasing technical aware­ ness while potentially reducing excitement driven by novelty. Variations in TPACK may further shape how students use AI in creative processes, affecting their autonomy and ownership. These findings highlight that AI-­related learning outcomes are influenced by psychological, cognitive and contextual factors, not just exposure (Hwang and Wu 2025; Jiang et al. 2025; Yin et al. 2026).

The demographic results show that female teachers had signifi­ cantly higher AI acceptance and AI literacy compared to male teachers. This finding complicates past assumptions that tech­ nological confidence and AI engagement are primarily associ­ ated with male educators. Instead, the results suggest that the integration of AI in creative fields may depend more on teaching flexibility, collaboration, and a willingness to experiment with new technologies than on traditional gender stereotypes. Recent evidence supports this idea, indicating that AI acceptance is more closely tied to teaching beliefs, professional adaptability, and perceived relevance than to demographics alone. (Yim and Wegerif 2024; Jin and Rui 2026; Zhu et al. 2026). Additionally, the lack of significant differences across educational levels indi­ cates that having formal academic qualifications alone does not fully explain readiness for AI. This finding challenges the belief that higher academic achievement automatically leads to better adaptation to new technologies. It suggests that competence in using AI in design education may develop more from hands-­on experience and ongoing exposure than from formal degrees. (Luo et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2025).

Further, the finding that teaching experience significantly af­ fects AI literacy but not AI acceptance shows an important dif­ ference between being ready to use AI and actually having the skills to do so in education. In this study, AI acceptance was conceptualized as a motivational and attitudinal construct en­ compassing perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, be­ havioural intention, and trust in AI systems. 6   |   Research Implications

This study adds to the growing conversation about generative artificial intelligence (GAI) in design education. It provides real-­ world evidence from Chinese higher education, where creativ­ ity, originality, and innovation are essential goals. Unlike much existing research, which mainly views AI as a tool to enhance learning and creativity, the current findings reveal a more com­ plex relationship among AI acceptance, AI literacy, learning mo­ tivation, and creativity in design fields. These conclusions have significant policy and practical implications for universities, curriculum developers, educators and policymakers who want to use AI in creative education responsibly and sustainably.

The discovery that AI literacy is negatively associated with learning motivation is especially notable, as it contrasts with the literature that positions AI literacy as empowering (Ocado 2025; Akman 2025; Wu and Zhang 2025). This outcome helps explain why H5 was not strongly supported, as the mediating role of motivation is weakened when AI literacy increases awareness of AI limitations. Students with higher CK may become more aware of the reduced spontaneity in AI-­generated outputs. At the same time, those with stronger TCK and TPACK might rec­ ognize the predictive nature of AI systems, which can lessen emotional engagement and intrinsic motivation. Likewise, the negative relationship between AI literacy and creativity clari­ fies why H4 was only partially supported and why H6 did not show a consistent pathway. Although AI literacy improves tech­ nical and pedagogical understanding, it may also encourage structured approaches that limit divergent thinking. Students with stronger TCK and TPACK may lean more on AI-­generated suggestions, which can hinder originality and risk-­taking in ex­ ploration. This supports concerns that AI systems may uninten­ tionally standardize creative outputs (Zhang et al. 2025; Herath et al. 2026; Qiu et al. 2026). At the same time, the direct positive relationship between AI acceptance and creativity confirms H3, showing that positive views of AI initially promote experimenta­ tion and creative engagement (Li et al. 2025; Ge and Shin 2026). However, this direct effect contrasts with the indirect pathways, in which increased AI literacy and reduced motivational en­ gagement weaken creative outcomes. Thus, while acceptance encourages initial openness and exploration, its benefits fade when mediated through deeper AI engagement processes.

6.1   |   Policy Implications

The findings suggest that higher education policies regarding AI in design disciplines should go beyond mere technology adoption. They should focus on balanced teaching methods and AI frameworks that are sensitive to creativity. Current policies often prioritize technological readiness, operational efficiency, and digital change but do not fully consider how overreliance on AI might weaken originality, intrinsic motivation, and inde­ pendent creative thinking. The study shows a negative indirect effect of AI literacy on creativity, suggesting that policies should not automatically equate AI skill with creative growth. Instead, AI governance policies in design education should address is­ sues like cognitive dependence, creative sameness, ethical de­ sign practices and overreliance on algorithms.

The results also highlight the need for specific AI integration policies across disciplines rather than a one-­size-­fits-­all digi­ tal strategy. Design education is fundamentally different from many STEM fields because it requires creative ambiguity, exper­ imentation, subjective interpretation and aesthetic exploration. As a result, policies that encourage broad AI adoption without protecting human-­centred creativity may weaken the founda­ tional principles of design education. Universities should create thoughtful AI guidelines that view AI as a supportive tool for co-­creation rather than a replacement for human imagination and artistic inquiry. Moreover, the significant role of teachers' AI acceptance and literacy underscores the need for national and institutional educational policies that invest in ongoing pro­ fessional development for design educators. These programmes should go beyond just technical AI training and cover critical AI teaching methods, ethical AI use, creative facilitation strategies

Furthermore, the positive link between learning motivation and creativity supports established theories that emphasize the role of intrinsic motivation in creative performance (Kadyirov et al. 2024; Jiang et al. 2025). This confirms the motivational aspect within H5 at the structural level, although its overall mediating strength remains limited. Creativity still relies on psychological engagement, autonomy and curiosity, even in AI-­ supported environments. Finally, the SEM findings highlight the contradictory nature of AI use in design education. While H3 is supported through a direct positive pathway, the broader model shows that AI literacy introduces a paradox that could suppress creativity through over-­optimization and reliance

The study also emphasizes the need to include AI ethics, intel­ lectual property awareness, originality standards and critical digital literacy in national curriculum standards for design ed­ ucation. With growing concerns about plagiarism, automated creativity and ownership of AI-­generated works, policymakers must establish clearer ethical guidelines that protect genuine creative development while enabling productive AI use in edu­ cational settings.

6.2   |   Practical Implications

From a practical standpoint, the findings recommend that de­ sign educators take a balanced approach to AI use in their teach­ ing. While AI acceptance can enhance creative engagement, the negative effects of AI literacy suggest that heavy reliance on AI tools may hinder independent creative exploration. Design edu­ cators should avoid teaching methods that focus solely on mas­ tering AI skills. Instead, AI activities should be integrated into reflective, inquiry-­based, and human-­centred creative processes that uphold students' autonomy and originality.

The findings also suggest that AI-­supported design learning environments should focus on developing the creative process rather than just the final products. Students should be encour­ aged to critically assess, reinterpret, and challenge AI-­generated suggestions rather than accept algorithmic outputs. Techniques such as iterative critique, reflective journaling, collaborative brainstorming, and concept justification may help students re­ main intellectually engaged and think independently while using AI technologies. The relationship between AI literacy and learning motivation should be carefully examined in light of the overall model results. In these results, learning motivation had only a small indirect effect on creativity. This means that while improved AI literacy may affect how motivated students feel—especially if they see creative tasks as being increasingly supported by AI—this change in motivation does not necessar­ ily lead to lower creative performance in the structural model. Still, shifts in motivation are important for teaching because they can influence the quality of student engagement, their persistence, and their willingness to explore new learning pro­ cesses. Educators should create AI-­supported learning environ­ ments that allow students to maintain control and ownership of their tasks. Assignments should promote experimentation, orig­ inality, and thoughtful reasoning rather than just focusing on ef­ ficiency. Even if motivation isn't the main driver of creativity in the model, it remains crucial for fostering meaningful engage­ ment and enhancing the deeper learning processes essential to creative thinking in AI-­integrated classrooms.

The study also highlights the need to rethink assessment prac­ tices in design education.
