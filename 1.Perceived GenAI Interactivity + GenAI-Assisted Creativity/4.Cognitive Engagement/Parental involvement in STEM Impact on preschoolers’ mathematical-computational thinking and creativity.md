ABSTRACT Parental involvement can support early cognitive skills, but its role in STEM education is not well understood. This study examines the impact of parental involvement in early childhood STEM education on mathematical-computational thinking and creativity in 5-year-old children. Using a quasi-experimental design, 28 preschoolers were divided into intervention and comparison groups. The intervention group participated in a 16-week structured STEM program, which included classroom activities and home-based tasks with parents. Data were collected using the Preschool Mathematical and Computational Thinking Observation Form and the Integrated Creative Test for Preschoolers-Creative Thinking Ability Section. Pretest scores showed group similarity, while post-test results revealed significant improvements in mathematical-computational thinking and creativity within the intervention group. Wilcoxon Signed Rank and Mann–Whitney U tests confirmed these findings, demonstrating a large effect size. The results highlight the importance of structured parental involvement in STEM education and provide insights into designing accessible home– school learning environments.

Introduction

From an early age, children look at their surroundings with deep curiosity and are eager to explore many questions about their environment. This fosters a natural interest in Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) disciplines (Banko et  al., 2013; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2022). STEM educa­ tion is increasingly seen as a key driver of economic inno­ vation, educational equity, and technological literacy, making its integration into early childhood education both a global priority and a developmental necessity (Martín‐Páez et  al., 2019; NASEM, 2022). It has also the potential to support competencies expected in the qualified workforce of the twenty first century, such as problem-solving, critical think­ ing, mathematical and computational thinking, and creativity (Mohaghegh & McCauley, 2016; Suherman et  al., 2022; Trevallion & Nischang, 2021). Initiating the structuring of STEM concepts and skills in early childhood allows children to explore more complex and abstract topics in depth as they grow older (Tippett & Milford, 2017). This structuring process is necessary for students to understand and generate solutions to complex problems required by the twenty first century (Martín‐Páez et  al., 2019). In early childhood, the family plays a critical role in chil­ dren’s holistic development, including cognitive, emotional, and social domains (Bronfenbrenner, 2005). Mathematical and computational thinking

Mathematical thinking is a structured mental process that students use when faced with complex mathematical prob­ lems that do not have an evident solution (Jawad et  al., 2021). It involves processes such as recognizing patterns, abstracting key information, and applying logical reasoning to solve problems (Tall, 2013). Computational thinking, on the other hand, refers to the process of developing solutions to abstract and automated problems (Yadav et  al., 2014). Waterman et al. (2018) describe it as a cognitive process that entails formulating problems, breaking them into smaller components, and structuring solutions in ways comprehensi­ ble to both humans and machines. Both types of thinking are critical for preparing students with future competencies (Wing, 2008). Regarding their relationship, mathematical and computational thinking share commonalities in problem-solving, modeling, data analysis, and interpretation, particularly in areas like statistics and probability (Pei et  al., 2018; Sneider et  al., 2014). These shared aspects align with the components of the SEPs (NGSS Lead States, 2013).

Mathematical and computational thinking are recognized as essential cognitive models for all students (Li et al., 2020) and are uniquely positioned within STEM education to develop students’ models of thinking (Li et  al., 2019). The integration of computational thinking into discipline-specific education within STEM fields (Lee et  al., 2020; Li et  al., 2020) suggests that both STEM content and contexts benefit from computational thinking (Çiftçi & Topçu, 2023; Shang et  al., 2023; Weintrop et  al., 2016). Furthermore, including computational thinking facilitates learning STEM content (Ng et  al., 2023; Sengupta et  al., 2013). Despite its recog­ nized importance, the integration of computational thinking into STEM education is a developing research area. This highlights the need for the design and exploration of varied teaching strategies (Wang et  al., 2022). Moreover, while research on the impact of STEM education on mathematical and computational thinking exists, studies focusing on pre­ school education and parental involvement in STEM educa­ tion remain very limited.

Building on this, the current study examines mathemati­ cal and computational thinking as an integrated form, con­ ceptualized as complementary cognitive processes that enhance problem-solving and reasoning within STEM edu­ cation. This integrated perspective emphasizes their shared elements, while also highlighting their unique contributions to fostering students’ higher-order thinking skills (Sneider et  al., 2014). To evaluate this integrated concept, an assess­ ment tool was developed using the SEPs framework (NGSS Lead States, 2013), chosen for its comprehensive alignment with the cognitive processes underpinning mathematical and computational thinking. This underscores their critical importance for 21st-century skills and highlights their foun­ dational role in STEM disciplines (Prinsley & Johnston, 2015).

Creativity, defined by Torrance (1965) as the ability to rec­ ognize problems, make predictions, form hypotheses, exper­ iment, and persist until a solution is found. In this study, creativity is conceptualized as the capacity to generate novel and useful ideas, solve problems in innovative ways, and express thoughts or emotions. This perspective aligns with Torrance’s framework but is tailored to capture the develop­ mental characteristics of preschool-aged children, where cre­ ativity is often reflected in exploration, play, and imaginative activities (Alfonso-Benlliure et  al., 2013; Leggett, 2017). Building on this understanding, the study adopts the frame­ work developed by Lee and Lee (2002) to assess creativity. This framework, rooted in the seminal work of Torrance (1965), assesses creativity in two domains: language and drawing. By capturing both verbal and visual aspects of cre­ ativity, it provides a well-rounded approach to understanding how young children express ideas and solve problems. This perspective ensures that the study remains focused on the developmental capacities of early childhood.

Early childhood is a critical period for fostering creativity, as children exhibit heightened curiosity and exploratory behaviors during this stage. Activities such as drawing, danc­ ing, storytelling, and game creation promote creative growth by encouraging children to express their ideas, experiment with new possibilities, and engage in imaginative play (Yates & Twigg, 2017). Furthermore, a stimulating and supportive educational environment accelerates this development by providing opportunities for children to explore and refine their ideas (Alfonso-Benlliure et  al., 2013; Boden, 2004). It is important to have structured activities that support cre­ ativity in the educational environment. Supporting creativity from a young age will lead to the emergence of adults who lead scientific discoveries in society (May, 2009). STEM edu­ cation fosters curiosity, creativity, critical thinking, problem-solving, and collaboration from an early age (Bybee, 2013; Chesloff, 2013; Prinsley & Baranyai, 2015). Aguilera and Ortiz-Revilla (2021) also highlight its impact on creativ­ ity development. However, research on the effects of parental involvement in preschool STEM education on creativity remains limited.

Method

Research design

To investigate the impact of parental-involved STEM educa­ tion on preschool students’ ‘mathematical and computational thinking’ and ‘creativity’, we used a quasi-experimental design with comparison groups and pre-and post-tests. This design was chosen to assess the effectiveness of the parental-involved STEM implementations in a controlled manner, aiming to reduce the influence of potential con­ founding factors (Creswell & Creswell, 2022). Participants were not randomly assigned to either the intervention or comparison group. The intervention group consisted of par­ ticipants who received the parental-involved STEM educa­ tion program, while the comparison group comprised those who did not. Unlike a true experimental design, the use of the terms intervention and comparison groups underscores the quasi-experimental nature of this study (Gopalan et  al., 2020). Additionally, it should be noted that each group in the present study consisted of 14 participants, which is con­ sidered a small sample size according to Pituch and Stevens (2015). Small sample sizes may limit the generalizability of findings. To mitigate this limitation, we calculated effect sizes to provide a more nuanced interpretation of the find­ ings, considering both statistical and practical significance (Cohen, 1988).

Program description

The intervention program consisted of 16 STEM activities adapted from the STEM curriculum for K-12 – TeachEngineering (2023), a widely used, standards-aligned educational resource created through collaboration among engineering faculty at U.S. universities and the American Society for Engineering Education. This curriculum includes hands-on, inquiry-based lessons designed to integrate sci­ ence, technology, engineering, and mathematics disciplines across grade levels. For this study, the selected activities were reviewed and evaluated by two experts in STEM and science education. The selection process prioritized activities that aligned with early childhood learning goals and the study’s focus on fostering mathematical and computational thinking and creativity through parental involvement. To ensure developmental appropriateness and contextual fit, the activi­ ties were then revised by four doctoral-level researchers spe­ cializing in preschool, STEM, mathematics, and science education. While the original TeachEngineering curriculum is not explicitly designed for parental involvement, the selected activities were modified to include home-based components, such as shared materials and family data col­ lection sheets, to enable meaningful participation by parents.

The 16 activities (See the Appendix) covered various STEM subfields. The activities were designed by emphasiz­ ing one or more STEM disciplines as core foci—primarily mathematics and/or science—while incorporating the remaining disciplines contextually, depending on the learn­ ing goals of each activity. Activities integrated concepts such as measurement, graphing, estimation, biological growth, data representation, and pattern recognition. Each activity was embedded within the structure of the preschool’s exist­ ing curriculum (MoNE, 2013), complementing its goals and routines without disrupting its play-based, child-centered approach. For example, "How Tall Are We?" activity was implemented with preschool children in the study’s interven­ tion group and structured as follows: Centered on the math­ ematics and science disciplines; the activity focused on creating graphs from measurement results, with a biological context addressing human growth and development. Implementation process

We organized a briefing session for the parents of children in both the intervention and comparison groups to explain the study’s procedures and collect informed consent forms, ensuring participants’ rights and confidentiality. During this session, the measurement tools were introduced to each group.

A separate meeting was held specifically for the interven­ tion group parents to outline the parental involvement pro­ cess in detail. This meeting consisted of two 40-minute training sessions. The first session provided an overview of the planned activities, clarified parents’ roles, and introduced the STEM concepts underlying each task. The second ses­ sion featured a sample activity and guidance on how to sup­ port children’s learning at home. To maintain ongoing support throughout the intervention, weekly face-to-face meetings were held at the preschool, typically at the end of the school day. During these sessions, the weekly STEM activity was introduced, its goals and procedures were explained, and parents were encouraged to ask questions, share ideas, and discuss potential implementation challenges. In this way, consistent and meaningful guidance was pro­ vided to promote effective parental participation. Visuals of both classroom and home activities are presented (see Figure 1).

Children in the intervention group participated in 16 structured STEM activities designed to complement their preschool curriculum. Each activity, lasting approximately 30–40 min, was conducted by the intervention team within the classroom setting. These activities emphasized parental engagement, with children and their parents completing related tasks at home. In contrast, no additional activities or follow-up meetings were arranged for the comparison group parents beyond the initial briefing session. These families followed the standard preschool curriculum (MoNE, 2013), which does not include STEM-specific content. The curricu­ lum focuses on cognitive, social-emotional, motor, and lan­ guage development through a play-based, child-centered approach that includes a variety of indoor and outdoor learning experiences.

The intentional difference in engagement—16 homesupported STEM activities in the intervention group versus none in the comparison group—allowed for a clearer exam­ ination of the potential impact of both STEM exposure and parental involvement on children’s learning outcomes. All activities were conducted by the same researcher in similar classroom settings to ensure consistency and control for external variables. Implementation procedures varied by activity content but generally included face-to-face interac­ tion, modeling, and hands-on exploration. This structured and well-supported approach enabled a focused investigation into the educational impact of the STEM program and the role of parental collaboration.

Data analysis

To determine whether parametric or non-parametric com­ parison tests should be used to address the research ques­ tions, we examined the assumptions for parametric tests. Normality in the dataset was evaluated by analyzing the pre­ test and post-test scores of the intervention and comparison groups. This analysis included skewness and kurtosis values as well as histograms. In the comparison group, the skewness and kurtosis values were 0.40 and −1.29 for the creativity post-test, and −1.11 and −0.30 for the mathematical and computational thinking pretest. In the intervention group, the skewness and kurtosis values were −0.02 and 1.18 for the creativity pretest, and −1.38 and 0.73 for the mathematical and computational thinking post-test. Furthermore, the dis­ tribution observed in the histogram graphs falling outside the specified range indicated that the data distribution was not normal. Discussion and conclusion

This study aimed to investigate the effects of parent-involved STEM activities on the development of mathematical and computational thinking, as well as creativity, among five-yearold preschool students. The results of this research provide supporting evidence that structured STEM activities imple­ mented with parental involvement lead to significant gains in both domains. Specifically, children in the intervention group demonstrated marked improvements in identifying mathematical patterns, making estimations, and reasoning through basic problem-solving steps—key indicators of mathematical and computational thinking. In parallel, they also exhibited enhanced originality and elaboration in their verbal and visual expressions, reflecting stronger creative thinking skills.

Consistent with Yadav et  al. (2014) and Waterman et  al. (2018), the present study demonstrates that young children, when supported through guided parental involvement, can engage in structured activities that promote early computa­ tional reasoning, such as decomposition and measurementbased estimations. Similarly, the improvement in mathemat­ ical thinking aligns with the findings of Tall (2013), who emphasized pattern recognition and quantitative reasoning as essential components of early mathematical development. The active participation of families appears to serve as a key mediator in this process. As suggested by Hadani and Rood (2018), and supported by Milner-Bolotin and Marotto (2018), involving parents in the educational process strength­ ens motivation, engagement, and the integration of abstract skills into everyday experiences. Our study supports this view by providing evidence that structured, home-based extensions of classroom STEM activities can significantly deepen children’s conceptual understanding. Taken together, the findings emphasize the potential of early, family-supported STEM interventions to foster foundational competencies in both mathematical-computational domains and creative thinking.

In family-involved STEM education, working with a small sample can be appropriate for ensuring high-quality imple­ mentation and meaningful parental engagement. Such pro­ grams require close communication with families, distribution of materials, and regular follow-up. For example, Erol et  al. (2025) employed a small-scale design when exploring girls’ engineering habits of mind. Similarly, Abanoz and Kalelioğlu (2025) emphasized that small-group settings in early child­ hood STEM education facilitate intentional, individualized pedagogical strategies that are crucial for cultivating algo­ rithmic thinking. In our study, working with a limited sam­ ple enabled more effective coordination and reliable observation, while also aligning with legal regulations on class sizes in early childhood education in Turkey.

The observed improvements in children’s mathematical and computational thinking and creativity may be linked to several key features of the implemented STEM program. First, the classroom activities were intentionally structured to introduce STEM concepts such as measurement, data repre­ sentation, and problem-solving in age-appropriate, hands-on ways (Bybee, 2013). For example, activities like “How Tall Are We?” integrated mathematics and biology, allowing chil­ dren to practice estimating, measuring, recording, and inter­ preting data through real-world connections. Second, the inclusion of a home component aimed to reinforce learning through parental involvement. Parents received instructional materials and were encouraged to engage in STEM tasks with their children. This aligns with theoretical perspectives emphasizing the role of guided participation and social modeling in learning (Bandura, 1977; Vygotsky, 1978). In our context, both the teacher and the parents functioned as responsive facilitators, helping children explore and internal­ ize STEM-related ideas through modeling and shared activ­ ity (NRC, 2015). Third, the program was designed to elicit both logical and imaginative thinking. Activities encouraged children to make predictions, measure, organize data, and generate new ideas based on observations—processes associ­ ated with both computational thinking and creativity. Through these combined elements, the program offered a developmentally appropriate and contextually meaningful structure for supporting children’s learning in the early years.

Despite its limitations, our study makes an important contribution to early childhood STEM education research and parental involvement practices. In response to calls for empirical investigation by Gülhan (2023) and Wan et  al. (2021), our research addresses the gap concerning the effects of parental involvement in STEM education on preschoolers’ learning outcomes. We provide preliminary evidence sug­ gesting that our implementations may support improvements in students’ mathematical and computational thinking skills as well as creativity. These findings suggest integrating parental involvement strategies into early childhood STEM programs could significantly improve learning outcomes. Future research should build on these findings to explore scalable and sustainable models of parental engagement in STEM education.

Furthermore, it is essential to note that the STEM inte­ gration we employ focuses on science and mathematics as the central components, incorporating SEPs (NGSS Lead States, 2013) into the process. The findings indicate that involving families in STEM activities is a valuable strategy for supporting preschoolers’ mathematical and computational thinking and creativity. Therefore, we suggest that policy­ makers consider structured parental involvement programs in early childhood curricula.
