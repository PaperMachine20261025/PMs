Reframing human–AI collaboration in higher education: behavioral pathways from generative AI to critical thinking and ethical creativity

Min Jou1 · Chih-Chi Kao1

Received: 8 October 2025 / Accepted: 18 June 2026 © The Author(s) 2026

Abstract Generative AI is increasingly positioned not only as a tool for accelerating produc­ tion but also as a collaborator that reshapes how individuals regulate cognition, enact innovation, and negotiate creative integrity. This study developed a dualmediation framework to explain how AI-assisted tool use, hereafter referred to as AI engagement, is associated with higher-order competencies. Drawing on Social Cognitive Theory, we conceptualized self-regulated learning (SRL) and innovative behavior (IB) as behavioral pathways linking AI-assisted tool use to critical think­ ing (CT) and creative integrity (CI). Survey data from undergraduate students in design-related higher education were analyzed using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM). The results showed that AI-assisted tool use was positively associated with SRL and IB, which were in turn associated with CT and CI. Significant indirect effects further indicated that reflective regulation and purposeful innovation served as important mechanisms through which AI-assisted engagement was linked to higher-order growth. A complementary qualitative phase illustrated how learners framed AI as a “cognitive partner” for idea clarification, iteratively repurposed AI-generated outputs for innovation, and grappled with ques­ tions of authorship and attribution. By integrating quantitative modeling with quali­ tative insights, the study reframes AI not merely as a productivity booster but as a catalyst for reflective, innovative, and ethically attuned practices in AI-mediated design contexts. These findings provide context-specific evidence for understanding human–AI collaboration and offer practical directions for embedding responsibility and criticality in AI-mediated learning environments, while advancing a processoriented account of how learners translate AI-assisted engagement into cognitive and ethical development.

Keywords  Human–AI collaboration · Self-regulated learning · Innovation · Critical thinking · Creative integrity

Introduction and research background

AI-mediated design contexts have increasingly become important arenas for culti­ vating creative thinking, visual communication, and applied problem-solving. As artificial intelligence (AI) technologies proliferate, this evolving landscape is under­ going profound transformation. Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT, DALL·E, and Midjourney now enable creators to generate conceptual sketches, recombine stylistic vocabularies, and assemble multimodal outputs with unprecedented speed, reshap­ ing not only creative processes but also the behavioral mechanisms underpinning human–AI interaction (Lee, 2025; Lee et al., 2025). While these affordances promise greater productivity and new expressive possibilities, a critical question persists: do such tools genuinely enhance higher-order capabilities—specifically critical thinking (CT) and creative integrity (CI)—or do they simply magnify surface-level efficiency (García-López & Trujillo-Liñán, 2025; Qadhi et al., 2024)?

This tension reflects a deeper challenge. Creators are not merely adopting upgraded instruments; they are renegotiating the psychological and behavioral foun­ dations of their practice. Within AI-mediated design contexts, individuals must iden­ tify problems, iterate solutions, and continuously recalibrate their cognitive strategies and creative decisions. Without purposeful scaffolding or self-regulation, the rapid diffusion of AI risks fostering passivity, over-reliance, and blurred moral boundar­ ies—outcomes that highlight the need to examine self-regulated learning (SRL) and innovative behavior (IB) as pivotal mechanisms linking AI tool use to higher-order competencies (Tzirides, 2024; Urmeneta & Romero, 2025).

To address, this issue, the present study draws on Social Cognitive Theory (Ban­ dura, 2001), particularly its principle of reciprocal determinism, which posits that personal factors, behaviors, and environmental influences dynamically shape learn­ ing and performance outcomes. Extending this perspective, the present study moves beyond viewing AI as a mere tool or static environment by conceptualizing it as a quasi-agentic collaborator that actively participates in learners’ cognitive regula­ tion and innovative enactment. Within this reframed perspective, AI-assisted design tools represent not only environmental affordances but also dialogic and generative stimuli that provoke learners’ reflective monitoring, strategic adaptation, and creative transformation. Prior studies identify SRL as a cornerstone for higher-order cognition and creative productivity, enabling individuals to navigate complexity while miti­ gating over-reliance on tools (Faza & Lestari, 2025; Xu et al., 2026; Zimmerman, 2002). IB, in turn, reflects the enactment of cognitive flexibility and contextual adap­ tation, allowing users to reinterpret and transform AI-generated inputs into novel and responsible creative outputs (Dogra & Parrey, 2024; Islam et al., 2025).

Building on this theoretical foundation, we proposed a dual-mediation model in which AI-assisted tool use acts as the exogenous driver, SRL and IB serve as parallel mediators, and CT and CI emerge as the core outcomes. To capture both the structural relationships and the nuanced psychological pathways linking these constructs, we employed an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018), integrating partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) with in-depth qualitative interviews. Rather than claiming broad generalizability, this study offers a behaviorally grounded account of how AI use, filtered through SRL and

IB, may support CT and CI within design-related higher education. These findings may inform broader discussions on human–AI interaction, while requiring further validation across disciplines, institutional settings, and professional environments.

Literature review

The role of creative integrity in design-related higher education

Design-related higher education represents a domain that integrates aesthetics, functionality, and social responsibility. Students in this field are expected not only to demonstrate creative skills but also to cultivate sensitivity to ethical and valueladen considerations. With the growing adoption of generative artificial intelligence (AI) in design education, a pressing challenge has emerged: how can AI-generated outputs be transformed into innovative outcomes that meet professional standards while upholding ethical and social responsibility? Within this context, scholars have highlighted the importance of ethical creativity—also referred to here as creative integrity (CI)—which emphasizes the integration of innovation with ethical judg­ ment (Gulumbe et al., 2025; Khosrowi et al., 2025; Omran Zailuddin et al., 2024; Peters & Angelov, 2025).

In design-related higher education, creative integrity is manifested across four interrelated dimensions. First, originality: students must approach AI-generated ideas as sources of inspiration rather than final solutions, engaging in reinterpreta­ tion and integration to assert personal style and professional value (Lee, 2025; Lee et al., 2025). Second, responsibility: design outcomes inherently involve feasibil­ ity and user safety, requiring students to critically examine AI proposals to ensure compliance with structural and regulatory standards (Hwang & Wu, 2024). Third, cultural sensitivity: design-related higher education contexts frequently incorporates cultural symbols and lifestyles, obligating students to critically filter AI outputs to avoid cultural appropriation or contextual misalignment (Akman, 2025; Li et al., 2024). Finally, sustainability: given the increasing importance of green design, stu­ dents are expected to adjust AI-generated solutions by considering materials, energy use, and environmental impacts to align with sustainable principles (Gulumbe et al., 2025; Khosrowi et al., 2025). Taken together, creative integrity extends the traditional understanding of creativ­ ity by embedding responsibility, cultural awareness, and sustainability into the design process. In AI-assisted contexts, it functions as a safeguard against the potential ero­ sion of professionalism and accountability, requiring students to engage in self-reg­ ulation and critical reflection. Ultimately, CI positions learners not merely as users of AI but as responsible co-creators, capable of producing innovative yet ethically grounded design practices. While design-related higher education contexts serves here as an illustrative domain, the concept of CI is broadly applicable across AI- mediated learning and work contexts, highlighting how ethical creativity can func­ tion as a generalizable behavioral outcome of human–AI interaction.

AI-assisted design tools and the reshaping of learning behaviors

Generative AI tools—such as ChatGPT, Midjourney, and DALL·E—are fundamen­ tally altering how knowledge is produced and creative workflows unfold within AI- mediated design contexts. These systems provide students with rapid prototyping, linguistic translation, and stylistic suggestions, allowing for accelerated ideation and draft generation in complex design tasks (Lee, 2025; Lee et al., 2025). Yet, this very convenience has raised concerns regarding attenuated cognitive engagement and the emergence of “creative complacency” (Tzirides, 2024; Urmeneta & Romero, 2025). While AI undoubtedly streamlines output and fosters novel forms of multimodal expression, its use can dilute students’ intrinsic cognitive processing. When learners treat AI as a definitive answer provider rather than as a collaborative partner, their potential to cultivate critical thinking (CT) and nuanced ethical awareness is signifi­ cantly compromised (García-López & Trujillo-Liñán, 2025; Qadhi et al., 2024).

Consequently, a central challenge for AI-mediated design contexts lies in how students can transform AI from a passive aid into a vehicle for self-regulated learn­ ing (SRL) and innovative behavior (IB)—two behavioral mechanisms that undergird deeper cognitive and ethical development (AlBlooshi, 2026; Batista et al., 2024). Framing AI use through SRL highlights the metacognitive regulation of strategies and effort, while IB reflects the proactive enactment of flexibility and contextual adaptation in applying AI outputs. Although illustrated here in design education, this challenge is broadly shared across AI-mediated learning and professional settings, underscoring the behavioral nature of how humans adapt to AI-driven transformation.

Self-regulated learning: cognitive regulation in AI-enhanced contexts

Self-regulated learning (SRL) refers to individuals’ proactive orchestration of their learning through goal setting, process monitoring, and strategic adjustment to achieve task mastery (Zimmerman, 2002). Its value becomes particularly evident in contexts that demand complex problem-solving and creative synthesis, where learners must dynamically calibrate cognitive and metacognitive processes (Faza & Lestari, 2025; Xu et al., 2026).

Within AI-enhanced environments, SRL functions as a psychological filter and behavioral regulator: learners must continually evaluate AI outputs for utility, authenticity, and creative potential, a process that inherently draws upon advanced regulatory capacities (Pintrich, 2000). Empirical studies suggest that individuals with robust SRL tendencies leverage AI to compare alternative solutions, clarify ambigui­ ties, and iteratively refine outputs—behaviors that contribute to deeper learning and the emergence of critical thinking (CT) (Akcaoğlu et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2023; Wong et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2025).

Thus, SRL is not merely a skill of adaptation; it constitutes a behavioral mecha­ nism through which AI’s technological affordances are translated into cognitive empowerment and ethical awareness. In the present model, SRL provides the first explanatory link between AI tool use and higher-order outcomes, offering a pathway toward both CT and creative integrity (CI) (see H1 and H5). Although examined here in educational design contexts, SRL may also be relevant to other AI-mediated learn­

ing and professional settings; however, further empirical validation is needed before broader claims can be made.

Innovative behavior: bridging technology use and creative responsibility

Innovative behavior (IB) encompasses the proactive generation of new ideas, the integration of disparate resources, and the enactment of novel solutions (Dogra & Parrey, 2024; Islam et al., 2025). In AI-mediated design workflows, IB manifests not only through creative outputs but also through learners’ capacity to reconfigure technological resources into personalized creative trajectories. Recent studies illus­ trate that individuals who treat AI-generated content as “scaffolding” rather than as a final product are more likely to produce original, diverse expressions and demon­ strate adaptive resilience in addressing ill-structured design challenges (Hwang & Wu, 2024; Pipuš, 2025). Particularly when confronted with fragmented datasets or high degrees of ambiguity, IB enables learners to synthesize AI-suggested elements with their own perspectives, constructing distinctive and responsible creative narra­ tives (Akman, 2025; Li et al., 2024).

As a behavioral mechanism, IB represents the enactment of cognitive flexibility and contextual adaptation, transforming environmental input into higher-order out­ comes. This dimension serves as the critical conduit between AI-assisted tool use and ethically grounded creativity, thereby grounding the rationale for H2 and H6. While highlighted here in design education, the behavioral role of IB may also be relevant to other AI-mediated learning and professional contexts; however, further empirical validation is needed before broader claims can be made.

Creative integrity: redefining learner responsibility in the AI era

Creative integrity (CI) emphasizes learners’ capacity to navigate authorship, stylistic coherence, and process transparency when AI-generated contributions are involved. Unlike traditional notions of academic integrity, which largely revolve around pla­ giarism and citation, CI foregrounds the creator’s reflective acknowledgment of human–AI collaboration and the ethical stewardship of co-produced artifacts (Omran Zailuddin et al., 2024; Peters & Angelov, 2025). As AI tools increasingly yield highquality, near-finished proposals, individuals must negotiate blurred lines of authorship and establish clear accountability for their creative outputs (Chiu, 2025; Wiese et al., 2025). Neglecting this reflective stance—by uncritically incorporating AI outputs— risks not only undermining originality but also introducing ethical vulnerabilities.

Cultivating CI, therefore, hinges on learners’ ability to enact critical discernment, responsible decision-making, and stylistic self-awareness, positioning it as both a behavioral and ethical outcome of human–AI interaction (Gulumbe et al., 2025; Khosrowi et al., 2025). Within the present framework, these competencies ground the outcomes hypothesized in H4 and H6, framing CI as a defining higher-order capability in AI-supported learning. Although illustrated here in design education, the imperative of CI extends broadly to AI-mediated creative and professional prac­ tices, underscoring its significance for contemporary human–AI interaction research.

Theoretical integration and hypothesis development

Synthesizing the literature, AI-assisted design tools can serve as catalysts for cogni­ tion and creativity, yet their behavioral and psychological impact depends on learn­ ers’ ability to regulate, innovate, and exercise ethical judgment. Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura, 2001) offers a suitable lens for modeling these interactions: behav­ ior is co-constructed through reciprocal dynamics among environmental affordances (AI tools), personal factors (SRL), and enacted behaviors (IB). Building on this tri­ adic framework, we propose a dual-mediation model in which SRL and IB operate as complementary behavioral mechanisms, linking AI tool use to higher-order out­ comes—critical thinking (CT) and creative integrity (CI).

Accordingly, we hypothesize the following:

H1  AI-assisted design tool use is positively associated with self-regulated learning (SRL).

H2  AI-assisted design tool use is positively associated with innovative behavior (IB).

H3  SRL is positively associated with critical thinking (CT).

H4  IB is positively associated with creative integrity (CI).

H5  AI-assisted tool use is positively and indirectly associated with CT through SRL.

H6  AI-assisted tool use is positively and indirectly associated with CI through IB.

H7  SRL and IB serve as complementary mediating mechanisms linking AI-assisted tool use to CT and CI.

This model frames AI not as an end in itself but as a contextual catalyst, whose sig­ nificance arises only when learners actively engage in reflective self-regulation and innovation-driven transformation. These processes enable AI-supported learning to move beyond surface-level efficiency, fostering the dual outcomes of cognitive depth and ethical responsibility. Although our model specifies seven primary hypotheses, we also acknowledge potential cross-path effects (e.g., SRL → CI, IB → CT), which we examined post hoc as exploratory extensions to further illuminate the dynamics of human–AI interaction.

Research methodology

Learning theme design and research context

The learning intervention was situated within design-related higher education, a con­ text characterized by aesthetic decision-making, functional problem-solving, and ethical responsibility. The course was structured to immerse students in authentic,

project-based design tasks, while embedding generative AI tools as cognitive and creative partners. Students engaged with a range of platforms—such as ChatGPT, Midjourney, Tripo3D, and Fusion 360 Generative Design—to navigate the full cycle of design practice, including conceptual ideation, stylistic exploration, spatial plan­ ning, and detailed verification of structural and material feasibility.

Within this project-based learning (PBL) context, students were challenged to not only translate AI-generated outputs into workable sketches and models but also to iteratively refine and critically evaluate them, ensuring functional feasibility, aes­ thetic coherence, and practical implementability. This learning environment fore­ grounded ethical and psychological challenges often overlooked in purely technical training: students were required to delineate the boundary between AI-generated and human-created content, to critically reflect on originality and attribution, and to nego­ tiate questions of authorship and accountability in AI-augmented design processes. Research design

To address the central aim of this study—unpacking how AI-assisted learning envi­ ronments, illustrated here through design education, shape cognitive regulation and the transformation of creative integrity—this research adopted an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018). This design is par­ ticularly suited to the study’s objectives because it allows for an integrated inquiry: a quantitative phase provides rigorous hypothesis testing of the proposed dual-medi­ ation model, while a subsequent qualitative phase captures the subtle psychological processes and context-specific dynamics that statistical models alone cannot reveal.

This study was situated within an explanatory sequential mixed-methods tradi­ tion rather than a purely qualitative tradition such as phenomenology or grounded theory. The primary purpose of the qualitative phase was not to generate a new theory inductively or to describe the essence of lived experience, but to explain, contex­ tualize, and deepen the interpretation of the quantitative structural model. Accord­ ingly, the study first used PLS-SEM to test the hypothesized relationships among AI tool use, self-regulated learning, innovative behavior, critical thinking, and creative integrity. The subsequent interview phase was then used to clarify the psychological and behavioral mechanisms underlying these statistical pathways, particularly how students regulated their AI-assisted learning, transformed AI outputs into innovative practices, and negotiated ethical responsibility in creative work.

This dual-phased approach also responds to two intertwined challenges in exist­ ing research on AI and learning. First, prior studies often emphasize technological affordances or outcome metrics while neglecting the behavioral and cognitive mech­ anisms that mediate these effects (AlBlooshi, 2026; Batista et al., 2024). Second, quantitative models, while powerful for detecting structural relationships, frequently obscure the heterogeneity of learner responses, including divergent strategies, reflec­ tive capacities, and ethical reasoning (Jin et al., 2023; Wong et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2025). By combining partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) with in-depth interviews analyzed through thematic coding, this study seeks to bridge these gaps, capturing both structural patterns and nuanced, learner-specific insights.

The research unfolded in two structured stages: Quantitative Phase We employed PLS-SEM to validate the hypothesized relation­ ships between AI-assisted tool use as the exogenous variable, self-regulated learning as the personal regulatory factor, innovative behavior as the behavioral factor, and two higher-order outcomes: critical thinking and creative integrity. This stage tested direct, indirect, and dual-mediation effects (H1–H7), conceptualized as behavioral mechanisms linking AI use to higher-order outcomes, thereby providing a structural

foundation for interpreting how Bandura’s (2001) triadic reciprocal determinism operates in AI-supported contexts.

Qualitative Phase Building upon the quantitative findings, we conducted semistructured interviews with purposively sampled students, selected based on extreme or contrasting profiles, such as high versus low SRL and CI scores. These interviews aimed to surface how students perceive AI’s role as a cognitive and creative partner, the strategies they employ to regulate AI-mediated learning, and the ways they nego­ tiate authorship and ethical boundaries. Data were coded and analyzed using NVivo, with emergent themes cross-referenced against the quantitative pathways to refine theoretical interpretation.

The study’s procedural flow is summarized as follows: [Quantitative Data Collection and Analysis] → [Structural Model Validation using PLS-SEM] → [Purposeful Sampling for Qualitative Inquiry] → [NVivo Thematic Analysis and Integrated Interpretation].

This design not only yielded structural evidence and complementary process-level insights but also embodies the theoretical commitments of Social Cognitive Theory. By situating AI-assisted tool use as an environmental stimulus, SRL as a personal regulatory process, and IB as the enacted behavioral bridge, the sequential design captured the reciprocal interactions among cognition, behavior, and environment (Bandura, 2001). In doing so, it allowed us to examine how engagement with AI may catalyze or constrain the development of reflective, innovative, and ethically respon­ sible practices within design-related higher education.

Sample and data sources

The study drew on data collected from 384 undergraduate students enrolled in design-related programs at multiple universities. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling. The inclusion criterion required all respondents to have prior experience using generative AI design tools, such as ChatGPT, Midjourney, Tripo3D, and Fusion 360 Generative Design. This criterion ensured that participants’ reflec­ tions on learning processes and creative practices were grounded in authentic AI- assisted design experiences.

Data were gathered through an online survey platform. A total of 420 survey invi­ tations were distributed, and after excluding invalid or duplicate responses, 384 valid questionnaires were retained, representing a 91.4% completion rate among invitees. This high response rate can be attributed to the structured administration of the sur­ vey within course-based learning activities. Specifically, the questionnaire was dis­ tributed during scheduled class sessions across the participating universities, where instructors briefly explained the purpose of the study and facilitated student participa­ tion. Although participation remained voluntary, the in-class setting, instructor coor­ dination, and direct relevance of the questionnaire to students’ ongoing AI-assisted design tasks contributed to the high level of engagement and completion.

The demographic composition reflected a relatively balanced gender distribution, with 55.0% male students (n = 211) and 45.0% female students (n = 173). Most par­ ticipants were between 18 and 24 years old, consistent with typical undergraduate cohorts in design-related programs. Importantly, the sample demonstrated substantial

engagement with AI tools: 50.8% reported using them “frequently,” 46.6% “very frequently,” and only 2.6% “occasionally.” Such high exposure suggests that partici­ pants were not casual experimenters but had meaningfully integrated AI tools into their learning routines, making them appropriate for examining the psychological and behavioral processes theorized in this study.

This demographic profile underscores the relevance of the sample for testing how sustained AI engagement intersects with self-regulated learning and innovative behavior to shape critical thinking (CT) and creative integrity (CI) in design-related higher education contexts. The qualitative participants were purposively selected from the quantitative sample based on their survey profiles (Table 1).

Research instruments

All quantitative measures in this study employed a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree, and were adapted from established instruments to ensure both validity and contextual relevance. Each scale was directly aligned with the theoretical constructs underpinning the hypothesized dual-mediation model (H1–H7).

The AI Tool Use Scale was informed by recent scale development research on students’ use of generative artificial intelligence tools (Dela Cruz, 2025) and adapted to fit the present study context. This scale captured the extent to which learners meaningfully integrated AI into their learning and creative processes, reflecting both frequency of use and functional engagement. It operationalized the environmental stimulus within Bandura’s (2001) social cognitive framework and served as the exog­ enous predictor in the structural model (H1–H2).

The Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) Scale was developed with reference to Zim­ merman’s (2002) three-phase model and Pintrich et al.’s (1991) MSLQ. This scale measured learners’ goal setting, monitoring, and reflective adjustment. SRL func­ tioned as a behavioral mechanism through which AI tool use was hypothesized to foster critical thinking (CT) (H1, H3, H5).

The Innovative Behavior (IB) Scale was adapted from established measures of innovative behavior in educational and organizational contexts (Ayoub et al., 2023; Ng & Lucianetti, 2016). This instrument measured learners’ tendencies to gener­ ate original ideas, synthesize diverse resources, and implement novel solutions. IB operated as the behavioral conduit linking AI engagement to creative integrity (CI), reflecting learners’ transformation of technological affordances into responsible cre­ ative outputs (H2, H4, H6).

The guiding questions explored learners’ cognitive experiences during AI-assisted learning, the strategies they used for selfregulation and innovation, and their reasoning about originality, attribution, and the ethical use of AI-generated content.

All instruments were reviewed by experts and revised to ensure their suitability for the present AI-assisted learning context. They were subsequently pilot-tested and refined to enhance item clarity, contextual relevance, and participant comprehen­ sion. Their psychometric properties, including reliability and validity indices, are reported in “Measurement model assessment” section. Although implemented within design education, these measures were designed to capture key behavioral processes involved in AI-assisted learning, including self-regulation, innovation, critical evalu­ ation, and ethical reflection. Therefore, they provide a context-sensitive measurement basis for examining human–AI interaction within design-related higher education.

Data analysis strategy

To balance the rigor of model validation with the openness required for interpret­ ing complex psychological processes, this study adopted a multi-layered analytical strategy integrating quantitative modeling, qualitative exploration, and cross-modal synthesis.

Quantitative phase. We conducted partial least squares structural equation model­ ing (PLS-SEM), which is well suited for testing emerging theoretical models involv­ ing multiple mediators and complex pathways, as reflected in the dual-mediation framework proposed in this study (H1–H7). All PLS-SEM analyses were conducted using the final valid sample of 384 respondents. Reliability and validity were exam­ ined using Cronbach’s α, composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE), HTMT ratios, and Fornell and Larcker (1981), while collinearity (VIF), effect sizes (f2), and predictive relevance (Q2 via PLSpredict) were also assessed. Both the measurement and structural models were evaluated to ensure not only statistical adequacy but also substantive theoretical insight.

Qualitative phase. Interview transcripts were imported into NVivo for thematic analysis. Using axial coding and constant comparative techniques, we extracted recurring cognitive, behavioral, and ethical patterns that illustrated how learners interacted with AI tools as cognitive partners, enacted self-regulation and innova­

tive behavior, and negotiated authorship and creative responsibility. These themes captured the negotiation of human–AI agency and were explicitly mapped onto the structural pathways identified in the quantitative phase, thereby reinforcing or prob­ lematizing the hypothesized relationships.

Integrated interpretation. Adopting a sequential explanatory strategy (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018), we synthesized the two data streams. This integration went beyond simple triangulation: qualitative findings contextualized, extended, and occa­ sionally challenged the quantitative model, helping to explain why certain mediation paths (e.g., AI → SRL → CT) were more robust and how subjective experiences com­ plicated the translation of AI affordances into critical thinking and creative integrity. By linking quantitative results with qualitative accounts, we clarified how SRL and IB operate as behavioral mechanisms in human–AI interaction.

Through this combined strategy, the analysis not only validated the proposed dual-mediation model but also deepened understanding of the dynamic learning loop through which AI-assisted tools interact with self-regulation, innovation, and ethical reasoning. This dual-layered approach ensured that the study contributes not just statistically supported relationships but also a context-rich, theory-driven narra­ tive about how human–AI interaction fosters—or constrains—critical and ethically grounded creative capacities across AI-mediated learning and professional contexts.

Results

To establish a foundational understanding of the sample’s distribution across the pri­ mary constructs, we first conducted a descriptive statistical analysis, summarizing learners’ engagement with AI-assisted design tools, self-regulated learning (SRL), innovative behavior (IB), critical thinking (CT), and creative integrity (CI). As this value is well above the midpoint (3.0), it reflects both high utilization and a relatively narrow distribution. This finding echoes prior evidence that generative AI tools are becoming embedded as routine cognitive and creative scaffolds in learning workflows (Hwang & Wu, 2024; Lee, 2025; Lee et al., 2025).

Such divergence reso­ nates with H1, H3, and H5, implying that learners’ ability to treat AI as a cognitive amplifier depends heavily on their regulatory maturity (Faza & Lestari, 2025; Xu et al., 2026; Zimmerman, 2002).

Students also exhibited a relatively strong tendency toward innovative behavior (M = 3.95, SD = 0.68). Creative integrity (CI) yielded a mean of M = 3.84 (SD = 0.69), indicating moderate awareness of original­ ity and ethical considerations, though high-frequency AI use may blur sensitivity to authorship and attribution boundaries (García-López & Trujillo-Liñán, 2025; Omran Zailuddin et al., 2024; Peters & Angelov, 2025; Qadhi et al., 2024). This nuance sup­ ports H4 and H6, suggesting that innovative behavior must be coupled with reflective practices to sustain value-driven creativity.

Taken together, these descriptive patterns reveal heterogeneity in learners’ psycho­ logical and behavioral profiles despite shared technological exposure. This variation underscores the necessity of examining how AI tool use interacts with SRL and IB to yield divergent outcomes. It also supports the theoretical rationale of Social Cog­ nitive Theory’s triadic interplay, in which environmental stimuli, personal regula­ tion, and enacted behavior dynamically shape higher-order competencies (Bandura, 2001). In this study, these patterns provide context-specific evidence for understand­ ing how AI-assisted learning may relate to cognitive and ethical development within design-related higher education.

Measurement model assessment

To ensure the robustness of the measurement framework and verify construct valid­ ity and reliability, we conducted measurement model assessment using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM). Following the guidelines of Hair et al. (2021) and Leguina (2015), the evaluation encompassed several key criteria: indicator reliability (outer loadings), internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α and composite reliability, CR), convergent validity (average variance extracted, AVE), and discriminant validity (Fornell–Larcker criterion and heterotrait–mono­ trait, HTMT, ratios (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Hair et al., 2021)).

In particular, the Q2 values for SRL (0.411), IB (0.567), CT (0.379), and CI (0.374) suggest that the model had meaningful pre­ dictive capacity beyond statistical fit alone. This pattern indicates that AI-assisted tool use, when considered alongside students’ self-regulated learning and innovative behavior, offers a useful framework for explaining higher-order learning outcomes in AI-mediated design contexts.

Qualitative research design and interview strategy

To complement the quantitative analyses and uncover the cognitive processes and ethical judgments that numerical models could not fully capture, this study employed a qualitative follow-up phase within the explanatory sequential design (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018). The aim was to probe more deeply into how students, during AI- assisted design tasks, exhibit self-regulated learning mechanisms, innovative behav­ iors, and context-sensitive interpretations of creative integrity—elements critical to testing and contextualizing the dual-mediation model (H1–H7).

Purposeful sampling strategy

Participants for the qualitative phase were selected using purposeful sampling to ensure that the cases represented both conceptual extremes and behavioral diversity. Two criteria guided selection:

(1).	 Students scoring at the upper or lower extremes on self-regulated learning and

creative integrity constructs, based on the survey data; (2).	 Students exhibiting high variance in AI tool usage frequency and innovative

behavior scores.

A total of 18 students from multiple universities’ design-related programs partici­ pated in this phase, comprising 12 primary interviewees and 6 additional follow-up cases selected to provide comparative and confirmatory insights. This composition ensured a balanced distribution of gender and year level, as well as practical experi­ ence with tools such as Midjourney, ChatGPT, and Notion AI, allowing the study to capture both reflective and applied perspectives.

Interview protocol and structure

Semi-structured interviews were designed to balance consistency with depth, incor­ porating exploratory prompts and follow-up probes. The questions were organized around three focal domains, each tied to the study’s theoretical framework and hypotheses:

(1)	 Learning Cognition in AI-Assisted Design

● What is your typical process when using AI tools for design tasks? ● How do you interpret AI feedback? What aspects do you find helpful or limiting?

(2)	 Strategies for Self-Regulation and Innovation

● When AI outputs conflict with your ideas, how do you adapt? ● Do you feel AI helps you develop new methods or creative strategies?

(3)	 Ethical Reflection and Responsibility in AI-Generated Content

● How do you decide what counts as AI-generated versus your own work? ● Are you concerned about originality when using AI? How do you manage citation, adaptation, or attribution?

All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using thematic coding. Open and axial coding were employed to iden­ tify recurring cognitive, behavioral, and ethical themes, which were then mapped onto the structural pathways established in the quantitative phase. To enhance cred­ ibility and interpretive reliability, two coders independently reviewed the transcripts, compared coding decisions, and resolved discrepancies through iterative discussion until consensus was reached.

Qualitative findings and thematic interpretation

To contextualize and extend the structural relationships identified in the quantita­ tive model, qualitative analysis was conducted with 18 participants (including the 12 interviewed and 6 additional follow-up cases), focusing on cognitive strategies, creative enactment, and ethical awareness in AI-mediated design learning. Through iterative coding, three core themes—comprising nine subcategories—emerged, each directly linked to the mediating mechanisms and outcomes specified in H1–H7. Theme 1: AI as a Cognitive “Mirror” for Thought Formation

Many students described AI-generated images or text drafts as external triggers that stimulated ideation rather than replaced it. As P14 articulated:

I’m not great at sketching concept ideas, so I let AI throw out a rough version first. Then I tweak it, and that helps me figure out what I actually want.

This perspective frames AI not merely as a production tool but as a reflective surface that helps students clarify nascent ideas (Schön, 1983). In relation to H1 and H3, such cognitive dialogues with AI activate self-monitoring and strategic planning, amplify­ ing students’ SRL capacities. Yet, this mirror effect is contingent: without reflective habits, AI’s convenience can slide into dependency. As P13 admitted:

Sometimes the AI’s results are good enough that I just stop there—it feels like turning in someone else’s homework.

This phenomenon of “cognitive outsourcing” mirrors the quantitative finding that some students exhibit high AI usage but low SRL, underscoring that AI alone does

not equate to learning gains. The transformation of AI into a cognitive amplifier hinges on learners’ regulatory and evaluative engagement (H5). Theme 2: Dynamic Regulation and Strategic Transformation in Creative Behavior

Students frequently portrayed AI as a catalyst for iterative experimentation and collaborative synthesis, shifting between “trial–revision–collaboration” cycles. As P01 explained:

I’ll run an AI-generated draft, share it with friends or my group, and we all add to it—like building a design platter together.

Such accounts expand the operationalization of innovative behavior beyond solitary output to encompass collective negotiation and adaptive design practices, resonating with H2 and H6.

Furthermore, students emphasized AI’s role in lowering the emotional and mate­ rial costs of failure, thereby encouraging creative risk-taking. P16 noted:

Before, I hated revising—I’d just abandon a design if I messed up. Now AI can test ten variations for me, so I’m willing to play around and then refine my own version.

Here, innovation is not just novelty but involves behavioral resilience and emotional recalibration, deepening the conceptual bridge between innovation and ethical cre­ ation (H7). Theme 3: Ethical Reflexivity Amid Ambiguity and Context

Students demonstrated a spectrum of context-dependent ethical reasoning regard­ ing AI-generated content, shifting from ambiguity to self-determined standards. P15 captured situational sensitivity:

I’ll check where the AI output came from—I don’t want copyright problems, especially for competitions or publications.

Such cases illustrate situated ethical practices, aligning with prior research that frames creative integrity as a dynamic, context-driven construct rather than a static trait (Omran Zailuddin et al., 2024; Peters & Angelov, 2025). These practices rein­ force H4 and H6 by showing how innovation, when tethered to contextual awareness, can translate into value-driven outputs.

Conversely, gaps in institutional guidance exacerbate uncertainty. As P12 remarked:

Our teacher just said, ‘No plagiarism,’ but didn’t clarify if Midjourney images count as plagiarism. It feels unclear.

Such regulatory vacuums force students into “self-negotiated ethics,” where creative integrity becomes a continuous internal dialogue balancing technical proficiency, creative intent, and social accountability. This finding underscores the need for AI-

mediated design contexts to embed explicit ethical frameworks, lest AI innovation amplify anxiety and ethical blind spots (Chiu, 2025; Wiese et al., 2025).

Integrating quantitative and qualitative insights

Bringing together the quantitative pathways and the qualitative narratives, the present findings reveal a learning ecology far more layered than a unidirectional causal chain. While the structural model supports the hypothesized direct and indirect relation­ ships (H1–H7), the qualitative accounts contextualize these statistical associations, illustrating how these mechanisms unfold in the authentic flow of design learning.

Self-regulated learning (SRL), for instance, does not merely manifest as an orderly sequence of goal setting, monitoring, and reflection (Zimmerman, 2002); rather, it materializes through students’ willingness to negotiate with AI outputs—sometimes treating AI as a collaborator in sense-making, other times as a sparring partner to refine ideas. This process resonates with Schön’s (1983) concept of reflection-inaction, in which practitioners think through and revise their actions while engaging with uncertain and evolving situations. In the present AI-assisted learning context, students’ interaction with AI outputs similarly became a reflective dialogue: they interpreted, questioned, modified, and recontextualized machine-generated sugges­ tions while gradually clarifying their own creative intentions. Such dialogic engage­ ment underscores the premise that SRL, as posited in H1 and H5, is less about mechanical strategy deployment and more about adaptive regulation that balances technological affordances with critical discernment (Faza & Lestari, 2025; Xu et al., 2026). Similarly, innovative behavior (IB) emerges not as a static index of novelty but as a dynamic interplay of agency, resilience, and ethical positioning. Students who actively repurposed AI-generated suggestions, integrated peer input, and confronted uncertainty embodied IB as a multidimensional capability, aligning with H2, H4, and H6. Their actions suggest that innovation in AI-supported contexts is insepa­ rable from emotional and moral considerations. This interpretation is consistent with prior research indicating that innovative behavior is not merely a matter of generating novel ideas but also involves motivation, responsibility, self-efficacy, and meaningful implementation (Dogra & Parrey, 2024; Islam et al., 2025). These qualitative insights deepen the structural logic by showing that IB is not only a mediator channeling AI’s effects toward creative integrity (CI), but also a site where technical exploration intersects with value-oriented reflection.

This integration amplifies Bandura’s (2001) assertion that learning is not a pas­ sive reception but a process of agentic construction, where the value of AI lies in its capacity to catalyze intertwined processes of regulation, innovation, and ethical reflexivity. It also complicates the narrative: while AI can accelerate ideation and scaffold execution, its affordances can just as readily tempt over-reliance or dilute the reflective depth that underpins critical thinking (CT) and CI. By weaving qualitative nuance into the quantitative framework, this study not only affirms the predictive coherence of H1–H7 but also situates these hypotheses within a context-rich narra­ tive that reflects the ambiguities and tensions of human–AI symbiosis.

Discussion

This study examined how AI-assisted design tool use is associated with undergradu­ ate students’ learning processes and ethical practices in AI-mediated design contexts, using a mixed-methods approach that integrated PLS-SEM modeling and qualitative interviews. The findings suggest that AI does not simply enhance learning outcomes; rather, it restructures how cognition, behavior, and ethics are co-constructed through learners’ interactions with AI-generated inputs. By focusing on the mediating roles of self-regulated learning (SRL) and innovative behavior (IB), this study shows that the educational significance of AI lies not only in what it enables students to produce, but also in how it reshapes the regulatory, innovative, and ethical processes through which students think, act, and create.

Behavioral pathways of regulation and innovation

Quantitative results confirmed that AI tool use was significantly associated with both SRL (H1) and IB (H2), suggesting that students who frequently engaged with plat­ forms such as ChatGPT and Midjourney were more likely to demonstrate strategic learning adjustment and creative ideation. These findings align with Zimmerman’s (2002) assertion that learner agency is driven by cognitive feedback loops and with recent research indicating that generative AI can support creativity and critical think­ ing when used as a catalyst for reflection and design exploration rather than as a shortcut for task completion (Lee, 2025; Lee et al., 2025).

Qualitative evidence further contextualized these associations. Rather than treat­ ing AI merely as an automated content generator, many students described it as a resource for clarifying ideas, reframing design problems, and stimulating alternative directions. At the same time, their reflections also revealed the risk of over-reliance when AI-generated outputs appeared sufficiently polished or immediately usable. These patterns suggest that AI can activate reflective regulation and intentional cre­ ativity, but only when learners are supported by appropriate scaffolding. Without such support, AI-mediated learning may encourage superficial acceptance rather than critical engagement.

Together, these results highlight SRL and IB as behavioral mechanisms that enable learners to translate AI engagement into higher-order outcomes. Within AI-mediated design contexts, AI appears most educationally valuable when it supports students’ capacity to plan, monitor, revise, experiment, and ethically evaluate their creative decisions. Rather than claiming broad generalizability, these findings provide con­ text-specific evidence that may inform future studies of human–AI collaboration in other learning and professional settings.

Mediating pathways of SRL and IB

The findings indicate that SRL and IB functioned as complementary mediating mechanisms (H5–H7). This suggests that AI’s educational value does not rest in its technological affordances alone, but is linked to learners’ regulatory and innovative enactment. SRL was strongly associated with CT (H3), while IB was associated with

CI (H4), indicating that cognitive regulation and creative enactment represent dis­ tinct but complementary pathways. This study extends prior research by conceptu­ alizing generative AI not merely as a technological tool, but as a behavioral and psychological catalyst associated with cross-level mechanisms of self-regulation and innovation.

Exploratory analyses also revealed two additional indirect effects: AI → SRL → CI and AI → IB → CT. Although these pathways were not hypothesized a priori, they are theoretically meaningful. SRL’s association with CI suggests that metacogni­ tive monitoring may also strengthen students’ ethical discernment, especially when they must decide how to evaluate, modify, or acknowledge AI-generated content. Conversely, IB’s association with CT indicates that adaptive experimentation may enhance evaluative reasoning by requiring students to compare alternatives, justify design choices, and refine AI-assisted outputs.

Qualitative narratives enriched this mediation logic by showing that students did not simply accept AI outputs but often reconstructed them through sketching, peer discussion, revision, and contextual adaptation. Such practices suggest that SRL and IB may mutually reinforce one another: regulatory persistence supports sustained revision, while innovative experimentation expands the range of possible solutions. Collectively, these findings position SRL and IB as complementary behavioral path­ ways associated with cognitive and ethical outcomes in AI-assisted design learning.

Creative integrity as ethical negotiation

The mediation effect of AI → IB → CI emphasized that creative integrity does not arise automatically from AI use. Rather, it requires deliberate transformation, attribu­ tion, and reflective judgment regarding AI-generated outputs. Passive adoption may leave ethical awareness underdeveloped, whereas recomposition, contextual adapta­ tion, and transparent acknowledgment can foster stronger responsibility.

Students’ accounts illustrated an emergent process of ethical negotiation. In some cases, students became more attentive to attribution when preparing work for com­ petitions, public display, or formal submission. In other cases, uncertainty emerged because institutional or classroom guidelines did not clearly define how AI-generated images, text, or design suggestions should be acknowledged. These examples reveal a regulatory gap in which learners must rely on personal judgment and informal norms to balance originality, accountability, and social context.

These findings echo recent calls for rethinking ethics education in the AI era (Chiu, 2025; Wiese et al., 2025). Rather than treating integrity as a simple binary between plagiarism and originality, creative responsibility should be understood as the capac­ ity to declare, justify, and critically evaluate the degree of AI involvement in creative work. Pedagogically, this implies a shift from rule-based moral instruction toward dialogic, context-sensitive ethical scaffolds. Within design education, such scaffolds may help students transform AI from a source of convenient output into a partner for reflective, responsible, and critically informed creativity.

Theoretical contributions

This study makes three theoretical contributions to research on human–AI collabo­ ration in higher education. First, it reframes generative AI from a passive tool or external learning environment into a quasi-agentic collaborator that participates in learners’ cognitive regulation, creative exploration, and ethical decision-making. This perspective extends Social Cognitive Theory by showing that AI-mediated envi­ ronments may function not only as contextual stimuli but also as interactive partners within reciprocal learning systems.

Second, this study introduces self-regulated learning and innovative behavior as dual behavioral pathways through which AI engagement is translated into higherorder outcomes. Rather than treating AI use as directly beneficial, the findings sug­ gest that its educational value depends on learners’ capacity to regulate, reinterpret, and transform AI-generated inputs. In this sense, SRL and IB constitute a co-regu­ lated system linking AI affordances with critical thinking and creative integrity.

Third, this study expands the concept of integrity from academic honesty to cre­ ative integrity. In AI-mediated design contexts, integrity is not limited to avoiding plagiarism; it involves originality, attribution, transparency, contextual judgment, and responsible co-creation. This reframing provides a broader ethical lens for under­ standing how learners negotiate authorship and accountability in human–AI creative processes.

Together, these contributions point toward a broader theoretical shift from toolbased accounts of AI use to an AI-augmented co-regulation perspective, in which learning outcomes emerge through the dynamic interaction among AI affordances, learner agency, behavioral transformation, and ethical judgment.

Limitations and future directions

This study integrates structural modeling and qualitative inquiry to advance under­ standing of how AI-assisted tool use is associated with students’ regulation, innova­ tion, and ethical judgment in design-related higher education. Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted.

First, the sample was limited to students enrolled in design-related programs, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. Although the model offers useful insights into how AI engagement is associated with self-regulated learning, inno­ vative behavior, critical thinking, and creative integrity, these relationships should not be assumed to operate identically across all disciplines. Future studies should examine the proposed framework in other academic domains and cultural contexts to determine whether similar behavioral mechanisms emerge under different learning conditions.

Second, the study relied on cross-sectional SEM data and retrospective interviews, which limited its ability to capture real-time regulatory, emotional, and behavioral processes. Students’ interactions with AI are likely to evolve across tasks, feedback cycles, and stages of learning. Future research could employ longitudinal designs, learning analytics, process-tracing methods, or digital trace data to examine how

self-regulation and innovative behavior develop dynamically in AI-mediated learn­ ing environments.

Third, the model focused primarily on self-regulated learning and innovative behavior as mediating mechanisms. Although these constructs were theoretically grounded and empirically supported, other variables may also shape the relation­ ship between AI use and higher-order outcomes. Future studies could incorporate constructs such as AI literacy, digital trust, epistemic vigilance, design thinking, or ethical sensitivity to provide a more comprehensive account of the human–AI–ethics ecosystem.

Fourth, the contextual scope of this study was limited to higher education settings. The ethical and practical implications of AI-assisted creativity may differ substan­ tially in professional and workplace environments, where creative outputs are subject to client expectations, commercial value, legal accountability, intellectual property regulations, and reputational consequences. In such high-stakes contexts, creative integrity may no longer function only as an internal reflective capacity but also as an externally evaluated professional responsibility. Similarly, self-regulation and innovative behavior may shift under workplace pressures, where efficiency, original­ ity, authorship, and ethical compliance must be negotiated simultaneously. Future research should therefore test the model in commercial design studios, professional creative industries, and other workplace settings to examine whether the pathways identified in this study remain stable or are reshaped by institutional, legal, and mar­ ket constraints.

By acknowledging these limitations, future research can refine the theoretical pre­ cision, empirical scope, and practical relevance of the proposed framework. Such efforts may help clarify the conditions under which AI integration supports, rather than undermines, critical, innovative, and ethically grounded forms of creativity.

Conclusion

Generative AI is not merely a technological addition to design education; it increas­ ingly participates in how learners conceptualize creativity, regulate their learning processes, and negotiate ethical responsibility. This study developed and validated a dual-mediation framework explaining how AI-assisted engagement is associated with self-regulated learning (SRL), innovative behavior (IB), and, ultimately, critical thinking (CT) and creative integrity (CI). By integrating PLS-SEM modeling with qualitative thematic analysis, the study identified both direct and indirect pathways through which AI engagement may be linked to cognitive and ethical growth.

Consistent with the structural model, AI engagement was associated with both SRL and IB, which were in turn associated with CT and CI (H1–H4). These outcomes do not stem from technological adoption alone; rather, they are linked to learners’ reflective regulation and purposeful innovation. SRL and IB functioned as comple­ mentary behavioral pathways associated with higher-order outcomes (H5–H7), underscoring students’ capacity to convert AI affordances into strategic actions and value-driven decisions. These findings extend Bandura’s (2001) principle of triadic

reciprocal determinism by showing how AI-mediated affordances, cognitive regula­ tion, and behavioral enactment converge in contemporary learning environments.

Qualitative evidence enriched this model by illustrating how these mechanisms unfolded in authentic contexts. Students described AI as both a “cognitive mirror” and a “dialogic partner,” helping them refine ideas and develop creative self-aware­ ness, while also revealing risks of dependency in the absence of pedagogical scaf­ folds. These accounts highlight that creative integrity, as a defining literacy in the AI era, extends beyond plagiarism avoidance; it requires the active construction of responsibility throughout the creative process (Omran Zailuddin et al., 2024; Peters & Angelov, 2025).

Theoretically, this study contributes by reframing AI from a passive tool into a quasi-agentic collaborator embedded within learners’ self-regulatory, innovative, and ethical processes. It also integrates AI tool use, SRL, IB, and CI into a processoriented framework that foregrounds behavioral pathways between AI affordances and higher-order outcomes. Practically, the findings suggest that AI integration alone does not guarantee meaningful learning; pedagogies must embed reflective regula­ tion, deliberate innovation, and contextualized ethical engagement to support AI- mediated criticality and creativity.

In essence, generative AI amplifies—rather than replaces—the imperatives of agency and responsibility in creative practice. The central challenge is not how widely AI is adopted, but how effectively learners engage with AI as a reflective and generative partner while making deliberate, responsible, and imaginative choices amid AI-enabled possibilities. These insights, while grounded in design education, may inform future research on human–AI interaction and the pedagogical, institu­ tional, and ethical governance of AI-mediated creativity.
