1. Introduction

To realize creativity’s diverse benefits, it is essential to develop accessible instructional strategies that engage and cultivate creative thinking skills. This argument has become increasingly prominent among researchers and educators, who commonly highlight creativity as an important 21st century skill with positive outcomes that range from personal benefits (Kaufman, 2023) to economic development (Florida, 2012) to transformative societal growth (Sternberg, 2021). Corre­ spondingly, researchers have called for education systems to purpose­ fully develop student creativity (Beghetto & Kaufman, 2014).

Creativity has been defined as “the interaction among aptitude, pro­ cess, and environment by which an individual or group produces a perceptible product that is both novel and useful as defined within a social context” (Plucker et al., 2004, p. 90). Background: To foster creativity in 21st century education, it is necessary to identify efficient instructional strategies capable of engaging and developing different thinking skills relevant to creativity. Aims: This study examined how the use of sequential thinking prompts and engagement in key creative sub­ processes enhanced creative problem solving. Methods: A sample of 364 university students were randomly assigned to one of four experimental conditions. Whereas control group participants thought about the problem and presented their solution, treatment partici­ pants were provided with sequential thinking prompts emphasizing specific creative subprocesses: anchoring, generation, selection, exploration, and evaluation. Personal characteristics and subprocess engagement (i.e., time spent and words produced) were measured and served as covariates. Results: Treatment group participants produced more creative solutions than control group participants (ds = .42–.52). This effect was fully mediated by subprocess engagement, as measured by time spent on task and the number of words produced. Amotivation was negatively associated with solution creativity. Engagement in the subprocess of anchoring emerged as a prominent, positive predictor of creative performance, highlighting the particular importance of setting goals and identifying constraints. Conclusions: Together, these findings indicate that integrating an extended sequence of thinking prompts can enhance performance in creative problem-solving by increasing task engagement. Although further research is needed to determine optimal prompt types and sequences, such prompts could represent accessible instructional strategies that could stimulate creative thinking in diverse educational settings.

1.1. Dimensions of creativity

Creativity takes place within a particular context (Plucker et al., 2004), which may encompass many different components. One would include the experience and skill level of the creator, which might range from personally meaningful (mini-c) to appreciated by others (little-c) to expert (Pro-c) to genius (Big-C; Kaufman & Beghetto, 2009). Most stu­ dent creativity would fall between mini-c and little-c. Another compo­ nent would be the domain; creativity expressed in writing, mathematics, cooking, and dance, for example, would all be quite different (Baer & Kaufman, 2017). In describing the multidimensionality of creativity, Rhodes (1961) presented four overlapping, interactive strands (i.e., the Four Ps), which offer a way to further explicate creativity; the creator (person) engages in different thinking stages (process) in a particular environment (press) which results in an outcome (product).

Rather than describing creativity as a unidimensional phenomenon, it is important to outline a project’s parameters and specify the di­ mensions that are being explored (Ivcevic, 2022). This study focused on how an experimental manipulation involving sequential thinking prompts (press) and engagement in creative subprocesses (process) predicted the rated creativity of solutions to a creative problem-solving task (product), while including creativity-related personal characteris­ tics as covariates (person). The study involved a sample of college stu­ dents with no specific training (mini-c to little-c) in the domain of everyday creative problem solving.

1.2. The creative process

Green et al.’s (2024) process-based definition describes creativity “as internal attention constrained by a generative goal” (p. 554). Repre­ senting one dimension of the 4P framework, the creative process refers to the dynamic flow of thoughts and behaviors that produce creative outcomes (Lubart, 2018), and different creative process models have emerged throughout the past century. Many models depict the creative process as a flexible sequence of distinct stages, such as problem iden­ tification, information gathering, idea generation, evaluation, and outcome assessment (Amabile & Pratt, 2016); problem construction, information encoding, category search, category specification, category combination and reorganization, idea evaluation, implementation, and monitoring (Mumford et al., 1991); or finding the problem, acquiring knowledge, gathering information, incubating, generating ideas, combining ideas, selecting ideas, and externalizing ideas (Sawyer, 2012). Other models highlight more recursive interactions between creative subprocesses, such as generation and selection (Campbell, 1960) or generation and exploration (Finke et al., 1992). Despite these differences, there is agreement that the broader creative process en­ compasses diverse subprocesses that include—but are certainly not limited to—idea generation. Further research is needed to understand how these diverse subprocesses can be optimally sequenced during creative work, but one observational study indicated that participants commonly moved through cycles involving anchoring (i.e., goal devel­ opment), generation, selection, exploration, and evaluation (Boldt,

Because both theories and empirical evidence indicate the creative process is a diverse, complex phenomenon, the overemphasis on DT in creativity research is lamentable. This said, DT tests and idea generation measures have their value; DT scores modestly predict creative achievement (Said-Metwaly et al., 2022), and producing diverse, orig­ inal ideas can ultimately lead to more creative products (Boldt & Strub, 2023). Yet, researchers have argued that DT tests ignore later creative process stages (Zeng et al., 2011), have limited generalizability (Tromp & Sternberg, 2022), and should not be equated with creativity (Baer, 2011). Mumford and McIntosh (2017) recommended using realistic, ill-defined problems, focusing on specific thinking skills, and examining interactions between divergent and convergent subprocesses.

Following these recommendations, the present study examined a sequence of five creative subprocesses during an authentic creative problem-solving task: anchoring, generation, selection, exploration, and evaluation. Again, there are areas of overlap between DT and CT, but some of these subprocesses are more divergent (generation and explo­ ration), whereas others are more convergent (anchoring, selection, and evaluation). Although this approach excluded certain elements often highlighted in creative process models (e.g., incubation, information gathering, externalization), this specific sequence of subprocess was selected because (a) the number of stages allowed for meaningful comparisons without requiring an excessive time commitment from participants; (b) all stages could be observed during a single data collection session; (c) as detailed in the following sections, studies have shown that each subprocess is represented in different creative process models, emerges spontaneously in creative work, and is predictive of creative performance; and (d) the subprocess sequence mirrors those observed during authentic creative work (see Boldt, 2019).

1.2.1. Anchoring Boldt and Kaufman (2025) proposed the term anchoring to describe moments when creators establish, refine, or reference task-related goals and constraints. Closely related to the problem construction stages featured in different creative process models (e.g., Mumford et al., 1991), anchoring constrains thinking both by setting broad creative goals and by monitoring the aims of specific actions. It occurs consis­ tently throughout the creative process and emerges in domains outside problem-solving. In think aloud studies, individuals regularly structure their thinking by defining and monitoring goals and constraints (e.g., Boldt, 2019), and related statements predicted product creativity in another drawing task (Boldt & Kaufman, 2025). Keith and Jagacinski (2023) found that prompting different goals enhanced creative problem-solving performance. Specifically, whereas outcome-related goals had a direct effect, the effect of process-related goals was medi­ ated by self-reported creative process engagement.

1.2.3. Selection It is not sufficient to merely generate preliminary ideas, as authentic creative work also involves selecting specific ideas to pursue. Cropley (2006) argued for the importance of convergent subprocesses such as decision-making, and selection is highlighted in numerous creative process models (e.g., Mumford et al., 1991). Selective subprocesses emerged throughout long-term workshops with visual artists, where the overall frequency of selection and product creativity were negatively related (Fürst et al., 2012). Another study found that effective concept selection contributed to creative problem-solving performance (Mumford & McIntosh, 2017).

1.2.4. Exploration After generating preliminary ideas, a creator must elaborate upon, expand, or experiment with them. Correspondingly, Finke et al.’s (1992) Geneplore model posits that exploratory phases are needed to elaborate on generative processes. Exploratory subprocesses are common in think aloud studies examining creative tasks such as design (Hay et al., 2017) and drawing (Boldt & Kaufman, 2025). Ruscio et al. (1998) found that statements indicative of exploration predicted poem creativity, and Baughman and Mumford (1995) demonstrated that prompting elabo­ ration enhanced solution originality in category combination problems.

1.2.5. Evaluation Creative process models often underscore the need for creators to critically evaluate their ideas and work (e.g., Mumford et al., 1991). Think aloud studies have shown that evaluative subprocesses emerge spontaneously during tasks such as group problem-solving (Leone et al., 2023). Similar to selection, the relationship between evaluation and product creativity remains unclear, with studies showing both positive (Tolkamp et al., 2023) and negative (Nazzal & Kaufman, 2020) re­ lationships with creativity.

1.3. Developing creative problem-solving processes in education

Meta-analyses have demonstrated the consistent, moderate effects of creativity training programs for adults (Haase et al., 2023) and K–12 students (Ruiz-del-Pino et al., 2022). Benefits are enhanced for programs that target underlying cognitive processes (Sio & Lortie-Forgues, 2024), including relevant elements of DT and CT such as problem identifica­ tion, idea generation, implementation planning, elaboration, and idea evaluation (Scott et al., 2004a). However, educators generally feel ill-prepared to foster student creativity (Mullet et al., 2016), opportu­ nities for sustained professional learning on creative development are rare (Davies et al., 2013), and most educational interventions last weeks or months (Ruiz-del-Pino et al., 2022). Consequently, research is needed to identify accessible, efficient strategies that engage and nurture stu­ dents’ creative thinking.

Although less effective than sustained training programs, prompts that induce DT and CT can also enhance creative output (Haase et al., 2023). Even basic instructions to “be creative” can enhance performance (Forthmann et al., 2019), presumably by motivating individuals to establish creative goals (Di Mascio et al., 2018). Such instructions are more effective when they are explicit and provided during untimed tasks (Said-Metwaly et al., 2020). 1.4. Creativity-related personal characteristics

Research has revealed a range of social, cognitive, motivational, and affective variables associated with creativity (Feist, 1998). Due to the large number of relevant personal characteristics and the time con­ straints inherent in experimental research, a limited number of variables were selected for the present study. Measures of problem-solving self-­ efficacy and experience, motivation, and metacognition were specif­ ically chosen because there is evidence demonstrating their association with creative outcomes, but further research is needed to clarify their role in domain-specific creative thought and production.

Reflecting one’s confidence in their own creative potential and abilities, creative self-efficacy is distinct but related to other creative self-beliefs, such as self-rated creativity and creative personal identity (Karwowski & Lebuda, 2016). Domain-general creative self-efficacy is associated with verbal and figural divergent thinking originality, ratings of drawing and writing creativity (Anderson & Haney, 2020), and the quality and originality of creative problem-solving task solutions (Royston & Reiter-Palmon, 2019). Studies have also shown that general creative self-efficacy is more strongly related with self-rated creativity in domains outside math and science (Boldt et al., 2024) and individuals’ creative self-efficacy is largely domain-specific (Snyder et al., 2021). Because more research has included domain-general measures, the present study assessed the domain-specific influence of creative problem-solving self-efficacy.

Relatedly, higher levels of domain-specific expertise are associated with higher creativity in writing (Fürst et al., 2017), music (Woodward & Sikes, 2015), drawing (Fayena-Tawil et al., 2011), and creative problem-solving (Medeiros et al., 2014) tasks. Other research has shown that the frequency of domain-specific activities is associated with crea­ tive achievement in related domains (Diedrich et al., 2018). A central goal of the present study was to assess the influence of creative sub­ process engagement in creative problem solving. Because novices and experts engage in subprocesses such as problem construction differently (Reiter-Palmon & Robinson, 2009), creative problem-solving experience was measured to control for its influence on process engagement and creative problem-solving performance.

Metacognition (Anderson & Haney, 2020) and self-regulation (Zieli´nska et al., 2023) appear to contribute to creativity, highlighting the importance of monitoring and controlling creative thought pro­ cesses. Dimensions of metacognition are associated with ideational abilities and creative behaviors (Puryear, 2015), and neurological evi­ dence suggests that metacognition is crucial for moderating the activa­ tion of brain networks that respectively contribute to DT and CT (Zhang et al., 2020). However, relatively few studies have used measures like the Mode Shifting Index (MSI; Pringle & Sowden, 2017). This measure was included in the present study to assess how awareness and skill in switching between divergent and convergent modes of thought might influence the creative problem-solving process and its outcomes.

1.5. The present study

With the aim of identifying instructional strategies to stimulate engagement in key creative subprocesses and enhance creative problemsolving performance, this study used an experimental design to manip­ ulate sequential thinking prompts while simultaneously considering the respective influence of creativity-related personal characteristics and engagement across stages of the creative process. Specific research questions included:

RQ1: Do participants guided through stages emphasizing anchoring, generation, selection, exploration, and evaluation produce more creative solutions than control participants who solved the same problems without guidance?

H1a. Treatment group #1 participants will produce more creative solutions than control group participants.

H1b. Treatment group #2 participants will produce more creative solutions than control group and treatment group #1 participants.

H1c. Treatment group #3 participants will produce more creative solutions than control group, treatment group #1, and treatment group #2 participants.

RQ2: Are personal characteristics—including measures of problemsolving self-efficacy and experience, motivation, and meta­ cognition—associated with solution creativity?

H2a. Problem-solving self-efficacy and experience, metacognition, and intrinsic motivation will be positively associated with solution creativity.

H2b. Amotivation will be negatively associated with solution creativity.

RQ3: Within treatment conditions, are higher levels of engagement in anchoring, generation, selection, exploration, and evaluation associated with more creative solutions?

H3a. Time spent during each subprocess stage will be positively associated with solution creativity.

H3b. Words produced during each subprocess stage will be positively associated with solution creativity.

H4a. Total time spent during all creative process stages (e.g., anchoring, generation, selection, exploration, and evaluation) will mediate the effect of treatment assignment on solution creativity.

H4b. Total words produced during all creative process stages (e.g., anchoring, generation, selection, exploration, and evaluation) will mediate the effect of treatment assignment on solution creativity.

2. Methods

2.1. Participants

An a priori power calculation using G*Power 3.1.9.7 indicated that the full sample regression analysis including 10 predictors (three group contrasts and seven covariates), a small-to-medium effect size of .10, α of .05, and power of .95 required a total sample size of 254. The treatment group regression analysis including 21 predictors (two group contrasts, seven covariates, and 12 subprocess engagement measures), a medium effect size of .15, α of .05, and power of .95 required a total sample size of 226. Based on these calculations, 364 participants were recruited from an introductory communications class at a public university in the Northeastern United States, who participated in exchange for course credit. The average age was 19.41 (SD = 1.50). Participants who dis­ closed gender identified as female (n = 187), male (n = 187), and gender variant/nonbinary (n = 2). They predominantly identified as White/ Caucasian (n = 221), Asian/Asian American (n = 56), Latina/Latino/ Hispanic American (n = 32), and Black/African American (n = 23). The most common majors were business (n = 117), STEM (n = 76), and health science (n = 44).

2.2. Measures

2.2.1. Personal characteristics Participants’ creative problem-solving self-efficacy was measured using a 3-item self-report measure adapted from Beghetto (2006). Using a 6-point Likert-type scale, participants stated the extent to which they agreed with three statements (e.g., “I have confidence in my ability to solve problems creatively”). Reliability for the scale was high (α = .88).

Participants’ creative problem-solving experience was measured using a 5-item self-report measure designed for the study. Using a 6-point Likerttype scale, participants stated the frequency in which they engaged in creative problem solving (e.g., “I use creative problem solving in my everyday life”). Reliability for the scale was high (α = .85).

The CTMS (Taylor & Kaufman, 2021) measured participants’ domain-specific motivational patterns. Based on acceptable constructand criterion-related validity evidence, the 20-item self-report scales were designed to be adapted for specific domains. The CTMS measures intrinsic motivation (e.g., “I engage in creative problem solving because of the pleasant sensations I feel”), extrinsic motivation (e.g., “I engage in creative problem solving in order to gain prestige”), and amotivation (e. g., “I engage in creative problem solving although I do not see the benefit in it”). Reliability coefficients were high for intrinsic motivation (α = .91), extrinsic motivation (α = .85), and amotivation (α = .83).

The MSI (Pringle & Sowden, 2017) measured participants’ self-reported ability to switch between associative and analytical modes of thought. The 11-item MSI includes two scales: metacognitive awareness of shifting (e.g., “While working on a task, I go through phases where I do a lot of thinking and other phases where I just sit back and muse over things/take a back seat”) and shifting competence (e.g., “I am good at both figuring things out logically and going with my instincts when deciding on a course of action”). 2.2.2. Creative problem-solving task The experimental manipulation in this study involved a sequential creative problem-solving task, which was delivered through a single, online, asynchronous Qualtrics session. All participants were provided with the following instructions: “In the next sections of this study, you will be asked to solve a problem creatively. You will be guided through a series of stages where you will focus on different parts of the creative problem-solving process. During each stage, you will type your re­ sponses to reflect your thinking.” Participants were then presented with an everyday problem: “You are a student studying at the University of Connecticut. You need to earn $5000 this year to pay for expenses. How could you solve this problem? Try to solve this in a way that would be both creative and possible for a university student (not necessarily yourself).” Like those used in prior problem-solving research (e.g., Royston & Reiter-Palmon, 2019), this specific problem was designed to represent an ill-defined problem that was relevant to participants’ lives and allowed a wide range of potential solutions. Although the problem was perhaps not overly novel, it was selected to align with the typical knowledge and experiences of undergraduate students (see Wigert et al., 2024). After being presented with the problem, participants were randomly assigned to a condition—either control (n = 97), treatment #1 (n = 91), treatment #2 (n = 95), or treatment #3 (n = 81). Thinking prompts, which varied across conditions, were presented in text, and participants were asked to document their thinking by writing in corresponding text boxes. Control condition participants were asked to complete two openended stages in which they (a) thought about how to solve the problem and (b) described their final solution.

To examine factors beyond the mere number of prompts, we exam­ ined the influences of engagement in specific experimental sub­ processes. Two measures assessed participants’ level of engagement during each stage of the task: (a) amount of time spent and (b) number of words produced. Although these reflect imperfect proxies for task engagement, both time (Tolkamp et al., 2023) and word count (Joy, 2008) have been used as engagement measures in creativity research. Due to the varied number of prompts and the open-ended nature of the task, overall engagement was not equivalent across conditions. Descriptive statistics for the total duration of the task (in minutes) for each condition were as follows: control (M = 7.36, SD = 7.87), treatment #1 (M = 26.17, SD = 19.53), treatment #2 (M = 27.93, SD = 25.20), treatment #3 (M = 33.55, SD = 30.95). Descriptive statistics for the total number of words produced were as follows: control (M = 133.08, SD = 100.37), treatment #1 (M = 425.49, SD = 205.08), treatment #2 (M = 417.89, SD = 240.21), treatment #3 (M = 431.57, SD = 275.91). The design of these different stages was intended to balance speci­ ficity and feasibility. It would have been optimal to experimentally manipulate each of the five target subprocesses; however, we did not have access to the number of participants required for such a design. Although our approach did not permit causal interpretations regarding the effects of anchoring, generation, or selection, the stage-specific engagement measures allowed us to assess the respective influence of each stage on solution creativity. We ultimately chose to focus manip­ ulations on exploration and evaluation stages, as these subprocesses have been particularly neglected in prior research. Using a similar design, Boldt and Strub (2023) found that manipulating prompts to engage in exploration, evaluation, and goal refinement did not improve performance on an open-ended drawing task. The present study sought to build on these findings by examining whether (a) more detailed prompts involving strategies for effective subprocess use would yield stronger effects and (b) sequential thinking prompts would be more impactful in a more structured domain such as creative problem solving. The control stages for exploration (justification) and evaluation (expression) were designed to prompt on-task behavior without specif­ ically teaching participants about the target subprocesses and stimu­ lating engagement in them.

For all three treatment conditions, participants were first asked to complete three stages: (a) anchor the problem, (b) generate potential solutions, and (c) select a solution. At this point, instructions for the next two stages varied across treatment groups. Treatment group #1 partic­ ipants completed two control stages in which they justified their selec­ tion and then described the emotions they experienced during the task. Treatment group #2 participants also justified their selection but were then asked to evaluate their solution. Treatment group #3 participants were asked to explore and then evaluate their solution. For target sub­ process stages (anchoring, generation, selection, exploration, and eval­ uation), participants were provided with both a description of the subprocess and strategies for applying it effectively. Descriptions and strategies were not provided for control stages (think about the problem, justification, and expression). During the last stage, all participants regardless of condition were asked to describe their final solution. The solutions presented in the final stage of the task were rated for their creativity using Amabile’s (1996) consensual assessment technique (CAT), a reliable and valid method that is widely considered to be the gold standard for assessing product creativity (Baer & McKool, 2009). Six quasi-expert judges served as raters, consistent with established protocol (Kaufman & Baer, 2012). This included five graduate students enrolled in a doctoral program focused on creativity, giftedness, and talent development. As a program requirement, each of these judges had taken coursework specifically addressing theories of creativity (e.g., the Five As; Glǎ;veanu, 2013) and frameworks for assessing creativity (e.g., scoring divergent thinking tests; Reiter-Palmon et al., 2019). The final judge was an advanced honours student who had completed extensive coursework and independent studies on creativity. Judges were pro­ vided with a description of the problem-solving task and then asked to independently rate each solution for its creativity on a 1–6 scale ranging from “not creative at all” to “very creative”. In line with CAT procedures, judges did not receive extensive training. Rather, they were asked to rate solutions using their own subjective criteria for creativity, compare response to one another rather than to an external standard, and use the full range of the rating scale. 2.3. Procedures

Data for the study was collected in a single, online, asynchronous session hosted through Qualtrics. Following a fixed sequence, partici­ pants provided consent and then completed the creative self-efficacy questionnaire, creative problem-solving task, creative problem-solving experience questionnaire, MSI, CTMS, and a brief demographic ques­ tionnaire. 2.4. Analysis

Missing data for personal characteristics measures (missingness = 1 %) were imputed using R’s Amelia package (Honaker et al., 2022). Preliminary analyses indicated that scores on these measures did not vary across conditions (ps > .10). However, total time on task and total words produced varied across conditions (ps < .01), with control con­ dition participants spending less time on task and producing fewer words than those in each of the three treatment conditions. To address these potential confounds, the mediation analysis (RQ4) examined treatment effects while controlling for task engagement.

A regression analysis including condition (control condition set as reference group) and personal characteristics was used to assess the overall effects of treatment prompts on solution creativity (RQ1) and the respective influences of personal characteristic measures (RQ2). A hi­ erarchical regression analysis including only treatment group partici­ pants assessed RQ3. With solution creativity as the outcome measure, condition (treatment group #1 set as reference group), personal char­ acteristics, and time and word count were entered at step 1. Time and word count were entered separately for each stage to assess each sub­ processes’ respective influence on solution creativity. Then, we indi­ vidually added condition-by-stage interactions to examine whether engagement in exploration and evaluation stages influenced solution creativity differently than engagement in control stages (i.e., justifica­ tion and expression). For both models, analyzing partial plots and Cook’s distance coefficients revealed no significant concerns regarding assumptions of linearity and homoscedasticity or influential cases. These analyses were performed using R version 4.1.2.

3. Results

Descriptive statistics and correlations for all measured variables are presented in Table 2. Notably, all measures of personal characteristics, with the exception of amotivation, were significantly intercorrelated (ps < .05). 4. Discussion

This study sought to clarify how sequential thinking prompts could enhance creative problem-solving performance by stimulating engage­ ment in key creative subprocesses. Treatment group participants who received an extended series of thinking prompts targeting creative subprocesses, which included anchoring, generation, selection, explo­ ration/justification, and evaluation/expression, outperformed

H2a was not supported, as no personal characteristic measures correlated positively with solution creativity, nor were these measures positive predictors in the regression analyses. However, amotivation was negatively associated with solution creativity in both the pre­ liminary correlation analysis and the whole-group regression analysis, providing full support for H2b.

Aside from a marginal trend involving time in the evaluation/ expression stage, time spent and words produced during each stage were positively associated with solution creativity, providing partial support for H3a and full support for H3b. Within regression analyses, time spent during the anchoring stage emerged as the strongest and most consistent predictor of solution creativity, whereas amotivation was the only sig­ nificant predictor among personal characteristics measures.

A sequential mediation analysis demonstrated that overall engage­ ment—measured as total time on task and total words produced—fully mediated the effects of both treatment prompts and amotivation. This provided full support for H4a and H4b, highlighting the critical role subprocess engagement plays in creative problem solving.

4.1. Sequential thinking prompts

Relative to the control condition, all treatment groups produced solutions that were rated as more creative. With small-to-moderate ef­ fect sizes ranging from .42 to .52, these treatment effects align with Haase et al.’s (2023) meta-analysis, which revealed an average effect size (Hedge’s g) of .45 in studies using similar manipulation methods. Although slightly lower than the effects of more extensive training ef­ forts (g = .61, Haase et al., 2023; g = .53, Sio & Lortie-Forgues, 2024), these results add to the considerable body of evidence demonstrating that creativity can be enhanced using efficient, targeted thinking prompts that guide individuals through divergent and convergent pha­ ses of the creative process (e.g., Wigert et al., 2024).

More specifically, the present findings indicate that a more pro­ longed and elaborate series of stages eliciting engagement in key crea­ tive subprocesses is more effective than basic instructions to think about a problem and then solve it. Meta-analyses have indicated that effect sizes for sustained training and short-term manipulations remain

The observed lack of differences across treatment conditions indi­ cated that specifically encouraging stages of exploration and evaluation had no effect, compared to control stages involving justification and expression. These control prompts were designed to stimulate on-task behavior without eliciting target creative subprocesses. Yet, explora­ tion and evaluation prompts were ultimately unsuccessful, aligning with other research demonstrating their null effects relative to control prompts (Boldt & Strub, 2023). Given that creative subprocesses spon­ taneously emerge in rapid, dynamic sequences, not discrete, linear stages (Boldt & Kaufman, 2025; Van Hooijdonk et al., 2023), it is likely that relevant subprocesses also arose during control stages, potentially diminishing the effects of exploration and evaluation stages. Alterna­ tively, justification and expression could themselves be helpful activities in creative problem solving, further attenuating differences across treatment groups. Because treatment group participants were prompted to engage in more stages than control group participants, and the spe­ cific treatment group did not differentially affect solution creativity, it appears that the number of instructional prompts may be more im­ pactful than their specific nature. Given the limitations inherent in the present study’s design, further research is needed to determine the in­ dependent effects of the instructional prompts.

Perhaps the most novel aspect of the present study was its mediation analysis, which illuminated causal mechanisms underlying the effects of sequential thinking prompts. Specifically, the treatment exclusively worked by stimulating higher levels of task engagement, as measured by the overall time participants spent on task and the total number of words they produced throughout the task. Time (Tolkamp et al., 2023) and word count (Joy, 2008) have previously been used as proxies for engagement, but to our knowledge, no previous research has included both and assessed their function as a mediator. Keith and Jagacinski (2023) offered parallel results in which self-reported creative process engagement mediated the effect of prompts eliciting the formulation of general and specific creative process goals (with goal formulation, aligning with the present stage of anchoring, also found to be influen­ tial). Adding to this evidence, the present findings provide a more extensive measure of creative process engagement, while similarly demonstrating that task instructions indirectly benefit creative perfor­ mance by enhancing engagement in the creative process. Given the sparsity of studies that have used such techniques, further research is needed to replicate and extend these findings, as well as to explore the independent effects of creative subprocess prompts above and beyond extended task duration and elaboration.

4.2. Personal characteristics

Nearly all the personal characteristics measured were uncorrelated with solution creativity, including problem-solving self-efficacy, problem-solving experience, shifting competence, metacognitive awareness of shifting, intrinsic motivation, and extrinsic motivation. These results were unexpected, as prior research has connected creative problem-solving performance with self-efficacy (Royston & Reiter-Palmon, 2019), expertise (Medeiros et al., 2014), and facets of motivation (Besançon et al., 2015) and metacognition (Puryear, 2015). Creative problem-solving self-efficacy and expertise were not measured using validated instruments, and the brief scales generated for this study may have failed to adequately measure these constructs. Although the CTMS and MSI are validated instruments, relatively few studies have incorporated them, and further research is needed to assess their rela­ tionship with authentic creative task performance. These findings could

Amotivation was the only exception to this pattern. It was initially correlated with solution creativity (r = −.12) and predicted solution creativity in the whole group regression model (β = −.13). Extensive evidence has shown that intrinsic motivation (r = .20) and, to a lesser degree, extrinsic motivation (r = .11) are associated with measures of creativity (Costa et al., 2015). However, fewer studies have explored the role of amotivation. Amotivation scores on the academic motivation scale have been found to correlate negatively with both convergent-integrative creativity on the Evaluation of Potential of Creativity test (r = −.23; Besançon et al., 2015) and performance on different verbal DT tasks (rs = −.20 to −.11; Urban et al., 2021). We are unaware of other studies that have examined relationships between domain-specific amotivation and authentic creative performance mea­ sures, and these preliminary findings suggest that within the present sample, amotivation had a notable negative relationship with creativity.

The sequential mediation analysis revealed a pathway from amoti­ vation to creativity ratings that parallelled the observed treatment ef­ fect; individuals scoring higher on amotivation tended to produce fewer words throughout the task, thereby negatively influencing their creative problem-solving performance. Although self-reported creative process engagement has been found to mediate the relationship between crea­ tive self-efficacy and supervisor ratings of employee creativity (Li et al., 2020), the present study may represent the first effort to explore mediation pathways involving amotivation, more direct measures of task engagement, and rated creative problem-solving performance. This pattern of findings has important implications for educational contexts, in that creative problem solving at the mini-c/little-c level could be influenced more by amotivation than by intrinsic motivation, and the key function of motivation appears to be to stimulate overall task engagement.

Given the wording of items in the CTMS’s amotivation scale (e.g., I engage in creative problem solving “although I do not see the benefit in it,” “even though I do not have a good reason for doing so”; Taylor & Kaufman, 2021, p. 12), combatting the negative influence of amotiva­ tion might involve communicating the real-world value of creative problem solving. However, within the context of the present study, amotivation may have also been conflated with participants’ willingness to engage in extended reflective writing. Furthermore, because the CTMS was administered after the creative problem-solving task, it is possible that amotivation scores were influenced by participants’ reac­ tion to the cognitively demanding task. This said, amotivation scores did not vary across conditions, F(3, 360) = 1.58, p = .195, indicating that the more demanding treatment conditions did not lead to higher amo­ tivation levels. Ultimately, further research is needed to replicate and extend these preliminary findings.

4.3. Creative subprocess engagement

Whereas other analyses examined global treatment effects and the influence of overall task engagement, the second regression analysis investigated the specific influence of engagement in each of the target subprocesses. The most significant insight to emerge was the powerful influence of the anchoring stage. During this stage, participants were prompted to develop task-related goals by considering available re­ sources, articulating criteria for success, and anticipating barriers and constraints. Even when controlling for condition, personal characteristic measures, and engagement in all other stages, time spent anchoring was positively associated with solution creativity (β = .18).

Although anchoring occurs throughout the creative process (Boldt & Kaufman, 2025), the present study situated this stage a single time at the beginning of participants creative work, aligning with longstanding conceptualizations of problem-finding/-identification/-construction

The other significant predictor in this regression model was word count during the solution presentation stage (β = .61). As this measure reflected solution length, the strength of this coefficient is perhaps un­ surprising. Elaboration is a common subscale in DT tests (Reiter-Palmon et al., 2019), and aligning with this approach, the present findings showed that more elaborate solutions were generally rated as more creative. Indeed, the influence of word count at this stage was so strong that it made all other engagement measures insignificant, aside from time spent anchoring. However, when removing solution presentation stage engagement measures from the analysis, words produced during the exploration/justification (β = .20) and evaluation/expression (β = .17) stages became stronger predictors of solution creativity. Ruscio et al. (1998) similarly found that engagement in exploration and eval­ uation correlated with product creativity, but it was unexpected that these relationships would not differ from engagement in control sub­ processes in the present study. Specifically, follow-up models exploring potential interactions revealed no differences between the respective influences of exploration and justification, or between evaluation and expression. Again, this may have occurred because justification and expression were similarly helpful, or because participants spontaneously engaged in exploration and evaluation regardless of the prompts that were provided, but additional research is needed to test these specific effects.

Contrastingly, engagement in generation and selection stages was consistently unrelated to solution creativity. Even when removing engagement in the solution presentation stage from the model, time spent and words produced in the generation and selection stages were unassociated with creativity ratings. The influence of selection remains somewhat unclear, as previous studies have found negative and positive relationships that vary across measurement approaches (Fürst et al., 2012; Mumford & McIntosh, 2017). Null findings regarding the gener­ ation stage, however, were unexpected. Idea generation is heavily emphasized in the measurement (Long, 2014) and training (Scott et al., 2004b) of creativity, despite ongoing concerns about its over­ representation in creativity research (Ivcevic, 2022). In this study, engagement in generation appeared less impactful than engagement in other subprocesses, particularly anchoring. Boldt and Kaufman (2025) similarly found that engagement in anchoring was a stronger predictor of creativity than generation in a drawing task think aloud.

Authentic creative work, which can include creative problem solv­ ing, involves much more than generating preliminary ideas. The present findings add to the ever-expanding body of evidence demonstrating that creative process research would benefit from moving beyond its his­ torical fixation on idea generation and exploring the respective in­ fluences of a wider range of impactful creative subprocesses, including both divergent and convergent modes of thought.

Although this study offers several novel contributions to creative process research, several key limitations must be acknowledged. First, the overall research design emphasized authenticity. Yet, many aspects

Second, due to the varied number of prompts across control and treatment groups and the lack of meaningful differences across treat­ ment groups, it is unclear whether these specific prompts enhanced creativity, or whether engagement and creativity were generally higher in these conditions because they included more stages. Future research might develop approaches to control for this confound (e.g., creating an equivalent number of control stages or imposing time restrictions). It would also be beneficial to manipulate each of the target subprocesses to assess their individual effects on solution creativity.

Third, to facilitate data collection within a single, time-efficient session, we chose to measure a select number of subprocesses and per­ sonal characteristics. It would be meaningful for future research to explore the respective influences of other creative subprocesses (e.g., associative thinking, incubation, externalization) and creativity-related personal characteristics (e.g., openness, emotional intelligence, domain knowledge and achievement).

Fourth, the latent creativity variable used in the mediation analysis may have been influenced by differential rater biases. Although reli­ ability was high and the sensitivity analysis revealed consistent findings across models, future research could explore approaches to explicitly modeling rater effects.

Fifth, other than the creative self-efficacy questionnaire, personal characteristics were measured after the creative problem-solving task. Although these measures were intended to capture relatively stable traits, participants’ responses may have been influenced by their re­ actions to the creative problem-solving task. Future research examining the mediating role of process engagement should consider this possi­ bility and administer questionnaires prior to creative tasks.

Finally, given the specific parameters of this study, inferences may not extend to creative performance in domains beyond everyday prob­ lem solving or populations beyond untrained college students studying in the United States. More research is needed to not only determine the stability of the observed relationships, but also whether they are generalizable to other domains (e.g., art) and levels (e.g., Pro-c) of creativity.

4.5. Implications for research and practice

Despite these limitations, this study provided novel evidence demonstrating that—when compared to a typical, unguided instruc­ tional approach—integrating an extended series of thinking prompts can enhance creative problem solving by increasing overall task engage­ ment. Requiring minimal time and resources, the thinking prompts used in this study taught participants about different subprocesses, provided heuristics for effective subprocess engagement, and guided them through the creative problem-solving process. Complementing findings from previous research, these results highlight the instructional poten­ tial of sequential creative thinking prompts, which could be easily in­ tegrated in diverse instructional contexts. The widespread use of this instructional strategy could help students engage in the creative process and improve their creative performance; however, further research is needed to determine how these effects vary across different prompts, tasks, populations, and instructional contexts.
