Abstract

Background: As the significance of artificial intelligence (AI) continues to increase,

there is a need for effective scaffolding and support for novice learners. Educators

have encountered challenges in effectively scaffolding novice learners AI concepts,

and providing appropriate motivational support. Research evidence has shown the

potential of game-based approaches to fostering secondary school students' AI liter-

acy and motivation to learn AI.

Objectives: This study developed an online platform TreasureIsland to gamify ebooks

and investigated whether and how students playing with it can effectively enhance

their AI literacy. This study aims to contribute an empirical and theoretical basis for

AI literacy education and promote the use of gamification that would be broadly

applied in other schools.

Methods: A quasi-experiment was conducted to evaluate the effects of the proposed

gamified approach, which included a control group using an ebook with playful

resources. To triangulate the quantitative results obtained from the pre and post-test,

focus group interviews were also conducted.

Results: The platform was effective in improving students' motivation, self-efficacy,

career interest, and understanding of AI concepts and ethics, but did not enhance

their confidence of using AI, and high cognition of applying, evaluating and creating

AI. TreasureIsland players demonstrated significant improvement in all affective and

cognitive domains, except for the ability to apply, evaluate, and create AI. Interviews

revealed that the gamified approach could promote students' AI literacy by adhering

to guidelines, including (1) creating a competitive and motivating learning environ-

ment through game mechanics, (2) providing scaffolding modules and feedback, and

(3) visualising complex AI concepts via simulations. Feedback collected from the study

suggested adding pedagogical elements such as flipped classrooms and project-based

1 | INTRODUCTION

As the impact of artificial intelligence (AI) on the world grows, there is

a rising need to develop the next generation's AI literacy

(UNESCO, 2022). AI is predicted to replace jobs, but at the same time,

new jobs related to technology will be generated, making students

who effectively master AI concepts and tools more valuable in their

future workplace. It is important to prepare students to become

responsible citizens who can use and apply AI technologies ethically

and efficiently (Chiu et al., 2021; Druga et al., 2019; Long &

Magerko, 2020; Touretzky et al., 2019).

AI literacy has become a new set of digital literacy skills that refer

to the skills and competencies for using AI technologies and applica-

tions. Frameworks (e.g., ISTE, DigCompEdu) have updated the latest

standards that inform schools of necessary digital knowledge, skills,

and attitudes that help students engage confidently, critically, and

safely with AI technologies (Su et al., 2022; Vuorikari et al., 2022).

Schools around the world have started to incorporate AI into their

curricula to develop students' AI literacy in a comprehensive and sys-

tematic manner (Chiu et al., 2021; Tedre et al., 2021; Touretzky

et al., 2019; Williams et al., 2023; Xia, Chiu, & Chai, 2022;

Yang, 2022).

Educators recognise the importance of adopting effective peda-

gogies to impart AI into the curriculum. Recent studies reported chal-

lenges of learning AI and the need to design appropriate strategies to

effectively develop students' AI literacy (Celik et al., 2022; Xia, Chiu,

Lee, et al., 2022). For example, novice learners may find traditional

classroom-based instruction unengaging and expect to be entertained

to sustain their motivation to learn outside of class. Other common

challenges include making complex AI concepts less intimidating and

providing scaffolding support (Yang, 2022; Zhang et al., 2023).

Research has demonstrated that tool-based approach and gamifi-

cation are promising in fostering students' digital literacy that instil a

sense of affective and cognitive engagement (Akgun &

Greenhow, 2022; Alam, 2022; Guo & Goh, 2016; Hsu et al., 2023).

For example, young learners have previously encountered various

challenges when learning computational thinking (CT) concepts. By

using age-appropriate tools, such as simulations and gamified plat-

Although experiential learning approaches offer potential benefits

for addressing needs to learn AI, the use of gamification is relatively

2 | LITERATURE REVIEW

To address the learning challenges of AI literacy and develop an effec-

tive instructional design, this section first presents existing curricula

and identifies challenges of learning AI. Second, the idea of educa-

tional tools for fostering students' AI literacy was examined, and gami-

fication is further discussed to understand possible design principles

that inform educators of effective approaches to develop AI literacy

instruction.

2.1 | AI literacy

Existing studies have explored what secondary students should learn

about AI (e.g., Kandlhofer et al., 2016; Chiu et al., 2021; Furey &

Martin, 2019; Williams et al., 2023). Many initiatives, policies, curric-

ula and teaching guidelines provide frameworks to suggest the age-

appropriate topic areas of K-12 AI curricula. For example, Touretzky

et al. (2019) organised discrete AI knowledge and skills as the five big

ideas in the AIK12 initiative, including perception, representation and

reasoning, learning, natural interaction, and societal impact. UNESCO

(2022) covered three major categories, including AI foundations;

ethics and social impact; and understanding, using and developing

AI. Chiu et al. (2021) designed an AI curriculum framework that

includes introduction and fundamentals to AI; how AI sees, hears,

speaks and reads; AI reasoning, simulating, thinking and creating;

social good, social impacts and challenges of AI, AI and ethics, and AI

and future of work. Casal-Otero et al. (2023) discussed learning tech-

nical, conceptual, and applied skills in a specific domain of interest,

with a particular focus on efforts to design models that frame AI liter-

acy proposals.

While these frameworks are promising, they tend to prioritise

instructional content over learning environments and pedagogies that

support students' needs. According to prior research, students often

networks and AI algorithms (Long et al., 2021; Ng et al., 2021). Sec-

ond, it is challenging for novice learners to learn AI without prior

knowledge due to its high-level abstractions, and lack scaffolding sup-

port to learn AI (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Ng, Leung, et al., 2023). It

also involves students' high cognition levels of AI learning, including

critical thinking, and problem-solving in real-world context

(Yang, 2022), collaboration with AI, and creativity (Taddeo & Floridi,

2018). Tedre et al. (2021) described the challenges that students face

in AI literacy courses, such as the shift from rule-based to data-driven

environments, and the need for age-appropriate pedagogical entry

points.

Ng et al. (2021) adapted Bloom's taxonomy and categorised AI

curriculum contents from low to high-order thinking tendency. Based

on the model, students should achieve (1) the ability to know and

understand basic AI concepts; (2) apply, evaluate and create AI appli-

cations; and equip with (3) the ethical competence that critically eval-

uate AI in different situations. This study is based on this model to

examine students’ cognitive engagement.

2.2 | Tools for developing AI literacy

Nowadays, there are more age-appropriate tools that help students

develop students' AI concepts, and engage students in experiencing

how to develop machine learning models without programming

knowledge. For example, Touretzky et al. (2019) identified some edu-

cational tools such as Teachable Machine, Quickdraw, and Scratch

extension that raise students' interest in creating their own AI crea-

tions. Voulgari et al. (2021) developed Artbot that uses a constructiv-

ist approach to introduce secondary students to AI learning content,

providing opportunities for exploration, experimentation, and reflec-

tion. Pope et al. (2023) surpassed Google's Teachable Machine to

develop GenAI Teachable Machine, which enabled students to link

classifier results to actions using text, sound, and pictures. Other AI

applications such as ChatGPT-empowered bots, Google AI experi-

ments, and Moral Machine are also publicly available for students and

are recommended in the ebook to familiarise young learners with AI

technologies, experiment with machine learning models, and gain

practical insights into the workings of AI mechanisms (Ali et al., 2022;

Formosa & Ryan, 2021; Kasneci et al., 2023; Vartiainen et al., 2020).

With these AI technologies, young learners can communicate with AI

via natural language and computer vision technologies, without prior

programming experience (Vartiainen et al., 2020).

Developing AI literacy involves not only understanding technical

concepts, but also being able to apply them in real-world contexts,

and complement with other high order thinking skills (e.g., creativity,

evaluation). With teaching tools, teachers can incorporate develop-

mentally appropriate experiences to attract students to learn AI, and

promote deeper understanding of AI concepts. Further, these tools

provide students with opportunities to conceptualise and experiment

with AI, as well as reflect on what they have learned through experiproject-based activities, which aligns with sociocultural learning

(Vartiainen et al., 2020) and design-oriented and collaborative peda-

gogy (Vartiainen et al., 2021). The tools also help alleviate cognitive

challenges and promote the development of higher-order thinking

skills, including interdisciplinary thinking, collaboration, critical think-

ing, and real-world problem-solving (Sakulkueakulsuk et al., 2018;

Henry et al., 2021).

2.3 | Gamification

Gamification is the process of incorporating game mechanics and

design principles to non-game contexts. It refers to the incorporation

of gaming mechanics, such as points, badges, challenges, leader

boards, honours, and incentives, into educational contexts to motivate

students to engage more deeply and meaningfully (Werbach

et al., 2012). Gamification for education is based on the notion that

well-designed games can inspire increases in learner efficiency and

creativity (Flores, 2015). The MDE model is considered as one of the

most effective mechanisms for transferring games into non-game con-

texts. The three elements of design that make gamification effective

are mechanics (M), dynamics (D), and emotions (E) (Robson

et al., 2015). Teachers adopt MDE to satisfy students' needs that drive

learning behaviours by providing its goals, environments, interactions,

and rules. The motivational nature of the gamified experience could

result in game dynamics that satisfy learners' desire for reward,

achievement, and competition. Such gamified dynamics can cause par-

ticipants to feel a variety of emotions, such as interest, motivation,

self-efficacy and satisfaction, confidence in overcoming a challenge,

disappointment, and anxiety (Robson et al., 2015). Using MDE can

cultivate students' digital literacy (Reynolds, 2016), which also sheds

light on the potential to develop students' AI literacy.

Given the complexity of and the prerequisite technical back-

ground knowledge required to comprehend AI, it is challenging to

learn AI, especially for K-12 students (Bennani et al., 2022). Since

there remains a lack of empirical studies exploring the specific roles of

gamification in enhancing AI literacy among students, we further

added game elements such as challenges, rewards, and feedback in a

gamified platform called TreasureIsland as an intervention. By doing

so, we aim to make the learning experience more engaging and inter-

active to meet students' affective and cognitive needs, potentially

increasing student motivation and improving AI learning outcomes.

Two learning environments, TreasureIsland and ebook, were devel-

oped to enhance students' AI literacy in affective and cognitive

domains. TreasureIsland is a hands-on platform based on the success-

ful gamified platform called Reading Battle, while ebook includes

videos, learning tools, readings, and practice activities. Gamified chal-

ence and language learning resources with reference to the Robson's

MDE model (Qiao et al., 2023).

The units cover foundational AI concepts, practical skills, and AI

ethics. Each module includes reading materials, videos, tools, and

gamification tasks to help students understand AI. Later modules

include high-order thinking activities to help students apply AI tech-

niques and explore different AI tools for analysis, creation, and pro-

ductivity. Appendix S1 displays 10 learning units based on the Ng

et al. (2021) framework for teaching AI literacy. Ethical principles such

as fairness, accountability, and transparency are also introduced to

promote responsible AI literacy.

3.1 | Ebook in control group

For the control group, we utilised an ebook platform that included

learning activities from the AI literacy curriculum, with the intended

learning outcomes presented in Appendix S1. To ensure student

engagement, we selected teaching tools that aligned with Montazami

et al. (2022) five criteria: curriculum, development team, feedback,

scaffolding, and pedagogical approach. For instance, we incorporated

AI-for-Ocean from Code.org, which was designed to captivate stu-

dents by involving them in the creation of simple ML models to clean

the ocean. The activities within this tool have clearly defined learning

goals, such as using AI to address ocean pollution, and complement

the units covered in the AI curriculum. Second, this tool was devel-

oped by a team of experts, and previous research has shown its effec-

tiveness in catering to students' developmental abilities (Martins

et al., 2023; Qiao et al., 2023; Williams et al., 2023). To provide guid-

ance and support, the tool offers real-time feedback to steer students

towards making the correct choices. The tool includes scaffolding ele-

ments like reading resources, scenarios, videos, animations, and

music/sound. As students make progress through the tool, they are

gradually introduced to more challenging tasks, such as identifying

and categorising fish shapes while distinguishing between garbage

and actual fish. Lastly, this teaching artefact can be implemented

within pedagogical frameworks such as project-based learning and

experiential learning (Martins et al., 2023; Williams et al., 2023).

Other tools, including QuickDraw, Teachable Machine, Moral

Machine, and Google AI Experiments, were also chosen to provide

students with an engaging learning experience to learn AI conceptual

and practical content. The choices of these tools in the ebook were

based on learners' cognitive development, and the instructions enable

students to play at their own pace after school (Figure 1). Quizzes

were provided at the end of the module to enable students to practice

their AI knowledge.

AI (N = 33, control group) whereas another class used TreasureIsland

quantitative results obtained from the pre and post-test, focus group

4.2 | Instruments

Pre-test questionnaires were used to gather demographic information

and measure students' baseline self-reported affective and cognitive

engagement about learning AI (Appendix S2). Post-test questionnaires

were given at the end of the last lesson to measure the effectiveness

of the ebook and TreasureIsland. The questionnaire was validated in a

previous study with an overall Cronbach's alpha value of 0.91 (Ng et al.,

2024). Follow-up interviews were conducted with teachers, students,

and experts to ensure questionnaire reliability.

We designed an AI knowledge test (Appendix S3) with multiple-

choice questions of similar difficulty to the TreasureIsland and ebook

quizzes. The questions focus on recalling basic AI knowledge, applying

and evaluating concepts, and knowing ethical principles. The questions

were designed based on the ebook content and guidelines for designing

multiple-choice questions, and were verified as relevant by teachers.

Focus group interviews were conducted with students to elaborate

their views on the gamified platform and ebook design, and how gamifi-

cation offers knowledge, affective and cognitive support to develop

their AI literacy (Appendix S4). These topics can help better understand

students' learning experiences and triangulate the quantitative results.

The interviews help to understand how the elements of TreasureIsland

and ebook can influence students' lower to higher order thinking skills.

5 | RESULTS

5.1 | Improvement of learning outcomes (RQ1)

First, the preand post-test scores were compared to investigate the

change of the students' level of engagement. Mann–Whitney U tests

on the pretest scores showed no statistically significant difference

between the two groups on any of the measures (p > 0.05). There-

fore, both groups were considered equal in terms of their prior knowl-

edge of the AI content and levels of engagement.

A nonparametric test was used since the data was not normally

distributed (p < 0.05 in the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and skewed

histograms). Therefore, Wilcoxon signed-rank test (a nonparametric

equivalent of the dependent t-test) was used when the assumptions

had been violated and the use of the dependent t-test is inappropriate

(Çakıroglu et al., 2017). The test results showed a significant improve-

ment from the preto post-test results for affective engagement

(Table S3), cognitive engagement and AI knowledge (Table S4).

In the ebook group, the mean scores of the post-test have

improved in intrinsic motivation, and self-efficacy (p < 0.05), except

for career interest and confidence, which showed a marginally signifi-

cant improvement (p = 0.05). With regards to cognitive engagement,

there was a significant improvement of the two cognitive domains

(i.e., know and understand; apply, evaluate and create), AI ethics, and

AI knowledge. The results of the TreasureIsland group indicated that

students significantly improved in all of the affective constructs. Simi-

lar to the ebook group, TreasureIsland players showed improvements

in the two cognitive domains, AI ethics, and AI knowledge.

6.2 | Cognitive engagement

6.2.1 | Know and understand

Twenty-seven students reported that the task-based activities

enabled them to construct AI knowledge. It was found that both the

ebook and TreasureIsland interventions effectively improved students

tested and self-reported AI knowledge and understandings. For

ebooks, games provided a supportive environment for experimenta-

tion and visualisation of complex AI concepts. Students were able to

develop practical skills and learn from their mistakes while completing

tasks like generating ML models and stylising images. In TreasureIs-

land, gamified elements were further incorporated into the ebook to

address the challenges of learning AI. The structured and guided

learning experience broke down complex concepts into modules and

gamified practices, making it easier for students to understand the

material. The platform also encouraged students' motivation through

positive competitions and gamified experiences. Instant feedback and

recognition further built cognitive support for continued learning. Stu-

dents claimed, ‘The materials are appropriate and enable me to get

the key ideas about AI’ and ‘The modules in the platform make com-

plicated AI concepts into smaller, and more manageable chunks.’

Other students stated, ‘I realised that gamification can be an

effective way to enhance my AI understanding. Before attending the

workshops, I had no knowledge about AI. After the workshops, I have

gained an understanding that AI involves building ML models, and the

use of data for object/voice recognition.’

6.2.2 | Evaluate and create

Students' abilities to evaluate and create AI were not enhanced by

either TreasureIsland or the ebook. Several potential issues were sug-

gested by the students. First, the tasks in the ebook and gamified plat-

form were found to be ‘black-boxed’, with students following

instructions without fully understanding the underlying principles.

While students could use AI tools to complete tasks, they did not

understand the concepts and algorithmic thinking behind them. Creatdents to continue learning AI as they grow and learn more advanced

AI-related mathematics and computer principles. A student commen-

ted, ‘There is still much AI knowledge I can't understand. I think I may

learn it in the future.’

Second, students raised time constraints as an issue. They sug-

gested extending lesson times to allow for more hands-on experience.

AI learning involves many practical skills, and creating and evaluating

AI artefacts, such as machine learning model building, can be time-

consuming. Teachers often prepare semi-finished or designated prod-

ucts, limiting students' ability to create their own work. Even in the

gamification condition, evaluating and creating involves high-order

thinking, and self-paced learning in the gamified platform may not

enable students to reach the cognitive level required.

Third, a teacher proposed the use of project-based learning that

helps students build collaboration, critical thinking and problem-

solving skills through co-creation and competitions (e.g., learn how to

work together using AI tools, collaborate with AI in a competition as a

team, and solve real-world problems together using AI). A teacher sug-

gested, ‘Using the gamified flipped classroom approach can help stu-

dents study basic concepts at home before attending in-person

lessons to learn advanced skills. This would allow for more lesson time

to engage students in project-based learning and hands-on activities,

while reducing direct instruction time.’ These suggestions highlight

the need for a cognitive and structured approach to teaching AI,

focusing on understanding the underlying principles and providing

students with sufficient time and scaffolding support to develop their

AI literacy.

Twenty-three students appreciated the use of TreasureIsland to

them to think critically about the concerns and limitations behind

AI technologies. A student mentioned, ‘… Teachers further lead

cerns behind AI.’

7.1 | Gamification as an affective support

AI concepts may pose learning challenges for young learners due to a

disparity between their everyday experiences and the abstract nature

of AI concepts. Through playful experiences, students can sustain

their motivation when playing TreasureIsland than when learning with

an ebook. By leveraging game mechanics, students could compete

with their peers, earn digital badges and points, reach higher leader-

board positions based on a storyline, all of which help their retention.

They feel more connected to what they have learnt and stay focused

on their learning.

As suggested by recent research, AI education aims to develop

students to become literates rather than consumers (e.g., Chiu

et al., 2021; Ng et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2022). We argue that Treasur-

eIsland begins to achieve this goal in a way by giving students the

opportunity to set their own goals and complete tasks on the plat-

form. Gamified platforms like TreasureIsland could gamify AI concepts

and served as a starting point to motivate students to become ready

to learn AI (Hsu et al., 2021; Ng & Chu, 2021).

In particular, TreasureIsland listed students' names on the

leaderboard and gave additional points for challenging questions.

Leaderboard was one of the most prominent game elements that

engage students in learning (Zainuddin et al., 2020). It announced

the top 15 students on the leaderboard and led to a controllable

competition and points could help determine whether students are

more active than their peers. Well-designed gamified platforms

should offer modules that give students autonomy and choice in

their learning, allowing them to take ownership of their AI literacy

development and explore topics that interest them (Li &

Chu, 2021). Providing timely and constructive feedback can help

students reflect their responses in the tasks, and encourages them

to continue learning and exploring (Qiao et al., 2023). Attali and

Arieli-Attali (2015) found that using multiple gamification tech-

niques motivates student learning. This includes providing motiva-

tion, vicarious experiences, social persuasion, and emotional

support. Gamification can help students feel confident in learning

AI and provide a scaffolding environment for hands-on tasks.

Challenging tasks build on foundational knowledge and enable stu-

dents to experiment with AI applications. The platform also offers

real-world scenarios to enable students to discover how AI is used

practices in various industries, such as gaming, creativity, and environ-

mental protection, students can explore career paths such as data

analyst, ML engineer, and AI researcher. Gamification has been shown

to improve student performance and confidence in AI learning

through positive competitive and motivating environments with lead-

erboards, badges, and points, which aligns with prior studies (Huang &

Hew, 2018; Lo & Hew, 2020).

7.2 | Gamification as a cognitive support

TreasusreIsland players had better AI knowledge and cognitive

engagement than those who learned with ebooks only due to the

platform's scaffolding supports and gamified content (Qiao

et al., 2023). Students found the ebook contents challenging and diffi-

cult to sustain learning without teacher support, whereas TreasureIs-

land encouraged after-school learning through gamification.

Gamification has the potential to facilitate students' self-

regulated learning using game elements (Landers et al., 2017; Qiao

et al., 2023). Students could compare the level they were on and

the total points they had to their peers. When students reached a

new level and achieved particular rewards and badges, it could

credit them for what they had accomplished. Ebook did not have

the same structure, as there were no rewards and levels. It was

difficult to constantly keep track of their progress (Zainuddin

et al., 2020). Leaderboard recorded the points that the students

gained, providing timely feedback and cognitive hints of their per-

formance (Jong et al., 2018; Zainuddin, 2018). With the profile

provided by the leaderboard, students could monitor their pro-

gress, which encourages them to sustain their effort. It enabled

students to see that they can manage more advanced AI concepts

by showing them the progress of other classmates via positive

competition.

Gamification can reduce cognitive challenges for novice learners

studying technologically driven content, such as AI, by leveraging

visual attractiveness and interactive simulations (Darejeh et al., 2021;

Kim & Shim, 2022; Su & Zhong, 2022). Gamification can improve stu-

dents' understanding of AI concepts through visual indicators of pro-

gress, clear objectives and goals, and immediate feedback.

Competitions can also boost motivation and build a sense of commu-

nity. Guided learning structures can break down complex AI concepts

into modules, making it easier for students to scaffold and digest the

material (Kim & Shim, 2022).

To become AI literate, students should not only focus on basic

knowledge and ethical principles, but also critically evaluate AI-

generated responses and collaborate with AI to solve problems

(Borenstein & Howard, 2021; Furey & Martin, 2019). However, no

significant differences were found in higher-level cognition between

preand post-interventions for both approaches, possibly due to the

use of multiple-choice questions and learning tasks. Interviews sug-

gested that authentic, project-based, and problem-solving elements

collaboration.

7.3 | Relations between engagement and AI knowledge

The study found that affective engagement sub-variables were corre-

lated with each other, with high motivation associated with high

career interest, self-efficacy, and confidence. Similarly, AI knowledge

was linked to both lower and higher-order cognitive skills, indicating

that students with stronger thinking skills tended to perform better in

AI knowledge. These findings align with prior research indicating that

affective and cognitive factors are interrelated, and students with

higher cognitive engagement tend to perform better (Çakıroglu

et al., 2017; Hanus & Fox, 2015; Ng & Chu, 2021). Additionally, stu-

dents with higher confidence in using AI had better AI knowledge,

highlighting the importance of building students' confidence to

improve their AI literacy.

AI ethics is correlated with affective domains, as ethical decision-

making and behavioural outcomes are influenced by people's affective

reactions to situations. Our interviews with students revealed that

empathy and inclusiveness can inform ethical views on AI, with stu-

dents providing examples of how AI can assist visually impaired indi-

viduals. This aligns with recent research that links ethics and

motivation to the inherent reason for behaving morally (Schaltegger &

Burritt, 2018; Young & Durwin, 2013).

7.4 | Practical implications and contributions of the study

The significance of this study can be understood in two aspects. First,

it is crucial to examine the specific approach of gamification in the

context of AI literacy education. The findings of this study suggest

that gamifying learning experiences is not only beneficial in traditional

subject areas but also in AI literacy education (Qiao et al., 2023; Zhao

et al., 2021). Furthermore, the complex and technical nature of AI

requires a unique exploration to determine the effectiveness of gami-

fication in fostering AI literacy among young learners. Gamifying

ebooks for AI learning can enhance students' motivation, self-efficacy,

career interest, confidence, and lower-level cognitive skills through

elements such as competition and scaffolding.

Second, this study is part of our design-based research that aims

to investigate effective learning strategies to motivate students to

sustain their AI learning at home, thus allowing for more in-class time

for engaging in maker projects within a flipped classroom setting

(Qiao et al., 2023). Recent studies in AI education have demonstrated

the effectiveness of playful experiences in enhancing AI literacy

through immersive scenarios, case-based discussions using role-

playing games, and guidance and feedback provided through AR-

based games (Deng et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2023). These studies

8 | CONCLUSIONS

AI literacy has become an essential component of digital literacy, and

schools are designing pedagogies to address learning challenges

and develop students' necessary competencies. Gamification has been

recognised as a successful pedagogical strategy across disciplines. This

study concludes that the use of gamification can provide affective and

cognitive support and an enjoyable experience for learners. Recom-

mendations for educators are listed in Appendix S5.

However, this study has some limitations. First, the sample

size of this study was small. Future studies could involve larger

samples. Second, while this paper mainly focuses on self-reported

affective and cognitive engagement, future studies should investi-

gate actual dimensions of learning that employ more comprehen-

sive learning about AI, including the actual knowledge and skills of

using AI applications (e.g., interacting with sensors, learning analyt-

ics). Third, this article does not adopt a novel approach to teach

students about AI, which may weaken the significance of this

study as there have been many existing studies examining gamifi-

cation in educational settings. Even if the study does not intro-

duce a groundbreaking new approach, it could still have value in

exploring the application of existing pedagogical strategies, like

gamification, to the domain of AI education. To address the chal-

lenges of teaching AI literacy education, it is important for future

researchers to continue exploring other innovative approaches to

prepare students for the AI-ready future. Fourth, one limitation of

this study is that it may only provide a superficial discussion that

covers too many details for the gamified ebook intervention. This

study's broad scope, covering ebook design, game mechanisms,

and content, may limit the in-depth analysis that is typical of

more focused academic work. However, as an initial cycle of a

design-based research approach, this study is comprehensive for

developing a learning experience around AI education and obtain-

ing valuable lessons learned. Though the broad focus limits deep

exploration in any one area, it provides a foundational basis for

future design-based research with a narrower scope to build upon.

We hope that the research evidence in this study can timely help

provide lessons learned that inform educators to design appropri-

ate instructions to teach AI, especially nowadays when many

countries/regions are developing and refining their AI curricula

and pedagogies.
