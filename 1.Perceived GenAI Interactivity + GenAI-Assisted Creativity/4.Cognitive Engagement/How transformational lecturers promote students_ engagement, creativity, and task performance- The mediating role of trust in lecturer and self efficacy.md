1. Introduction

Today's employees face high levels of uncertainty concerning career management and employability (Ng & Feldman, 2014). Thus, developing employability competencies is a crucial success factor for employees (Guzman de & Choi, 2013). Besides task performance, organizations and research scholars have focused on the development of employee engagement and creativity (George & Zhou, 2002; Kühnel, Sonnentag, & Bledow, 2012). Employees' engagement is positively related to employees' active learning, in-role as well as extra-role performance (Bakker, Demerouti, & Brummelhuis, 2012; Salanova, Agut, & Peiró, 2005), and financial returns (Xanthopoulou, Bakker, Demerouti, & Schaufeli, 2009b). Employees' creative ideas and high performance build the basis for organizational innovation and success (Madrid & Patterson, 2016; Oldham & Cummings, 1996). Recently, the call for these career relevant concepts has reached the domain of vocational development and educational psychology (Qureshi, Wall, Humphries, & Bahrami Balani, 2016; Upadyaya & Salmela-Aro, 2015). Practitioners as well as researchers claim that we should foster engaged, creative, and performance-oriented behavior at the workplace but also in educational institutions and particularly in higher education (Craft & Jeffrey, 2008;

In today's fast changing and increasingly complex environments the promotion of ongoing employees' task and contextual performance is an important issue. Thus, this longitudinal study investigates the impact of transformational teaching which is derived from transformational leadership concept on students' motivational states (course-related self-efficacy and trust in teacher) and subsequently on students' increased engagement, creativity and task performance in higher education. The study (N = 165) hypothesized positive changes in university students' motivational states and performance criteria over a period of six weeks. Mediation analyses showed that trust in lecturer but not students' course-related self-efficacy served as a mediator in the relationship between transformational teaching and an increased degree of study engagement, an increased degree of student creativity, and task performance. Our results offer theoretical and practical implications for the development of career relevant concepts and teaching in higher education.

Contrary to school context, students in higher education experience higher workload, larger class sizes, and a more distant teacher-student relationship (Lowe & Cook, 2013). At the same time students in higher education must shape their learning processes independently (Hassel & Ridout, 2018; Murtagh, 2010). Thus, the question of how university lecturers can support learning-related attitudes, skills and behavior is of critical importance. Therefore, in our study we investigate how transformational teaching–a promising teaching style derived from transformational leadership concept–and its motivational impact predict changes in students' engagement, creativity, and task performance over the course of one semester.

1.1. Transformational teaching and motivational states

Transformational leadership is probably the most well-established leadership style up to date. Studies have linked it to numerous positive follower outcomes such as job satisfaction (Braun, Peus, Weisweiler, & Frey, 2013), thriving (Niessen, Mäder, Stride, & Jimmieson, 2017), or inand extra-role performance (Gang, Oh, Courtright, & Colbert, 2011; Judge & Piccolo, 2004; Kuonath, Specht, Kühnel, Pachler, & Frey,

Although first studies with school children indicate that transformational teaching is linked to beneficial outcomes, such as students' self-efficacy or autonomous motivation (Beauchamp, Barling, & Morton, 2011; Wilson et al., 2012), to the best of our knowledge, this relatively new teaching concept has not been studied in the context of higher education, yet. This is although transformational teaching seems especially apt to the context of higher education as it empowers students as independent learners.

We expect transformational teaching to predict study motivation in higher education by addressing two central motivational states which Hannah, Schaubroeck, and Peng (2016) describe as “reason-to” and “can-do” motivations. More specifically, we propose trust in lecturer to be the “reason-to motivation” and course-related self-efficacy to be the “can-do-motivation” (Raub & Liao, 2012). In a further step, we expect both motivational states to mediate the association between transformational teaching and three central student outcomes - study engagement, student creativity, and task performance (see Fig. 1).

1.1.1. Transformational teaching and trust in lecturer In the past, organizational research has already acknowledged the important role of interpersonal trust for the functioning of individuals and organizations (McAllister, 1995). Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman (1995) propose a well-established model of trust defining trust as “the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party” (p. 712). This vulnerability is based on the assumption that the other party seems to be trustworthy, that is able, benevolent, and of integrity (Mayer et al., 1995). Furthermore, trust is often viewed as multidimensional construct consisting of cognitiveand affect-based trust (McAllister, 1995). In our study, we focus on affect-based trust as this facet of trust is characterized by a caring relationship (Madjar & Ortiz- Walters, 2009) which is also inherent in transformational teaching behavior. 1.1.2. Transformational teaching and course-related self-efficacy In order to examine students' “can-do” motivation we focus on their course-related self-efficacy. Self-efficacy is defined as the “beliefs in one's capabilities to mobilize the motivation, cognitive resources, and courses of action needed to meet given situational demands” (Wood & Bandura, 1989).

Following social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1986), self-efficacy emerges through four different ways: mastery experience, verbal persuasion, vicarious learning, and physiological arousal. We propose that transformational lecturers provide their students with opportunities for mastery experience as they motivate students by formulating high expectations (Nielsen & Munir, 2009). Additionally, transformational lecturers offer verbal persuasion by persuading students of their capabilities to meet these expectations. By providing intellectual stimulation, transformational lecturers help students to find new ways to deal with challenges they encounter during their studies, finally resulting in higher levels of self-efficacy (Nielsen & Munir, 2009). By motivating and supporting students individually, transformational lecturers most likely help students to reduce anxiety arousal during challenging situations. Having successfully mastered challenging situations and the respective physiological arousal will further strengthen students' selfefficacy beliefs (Bandura, 1993).

In spite of this reasoning, research on the link between transformational leadership and self-efficacy has been inconclusive (Gong, Huang, & Farh, 2009; Hannah et al., 2016; Kark, Shamir, & Chen, 2003; Nielsen & Munir, 2009). One reason for these inconsistent results may be the distinction between rather general versus more specific dimensions of self-efficacy. Contrary to studies measuring general self-efficacy (Nielsen & Munir, 2009), studies measuring specific facets of self-efficacy, such as creative or role self-efficacy, have shown a positive relationship between transformational leadership and self-efficacy (Hannah et al., 2016). We therefore focus on the specific facet of course-related self-efficacy and propose that transformational teaching will increase students' course-related self-efficacy over time.

Hypothesis 2. Transformational teaching predicts an increase in students' course-related self-efficacy.

As stated above, a trusting relationship creating a sense of obligation serves as a motivational reason to exert additional effort on behalf of the other party (Schaubroeck et al., 2013). Thus, students having a trusting relationship with their teacher are likely to feel responsible to engage in their studies. Prior research showed that trust in supervisor predicted trainee work engagement (Chughtai, Byrne, & Flood, 2015). We aim at expanding these findings by showing that increased trust in lecturer will predict increased study engagement and that trust serves as a mediator in the relationship between transformational teaching and study engagement.

Furthermore, organizational research has often studied self-efficacy as an important personal resource (Hannah et al., 2016; Xanthopoulou, Bakker, Demerouti, & Schaufeli, 2009a). Following Conservation of Resources Theory (Hobfoll, 1989), people try to preserve, protect, and grow resources. Thus, individuals do not only try to protect their resources against threats but also try to accumulate them through resource investment. Thus, in the face of increased self-efficacy students will feel capable to be successful and thus most likely invest in study engagement in order to accumulate further resources (such as approval through good study grades and thereby access to further resources). Furthermore, people who feel able to deal with upcoming challenges have been shown to exhibit higher degrees of engagement (Bakker, Sanz Vergel, & Kuntze, 2015; Llorens, Schaufeli, Bakker, & Salanova, 2007; Neff, Niessen, Sonnentag, & Unger, 2013; Xanthopoulou et al., 2009a). Accordingly, we hypothesize that increased course-related-selfefficacy predicts increased student engagement. Furthermore, we expect that increased course-related self-efficacy serves as a mediator in the relationship between transformational teaching and increased study engagement.

Hypothesis 3. Increased trust in lecturer (H3a) and increased courserelated self-efficacy (H3b) predict increased study engagement over time.

1.2.2. Motivational states and student creativity Creativity is defined as “the production of novel and useful ideas in any domain” (Amabile, Conti, Coon, Lazenby, & Herron, 1996, p. 1155) and has recently become an essential goal in educational science (Pang, 2015). Concerning trust, following the reasoning by Madjar and Ortiz- Walters (2009) and Brattström, Löfsten, and Richtnér (2012), we argue that a trusting relationship between lecturer and student will foster students' feelings of safety. We propose that in this context the affective component of trust will be of special importance as the feeling of a psychologically save environment in turn will increase students' willingness to take risks and to explore new cognitive pathways without the fear of failure (Brattström et al., 2012; Henker, Sonnentag, & Unger, 2015). Students who trust their teachers are likely to have more cognitive resources and attention available and therefore achieve higher

levels of creativity which could already be shown in the organizational context (Madjar & Ortiz-Walters, 2009). Therefore, we hypothesize that an increased level of trust in lecturer relates to an increase in students' creativity, and that trust in lecturer mediates the relationship between transformational teaching and increased student creativity.

With respect to student self-efficacy, studies in organizational research have shown employee self-efficacy (Oldham & Cummings, 1996; Rego, Sousa, Marques, & Cunha, 2012) and creative self-efficacy (Tierney & Farmer, 2002) to be linked to employee creativity. Creative self-efficacy also mediated the relationship between transformational leadership and follower creativity (Gong et al., 2009). According to the reasoning of Rego et al. (2012) as well as of Tierney and Farmer (2002), people with higher self-efficacy should be more confident to come up with novel and useful ideas. They set themselves challenging goals, take higher risks, and are more persistent when facing challenging situations – all these factors are preconditions necessary for creativity. Thus, we propose that increased course-related self-efficacy predicts student creativity and serves as a mediator in the relationship between transformational teaching and student creativity.

Hypothesis 4. Increased trust in lecturer (H4a) and increased courserelated self-efficacy (H4b) predict increased student creativity over time.

1.2.3. Motivational states and task performance In organizational research, performance is a multidimensional construct that roughly divides into task and contextual performance (Binnewies, Sonnentag, & Mojza, 2009b; Motowidlo, Borman, & Schmit, 1997). Thereby, task performance is each individual's direct contribution to the achievement of his or her work tasks and formal organizational goals (Gang et al., 2011; Motowidlo et al., 1997). In educational studies, the state-of-the-art indicator of academic task performance is students' course grades (Bakker et al., 2015; Komarraju & Nadler, 2013). Thus, we examine academic task performance by focusing on students' achievements in the respective course exam.

As argued before, students who have a trustful relationship with their lecturer will have more cognitive resources available (Madjar & Ortiz-Walters, 2009). Moreover, according to social exchange theory, they are likely to put more effort into their studies (Schaubroeck et al., 2013). Following this reasoning, students should be able to achieve better learning results (i.e. better course grades). In organizational research, the link between trust and performance is relatively well established (Dirks & Ferrin, 2002; Madjar & Ortiz-Walters, 2009; Schaubroeck et al., 2013). We therefore hypothesize that students' affective trust in their lecturer predicts task performance and that trust will mediate the association between transformational teaching and task performance.

1.3. Overview of the empirical contribution

In the current study, we investigate the effects of transformational teaching on increased study engagement, creativity, and task performance via students' self-efficacy and trust in lecturer. To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine transformational teaching in the context of higher education. Yet, considering the specific challenges students face in higher education and the need to develop employability capacity transformational teaching seems to be an especially promising approach.

Following the framework by Raub and Liao (2012), as well as Hannah et al. (2016), we expand previous literature by investigating underlying motivational states linking transformational behavior to beneficial follower outcomes in the context of higher education. We assume that trust in lecturer and students' self-efficacy should be two crucial mechanisms covering students' “reason-to” and “can-do” motivation thereby linking transformational teaching to students' engagement, creativity, and task performance.

Finally, we contribute to existing research by using a longitudinal design. Thus, we address claims of existing research to conduct more longitudinal studies (Ployhart & Vandenberg, 2010) and illustrate a more dynamic relationship between transformational behavior and motivation as well as performance. By focusing on the change within the respective outcome variables (except students' course grades), we are even able to eliminate reverse causation or effects of potential third variables (Binnewies, Sonnentag, & Mojza, 2009a; Zapf, Dormann, & Frese, 1996).

2. Material and methods

2.1. Participants and Procedure

Participants were students of a large German university. They were contacted via the university's student mailing list, via a mailing list of students interested in psychological studies, via their participation in the university's student mentoring program, or approached face-to-face in central places on the campus. A prerequisite for participation was to have at least one lecture which took place during the whole semester with a single lecturer. To thank participants, students were offered to obtain a profile of their learning patterns (Vermunt, 1994) including feedback how to improve their learning strategies. Additionally, they

Currently there is little knowledge about the appropriate timing of measurement occasions in longitudinal studies (Ployhart & Vandenberg, 2010). Therefore, we chose our measurement occasions according to the following considerations: The first questionnaire (Time 1) was administered three to four weeks after the beginning of the semester, so that students had had the opportunity to get to know their lecturers and were able to judge their teaching style. Initially, in the first questionnaire students were asked to report all the lectures with a single lecturer they attained during the semester. By random selection one of the lectures was chosen by the computer system and students had to fill in all questionnaires referring to this lecture. The first questionnaire also contained demographic variables, predictor, mediator, and outcome variables. The second measurement occasion (Time 2) which contained mediators and outcomes was assessed approximately six weeks after Time 1, at the end of the semester. We assumed six weeks to be enough time for a transformational teacher to build trust and increased course-related self-efficacy as well as increased engagement and creativity. Finally, participants were asked to report the grade they obtained in the exam of the lecture at Time 3, a few weeks after the end of semester. In total, the final sample in Time 1 and Time 2 consisted of 165 students with 26.1% being male and 73.9% being female. We controlled for students' gender in the later analyses because of this unproportioned gender distribution. Students' average age was 22.58 (SD = 6.03) and they already had been studying for 3.49 (SD = 2.44) semesters. Students of different faculties took part in the study with 24% being from the field of humanities and cultural sciences, 43% being from the department of social and economic sciences or being a law student, and 33% being from the domain of natural sciences or medicine. In the end, 78 students of this sample also participated at Time 3. MANOVAS conducted for a dropout analysis revealed no significant differences with respect to all study variables assessed at Time 1 between the participants at Time 1 and Time 2 and the students that took part at Time 3.

2.2. Measures

All questionnaires were administered online via Unipark. All measures have been previously validated and should show good psychometric properties as they were previously used in much-cited articles. All items were in German and retrieved by using either the back translation method (Brislin, Lonner, & Thorndike, 1973) for the scales developed originally in English or by assessing scales already used in international publications with German samples. Participants rated all measures on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (totally disagree) to 5 (totally agree).

2.2.1. Transformational teaching We assessed transformational teaching with an adapted version of the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ) (Bass & Avolio, 1997). The adapted transformational leadership scale which consists of 16 items (Time 1: α = .92) was validated for the student context by Beauchamp et al. (2010) and showed good psychometric properties in other studies (Beauchamp et al., 2011; Beauchamp et al., 2014). The scale pertains the following four dimensions: individualized consideration, inspirational motivation, idealized influence, and intellectual stimulation with four items, respectively. A sample item for intellectual stimulation is “My lecturer encourages me to look at issues

2.2.2. Trust in lecturer We used four items (Time 1: α = .87, Time 2: α = .87) of McAllister's (1995) scale of affect-based trust which we adapted to the student context to measure students' trust in their lecturer. We selected the items according to their factor loadings and content fit. The scale showed good psychometric properties in previous research (Madjar & Ortiz-Walters, 2009; Schaubroeck, Lam, & Peng, 2011). A sample item is “If I shared my problems with this lecturer, I know (s)he would respond constructively and caringly”.

2.2.3. Course-related self-efficacy We assessed course-related self-efficacy with the New General Self- Efficacy Scale (NGSE) by Chen, Gully, and Eden (2001) consisting of eight items (Time 1: α = .94, Time 2: α = .95), which we adapted to the context of a single student course. Previous studies have already applied the scale in specific contexts (Neff et al., 2013) and obtained good reliability. A sample item is “When facing difficult tasks in this course, I am certain that I will accomplish them”.

2.2.4. Study engagement Study engagement was assessed with the 9-item (Time 1: α = .92, Time 2: α = .93) Schoolwork Engagement Inventory (Salmela-Aro & Upadaya, 2012). This instrument is based on the short Utrecht Work Engagement Scale originally developed by Schaufeli (2006), validated in the educational context. The scale has successfully been used in prior studies (Salmela-Aro & Upadyaya, 2014; Upadyaya & Salmela-Aro, 2015) and measures the three sub dimensions vigor (e.g., “Within this lecture, I am bursting with energy.”), dedication (e.g., “I am enthusiastic about my studies within this lecture.”), and absorption (e.g., “Time flies when I am studying within this lecture.”) with three items each.

2.2.5. Student creativity We used four of the thirteen items (Time 1: α = .88, Time 2: α = .90) of an established creativity measure (George & Zhou, 2001). We chose the respective four items based on their fit to the educational context and further adapted them to assess creativity within the lecture. Previous research has often used the scale and adapted it to specific contexts (Aleksić, Černe, Dysvik, & Škerlavaj, 2016; Amundsen & Martinsen, 2015; Boon, Vangrieken, & Dochy, 2016). A sample item is “I often have new and innovative ideas within this lecture”.

2.2.6. Task performance Students' course grades are a commonly used as objective performance criterion (Bakker et al., 2015). Following previous research, we used self-reports to assess students' course grades at Time 3 (Komarraju, Ramsey, & Rinella, 2013). Course grades differed from 1 (outstanding) to 5 (not succeeded). As course grades are highly dependent of the department (Achen & Courant, 2009; Beenstock & Feldman, 2018), students' grades were weighted around the average grade of the respective study domain in order to gain comparable results.

2.2.7. Control variables We controlled for students' sex as prior research showed gender to have an impact on academic performance (Buchmann & DiPrete, 2006). For the same reason we also included age and study domain (Achen & Courant, 2009; Beenstock & Feldman, 2018) as control variables for the analyses. With regard to study domain, we divided students in groups from the field of humanities and cultural sciences, social and economic sciences or being a law student, and the domain of natural sciences. For the analyses which contained the outcome of task performance we only included students' age and gender as control variables as study domain was used to weight students' course grades. Note however that results also stayed the same without the inclusion of the respective controls.

To test changes in our mediator variables (trust in lecturer and course-related self-efficacy) as well as changes in our outcome variables (study engagement and student creativity) we used hierarchical regression analyses controlling for the respective Time 1 measures. This means that further predictors entered in the model explain variance in the difference between Time 1 and Time 2. Specifically, for all of these analyses we compared the following two models: Model 1 contained the descriptive controls and the respective Time 1 measures of mediators and outcomes. In Model 2, we entered the respective predictors. We relied on hierarchical linear modelling and avoided structural equation modelling because of the relatively small sample size of 165 students in our study which does not meet prevalent sample size recommendations for structural equation modelling in literature (Kline, 2011; Nunnaly, 1967). Furthermore, we used a bias-corrected bootstrap procedure with the PROCESS macro for SPSS by Preacher and Hayes (2008) with 10000 resamples to test the indirect effects of Hypotheses 6a, b, c and 7a, b, c. We obtained similar results calculating all analyses for each mediator variable separately.

3.1. Construct validity

To examine whether transformational teaching, trust in teacher, course-related self-efficacy, study engagement, and student creativity display different constructs at both measurement occasions, we conducted confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs) for each measurement occasion in Mplus (Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2012). We hereby used the single items of the five study constructs as manifest indicators. We omitted item parcelling due to its critical discussion in the literature in terms of indicating misleadingly good fit indexes (Marsh, Lüdtke, Nagengast, Morin, & von Davier, 2013) as well as providing reduced information (Meade & Kroustalis, 2006). At Time 1, the hypothesized five-factor model showed a better fit (χ2 = 1526.059, df = 769, RMSEA = .077, CFI = .840) than the best fitting four-factor model (Δχ2 = 122.911, Δdf = 4, p < .001), the best fitting three-factor model (Δχ2 = 372.539, Δdf = 7, p < .001), the best fitting two-factor model (Δχ2 = 704.685, Δdf = 9, p < .001), as well as the one-factormodel (Δχ2 = 1318.563, Δdf = 10, p < .001). Results of CFAs at Time 2 showed similar results. The hypothesized four-factor model showed a good fit to the data (χ2 = 541.202, df = 269, RMSEA = .078, CFI = .920) that was superior to the three-factor solution (Δχ2 = 185.172, Δdf = 3, p < .001), the two factor model (Δχ2 = 385.188, Δdf = 5, p < .001), and the one-factor model (Δχ2 = 1153.438, df = 6, p < .001).

4. Discussion

In the current study we examined transformational leadership style in the context of higher education. We were particularly interested in the impact of transformational teaching on students' motivational states and an increased degree of positive student outcomes, namely study engagement, student creativity, and task performance over time. Thereby, we shed more light on the motivational mediating mechanisms, increased trust in lecturer and increased course-related self-efficacy.

Our results confirm the prediction that transformational teaching relates to increased trust in lecturer. In a further step, as hypothesized, an increased level of trust in lecturer in turn predicted study engagement, student creativity, and objective task performance. As predicted, increased trust in lecturer mediated the relationship between transformational teaching and study engagement, student creativity, and task performance. Contradictory, our results did not support a positive relationship between transformational teaching and increased course-related self-efficacy. Nevertheless, as hypothesized, increased course-related self-efficacy predicted an increased degree of study engagement and increased student creativity, whereas contrary to our prediction, increased course-related self-efficacy did not predict task performance. Furthermore, our results did not support increased course-related selfefficacy as a mediating mechanism in the association between transformational teaching and study engagement, student creativity, and task performance. Below, we discuss these results and their theoretical and practical implications.

Our finding of the relationship between transformational teaching and increased trust in lecturer is in line with existing research on transformational leadership and follower trust (Braun et al., 2013; Dirks & Ferrin, 2002). Likewise, our results which show that an increased level of trust in lecturer in turn predicts changes in relevant student performance criteria, namely study engagement, student creativity, and task performance are in line with existing organizational research (Chughtai et al., 2015; Madjar & Ortiz-Walters, 2009). Furthermore, we could identify increased trust in lecturer as a potential “reason-to” (Hannah et al., 2016; Raub & Liao, 2012) motivational mediator in the relationship between transformational teaching and beneficial student outcomes. Therefore, we contribute to existing research in several ways: First, by adding to existing research on transformational leadership and initial results of transformational teaching in the school context, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to successfully apply transformational teaching in the context of higher education. As students in higher education not only face challenges as higher workload, larger class sizes and more distant teacher-student relationships in comparison to school (Lowe & Cook, 2013) but also need to learn more independently (Hassel & Ridout, 2018; Murtagh, 2010), transformational teaching seems to be a promising teaching style especially in higher education. Accordingly, we could show that transformational behavior in the context of higher education predicts positive changes in motivational and performance-related student criteria. These constructs in turn are crucial for them to succeed in education and later life time (Craft & Jeffrey, 2008).

Next, by employing students' course grades as an objective measure of task performance we address existing claims to reduce subjective bias in the measurement of outcome variables (Dvir, Eden, Avolio, & Shamir, 2002). Furthermore, we underpin and extend theoretical (Raub & Liao, 2012) and empirical propositions (Hannah et al., 2016) concerning the role of motivation as a mediating mechanism in the relationship between transformational teaching and beneficial outcomes. We thereby contribute to the understanding of how transformational teaching works, and to the comprehension of the role that trust in lecturer plays as a motivational state. More specifically, we focused on affective trust which corresponds to transformational behavior such as individual consideration (Madjar & Ortiz-Walters, 2009). In addition, especially this emotional bond between leader and follower via affective trust seems to be crucial for social exchange and a psychological safe environment (Schaubroeck et al., 2011). Social exchange and a psychological safe environment in turn provide the basis for high engagement, performance, and creativity.

Finally, we contribute to previous research by applying a longitudinal design that allows us to illustrate a more dynamic relationship between our study variables and to make predictions of change over time. Research often has tested mediating processes with cross-sectional designs (Maxwell & Cole, 2007). Yet, cross-sectional approaches fail to represent longitudinal processes, such as mediations, because they tend to overor underestimate longitudinal effects (Maxwell & Cole, 2007). Thus, our study successfully integrates a temporal perspective as implied in mediation. Furthermore, by using repeated measurement of our mediator and outcome variables we are able to eliminate reverse causation or effects of potential third variables (Binnewies et al., 2009a; Zapf et al., 1996).

Concerning our second mediating mechanism–course-related selfefficacy as a “can-do” motivation–our data show mixed results. On the one hand, we were able to establish a link between increased courserelated self-efficacy and increased study engagement and creativity. These findings are in line with previous research (Neff et al., 2013; Rego et al., 2012) and additionally extend existing findings by making predictions of change over time. However, our data did not support our hypotheses regarding a significant relationship between increased course-related self-efficacy and our third performance criterion,

Furthermore, transformational teaching was neither related to an increased level of course-related self-efficacy, nor did course-related self-efficacy reveal to be a “can-do” motivational state mediating the relationship between transformational teaching and students' in-role and extra-role performance. Previous research on transformational leadership and self-efficacy showed inconclusive results in terms of this relationship. Whereas researchers like Kark et al. (2003) could not establish a link between transformational leadership and self-efficacy in a cross-sectional design, Hannah et al. (2016) even confirmed the relationship between transformational leadership and increased self-efficacy over time. Contrary to the latter study, the period between the first and the second measurement occasion in our study (six weeks) might have been too short for a transformational teacher to enhance students' course-related self-efficacy. Self-efficacy is a rather trait-like construct and the time interval of fourteen weeks in the study of Hannah et al. (2016) might have been more appropriate to foster change in self-efficacy.

Albeit, our results did not support course-related self-efficacy as a mediating mechanism, trust in lecturer explained the relationship between transformational teaching and student outcomes as a “reason-to” motivation. The fact that increased trust in lecturer serves as a mediator over and above course-related self-efficacy also demonstrates the robustness of this mediating mechanism.

4.2. Limitations and future research directions

One limitation of the current study is that we only used self-reported data which can increase the possibility of same source bias (Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee, & Podsakoff, 2003). However, we tried to reduce same source bias by using a longitudinal design with two measurement occasions and a third one for assessing students' grades (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Therefore, we were able to temporally separate the measurement of transformational teaching and mediator and outcome variables. In addition, we even made predictions of change over time by measuring mediator and outcome variables at the beginning of the semester and six weeks later. Furthermore, by integrating a more objective performance measure including students' course grades at Time 3 we were able to reduce shared variance of students' self-reported variables and task performance. Nevertheless, future studies could integrate a third measurement occasion that also separates the assessment of mediator variables and self-reported outcomes, creativity and engagement. Additionally, one could include important sources such as the perspective of the respective teachers or the organizational perspective. For instance, it might be interesting to take self-other ratings with respect to transformational teaching into account as both perspectives most likely provide additional information (Vazire & Mehl, 2008). As to the approach of data analysis, future studies could apply structural equation modelling to test this study's model as a whole using larger sample sizes.

Other limitations are the problem of dropouts from the first and second to the third measurement occasion and the larger number of female than male participants in our sample. Regarding dropouts, other studies with longitudinal designs report similar dropout rates of up to 50% or more (Ployhart & Vandenberg, 2010). Additionally, we

In the current study, we looked at the underlying mechanisms of the relationship between transformational teaching and students' performance criteria. In addition, future studies could consider conditions that foster or hinder positive effects of transformational teaching on beneficial student outcomes. For example, students' goal orientation (Gong et al., 2009) might work as a moderator. We assume that transformational teaching unfolds its potential especially when students have a high learning goal instead of a high performance goal orientation.

Considering the many ways trust has been conceptualised in prior research (Dirks & Ferrin, 2002) future studies could integrate a more comprehensive picture of trust as a mediating mechanism in the relationship between transformational teaching and student outcomes. Thereby, it would be interesting to investigate the additive effects of cognition-based and affect-based trust (Schaubroeck et al., 2011) as well as different effects on outcomes. One might speculate that cognitive trust could have a stronger impact on performance (Yang, Mossholder, & Peng, 2009) while affect based trust may be more strongly related to affective outcomes such as engagement. In addition, it would be interesting to investigate the link between transformational teaching not only on the individual but also on the team level by including trust between students and its impact on student outcomes (Braun et al., 2013).

Finally, it could be interesting to investigate effects of transformational teaching on self-efficacy not only in lectures across all departments but specifically in courses with high drop-out rates such as engineering and natural science (Heublein, 2014). Prior research found low initial self-efficacy to be one major reason for drop-out (Georg, 2009). One could assume that future studies might find a link between transformational teaching and self-efficacy especially for courses in these respective departments as transformational behavior might be especially apt to address students with low self-efficacy.

4.3. Practical implications

Results of our study can also provide practitioners with first insights regarding how to foster students' motivation and subsequently students' study engagement, creativity, and task performance in higher education. This might help to prepare students for their later professional life. First, lecturers in higher education should show their individualized consideration for their course participants, for example by answering students' questions or by providing constructive feedback. Furthermore, lecturers should stimulate and challenge their students by encouraging them to take different perspectives on a subject or by setting specific, challenging, but manageable goals. Finally, lecturers should be aware of their function as a role model and reflect and thus regulate their own behavior.

In addition, organizational research showed that training can foster transformational leadership effectively by applying self-reflection methods, role play, and group discussions with a focus on transformational behavior (e.g. goal setting) (Barling, Weber, & Kelloway, 1996; Dvir et al., 2002). Thus, transformational teaching training could be a valuable component of further education of university lecturers, high school teachers, and other lecturers who offer courses (e.g., teachers of language courses).
