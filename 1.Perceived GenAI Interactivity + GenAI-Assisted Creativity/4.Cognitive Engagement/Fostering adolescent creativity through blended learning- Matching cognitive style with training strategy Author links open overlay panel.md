Fostering adolescent creativity through blended learning: Matching cognitive style with training strategy

A B S T R A C T

Keywords: Creativity Creativity training Blended learning Cognitive style Active engagement Passive engagement

Creativity is an essential skill for adolescents to effectively deal with future uncertainties and complex challenges. This study investigated the effectiveness of training strategies tailored to different cognitive styles in a blended learning environment. A total of 123 fourth and fifth-grade students participated in the study and were classified as either field-dependent (FD) or fieldindependent (FI) cognitive style based on the Embedded Figures Test. They were then assigned to either an active or passive engagement group, where they received online instruction on ten creativity training techniques, followed by classroom-based practice. Results indicated that for the AUT, FD participants showed significantly higher flexibility and originality under passive engagement, while no differences were observed for FI participants. In the creative imagination task, FD participants demonstrated greater flexibility and originality with active engagement, while FI participants showed higher scores on both indices with passive engagement. FI partic­ ipants also generated more useful ideas in the scientific design task under passive engagement and demonstrated greater openness in the drawing task under active engagement. No interaction effects emerged for the RAT and the creative self-efficacy. These findings underscore the importance of adapting training strategies to individual cognitive styles to foster adolescent creativity in blended learning environments.

1.1. Definition and measurement of creativity

Creativity is defined as the ability to generate novel and useful ideas or products (Runco & Jaeger, 2012). It drives technological innovation, economic and social development, and enhances mental health and well-being (Tan et al., 2021). In particular, creativity has been recognized as a key competency for adolescents to effectively cope with future uncertainties and various challenges (Cremin & Chappell, 2021).

Creativity involves two fundamental thinking processes: divergent thinking and convergent thinking (Guilford, 1967). Divergent thinking refers to the ability to think in multiple directions and generate many potential solutions or ideas. It is often assessed using tasks such as the Alternative Uses Task (AUT), where individuals are asked to generate as many uses as possible for a common object (Guilford, 1967). Responses are typically evaluated on indices such as fluency (number of ideas), originality (uniqueness of ideas), and flexibility (variety of idea categories). In contrast, convergent thinking involves logical reasoning and the integration of information to arrive at a single correct solution. It is typically measured using tasks like the Remote Associates Test (RAT), which requires partic­ ipants to identify a target word that can form meaningful compounds with three clue words (Simonton, 2003). Other common measures of creativity include scientific design tasks, which integrate both divergent and convergent thinking to evaluate an in­ dividual’s ability to solve scientific problems (Hu & Adey, 2002); and creative self-efficacy—the belief in one’s capacity to produce creative outcomes, is also widely used as an indicator of creative potential and motivation (Haase et al., 2018).

1.2. Creativity training

Meta-analysis evidence has consistently demonstrated the effectiveness of creativity training programs. These interventions have demonstrated moderate to large effects on enhancing creative performance (Ma, 2006; Scott et al., 2004; Sio & Lortie-Forgues, 2024). Particularly, training programs that target underlying cognitive processes tend to yield more robust outcomes (Ma, 2006; Scott et al., 2004). However, creativity is not solely a cognitive function—it is influenced by a range of personal and contextual factors. To reflect this complexity, many creativity training programs adopt a more comprehensive approach that addresses multiple dimensions of creativity, as highlighted in the 4Ps model of creativity (person, process, press, product; Rhodes, 1961). For example, Gu et al. (2019, 2023) proposed the 5-I creativity training framework, which incorporates five interrelated components: Inclination, Ideation, Inter­ action, Identification, and Inspiration. Specifically, Inclination focuses on developing traits conducive to creative thinking, such as tolerance for ambiguity (Wu et al., 2019). Ideation targets cognitive skills that aid creative thinking, like conceptual combination (Gu et al., 2022a). Interaction emphasizes the support of physical and interpersonal environments for creative activities. Identification focuses on the assessment of creative ideas. Inspiration aims to stimulate creators’ motivation. Empirical studies have confirmed the effectiveness of 5-I training in promoting creativity among both children (Gu et al., 2019) and university students (Gu et al., 2023). The current study employed the 5-I framework as the theoretical foundation for a set of training techniques aimed at fostering students’ creativity.

1.3. Fostering creativity through blended learning

Despite the recognized importance of creativity in education, traditional classroom settings often face significant limitations in fostering students’ creative capacities. These include limited instructional flexibility, lack of diverse pedagogical forms, and insuffi­ cient teacher expertise in fostering creative thinking (Davies et al., 2013). In this context, technology-supported learning approaches, such as flipped learning (Hsia & Hwang, 2021; Hsia et al., 2021), virtual reality (Hidajat, 2024), electronic brainstorming (Li et al., 2023), and blended learning (Rasheed et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2023), offer promising avenues for enhancing creativity. Blended learning—defined as the purposeful integration of face-to-face and online learning environments, has been widely adopted from K-12 to higher education (Yu et al., 2023). Typically, blended learning delivers core content via online modalities (e.g., instructional videos), followed by face-to-face activities such as discussions, collaborative problem-solving, or hands-on applications (Zhang et al., 2020). This format offers consistent content delivery across learners and mitigates challenges associated with fully online learning, such as technological inaccessibility or limited student engagement. Meanwhile, face-to-face sessions promote deeper engagement through real-time feedback, peer interaction, and contextualized practice (Lin et al., 2024; Nortvig et al., 2020). Research has suggested that when online components are sequenced prior to in-person sessions, learners are better prepared and more engaged in subsequent classroom discussions (Yu et al., 2023). Despite these benefits, blended learning also presents challenges. The integration of online and face-to-face components requires careful coordination to avoid cognitive overload, redundancy, or disengagement, particularly in younger learners who may lack self-regulatory skills (Brod, 2021a). Additionally, students may struggle to navigate digital tools or lose motivation if engagement is not actively supported through scaffolded learning activities (Rasheed et al., 2020). Thus, it is essential to consider individual learning preferences and implement blended learning with targeted pedagogical strategies that effectively sustain and enhance student engagement throughout the learning process.

Recent research has highlighted the potential of blended learning to foster students’ creative thinking and other higher-order cognitive skills by offering diverse opportunities for exploration, problem-solving, and autonomous learning (Lin et al., 2024; Sophonhiranraka et al., 2015). For instance, Sophonhiranraka et al. (2015) demonstrated that students in blended learning envi­ ronments outperformed their peers in traditional settings on creativity tasks, likely due to the diversified learning modalities and increased autonomy provided by the blended format.

However, not all blended learning designs are equally effective in promoting creativity (Rasheed et al., 2020). The instructional strategies embedded within blended learning environments play a crucial role in determining their impact. According to the ICAP framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014), which categorizes engagement into passive, active, constructive, and interactive levels based on cognitive processing, the nature of students’ engagement significantly influences learning outcomes. In line with this framework, research in computer-based and online learning contexts often adopts a simplified distinction between active and passive engagement strategies (Michel et al., 2009). Active engagement encourages deeper cognitive involvement, providing learners with greater op­ portunities to engage in higher-order thinking and complex problem solving (Alkhatib et al., 2018; Guan et al., 2023). Several studies have emphasized the value of incorporating active engagement techniques into blended learning environments, highlighting their positive effects on students’ creative thinking and problem-solving abilities (Lin et al., 2024; Wahyudi et al., 2020). For instance, Lin et al. (2024) showed that students in a blended learning program who actively practiced creativity techniques—such as applying creative problem-solving strategies—demonstrated significant gains in both creative thinking and physical creativity. Passive engagement, which involves reviewing or repeating previously learned content without modification, by contrast—tends to activate lower levels of cognitive processing (Chi & Wylie, 2014). Although such strategies can support foundational understanding, it does not always stimulate the mental flexibility required for creative thinking. This distinction is further supported by research on the testing effect, which demonstrates that active retrieval and application of knowledge promote superior retention and transfer compared to passive review (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).

1.5. Influence of cognitive style in creativity

Learner characteristics, such as cognitive style, significantly affect how students interact with engagement strategies. Field dependence–independence (FD/FI) is a well-established cognitive dimension that affects how individuals process information (Witkin et al., 1977). Field-independent (FI) learners are more analytical, self-directed, and capable of internal information restructuring, while field-dependent (FD) learners tend to rely on external cues and benefit more from structured guidance (Nisiforou & Efi, 2015; Zhang, 2004). Based on these characteristics, it has been suggested that FI learners thrive in active learning environments that emphasize self-regulation and exploration, whereas FD learners may perform better with passive or guided strategies that provide external structure (Saracho, 1997). However, emerging research suggests that this relationship is not always straightforward. For instance, Gu et al. (2023) found that while FI students performed equally under active and passive training, FD students exhibited greater improvements in divergent thinking under active learning conditions. Conversely, Chen and Macredie (2002) reported that in hypermedia environments, FD learners tend to perform better when provided with passive and structured learning strategies. Another study found that FI and FD students performed equally well in electronic brainstorming tasks when external cues were provided (Li et al., 2023).

1.6. The present study

These findings point to the complex interaction between cognitive style and engagement strategy. Creativity outcomes are not solely determined by cognitive style, but rather by the extent to which instructional approaches align with both the learner’s needs and the cognitive demands of the task. This interaction is especially important in blended learning environments, which offer unique opportunities for tailored engagement to develop creativity. Despite growing interest in this area, most existing research has focused on online-only settings, with limited attention given to how cognitive style and engagement strategies interact in blended contexts.

Hence, this study aims to investigate how cognitive style (FD, FI) and engagement strategy (active engagement, passive engage­ ment) interact to influence adolescent students’ creativity in blended learning settings. The research seeks to provide insights into how blended learning environments can be designed to optimally foster creativity, particularly by aligning engagement strategies with students’ cognitive profiles. Considering the inconclusive findings on the interaction between cognitive style and learning strategies in relation to creativity (Gu et al., 2022b; Li et al., 2023), and the fact that active learning, while beneficial for university students, it may not always be suitable for younger learners who require more structured guidance when acquiring new knowledge (Brod, 2021a), this study proposes the following hypotheses:

H1. For FI adolescent students, there will be no significant difference in creative performance between those trained with active engagement strategy and those with passive engagement strategy.

H2. For FD adolescent students, training with passive engagement strategy will significantly enhance their creative performance, with the effect being greater than that of the active engagement strategy.

2.2. Measurements

In the current study, six creativity tasks were employed to assess the effects of the training on various dimensions of creativity. The AUT and the drawing task were used to measure participants’ divergent thinking, with the former targeting verbal creativity, and the latter figural creativity. Creative imagination task was used to measure participants’ imaginative creativity, while the RAT measured participants’ convergent thinking. Scientific creativity was captured through a scientific design task that required students to generate solutions to real-world scientific problems. In addition, creative self-efficacy was assessed to evaluate participants’ confidence and belief in their creative potential. Two versions of each task were used and counterbalanced between the pretest and posttest.

AUT. This task required participants to think of as many different uses as possible for a common item (e.g., a brick) within 3 min (Guilford, 1967). Two trained raters scored the participants’ ideas on three dimensions: fluency, flexibility, and originality. Fluency: The number of relevant and clearly described ideas. Flexibility: The number of different categories the ideas fall into. Fluency and flexibility were counted by one rater. Originality: The degree of uniqueness and novelty of the ideas, which were rated on a 1 to 5-point scale, with "1″ indicating "not original" and "5″ indicating "very original." The mean score of all ideas represented the participant’s originality score. Due to the large sample size and number of responses, rater 1 independently rated the originality of all ideas, while rater 2 rated 50 % of the ideas to examine rater reliability in accordance with the consensual assessment technique (Amabile, 1983). Reliability analysis revealed good inter-rater reliability between the two raters, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.900. Creative imagination task. Participants were presented with a geometric figure and asked to think of as many original ideas as possible for what the figure could represent within 3 min Torrance, (1966). Ideas were rated on fluency, flexibility, and originality, using the same criteria as the AUT. The ICC for originality between the two raters was 0.918, indicating good reliability.

Scientific design problem. This task was adapted from Hu and Adey (2002), asking participants to think of as many different so­ lutions as possible to a design problem within 4 min (e.g., "How to design and improve a bicycle to make it more unique, useful, and attractive?"). Ideas were rated on fluency, originality, and usefulness (effectiveness and practicality of the ideas), using the same

Drawing task. Participants were given 4 min to add details to a given shape to create a complete, original drawing (Torrance, 1974). Drawings were rated on originality, elaboration (degree of complexity and richness of details), and openness (tendency to extend and modify the drawing), using criteria similar to originality scoring in the AUT. The ICCs for originality, elaboration, and openness were 0.946, 0.887, and 0.907, respectively, indicating good reliability. RAT. In the current study, participants had 3 min to find solution words for 10 sets of words. Each correct answer earned 1 score, with a total score of 10.

Creative self-efficacy scale. The scale, designed by Liu et al. (2014), comprised 4 items (e.g., "I feel that I am good at coming up with novel ideas"). It used a 7-point Likert scale, with responses ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Higher average scores indicated higher creative self-efficacy. The reliabilities for the scale were Cronbach’s alpha = 0.826 and 0.936 in the pretest and posttest, respectively.

Cognitive Style was measured at pretest using the Chinese version of the Embedded Figures Test (EFT) adapted by Song et al. (1988). The EFT required participants to identify simple shapes out of complex figures. The test comprised three stages: one practice (not included in the final score) and two formal tests with 10 items each, completed within 5 min per stage. Each correct answer earned 1 score, with a total score of 20. To account for potential affective influences on training outcomes, participants’ emotions were assessed at both pretest and posttest using the Positive Affect and Negative Affect Scale for Children (PANAS), as adapted by Pan et al. (2015). This scale included 30 items divided into positive affect (e.g., happy) and negative affect (e.g., upset), with 15 items each. It used a 5-point Likert scale, with re­ sponses ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very). Higher average scores indicated higher levels of positive or negative affect. In this study, the reliabilities for positive affect were Cronbach’s alpha = 0.886 and 0.934 in the pretest and posttest, respectively, while those for negative affect were 0.934 and 0.964, respectively.

2.3. Study design and procedure

The current study employed a 2 (Cognitive Style: FI, FD) × 2 (Training strategy: active engagement, passive engagement) betweensubjects pretest-posttest design. The study procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Pretest. The total duration of the training was six weeks. One week prior to the training, all students participated in the pretest in their classrooms, guided by the researcher and research assistant. Students were first asked to complete the PANAS to assess their current emotional state. Next, following task instructions, students were guided to complete the six creativity tasks (see Section 2.2) to assess their baseline creativity levels. Finally, students completed the EFT to determine their cognitive style. The pretest lasted approximately 30 min.

Based on prior classification criteria (Gu et al., 2022b; Tinajero & P´aramo, 1997), participants who scored above the median score were classified as FI, while those who scored below the median were classified as FD. The researchers conducted a Shapiro-Wilk test on the EFT scores, indicating a normal distribution (p = .065) with a median score of 8.00. Therefore, students scoring ≤8 were classified as FD, and those scoring > 8 were classified as FI. The distribution of cognitive styles across training groups and grades is shown in Table 1.

Creativity Training. Ten exercises were implemented, each adapted from established theoretical frameworks and empirical studies (Meyer, 2011; Oleynick et al., 2014; Osborn, 1953; Ritter & Mostert, 2017; Ritter et al., 2012; Skillicorn, 2013; Torrance, 1966; Wu et al., 2019, 2018). These techniques were designed to target one or more components of the 5-I training framework. An overview of these exercises is provided in the Table 2. Four training groups were established according to study design. Each group was led by a trained research assistant (main trainer) and a teacher. Before the training began, four trainers participated in a preparatory session to ensure their full understanding of the study’s objectives, training content, and procedures. To maintain consistency, all trainers fol­ lowed a standardized instructional procedure. Each trainer was subsequently assigned to a specific group, while classroom teachers assisted with maintaining order and facilitating the overall flow of the sessions.

Following the pretest, students participated in eight training sessions over a four-week period (two sessions per week), during which they were introduced to ten creative thinking techniques. Each session lasted approximately 20 min and covered one or two techniques, depending on the scheduling constraints set by the school. Each session began with a short instructional video (2–3 min), which was played twice to reinforce comprehension of the target technique. Students were required to watch the videos in their classroom. For example, while learning the "conceptual combination" technique which targeted Ideation, Interaction, and Identifi­ cation components of the 5-I framework, the video first defined and explained the cognitive mechanism of conceptual combination, demonstrated its use through an example, and then provided a brief summary. 4. General discussions

The current study investigated how cognitive style interacts with training engagement to influence creativity in a blended learning environment among adolescents. Overall, the findings suggest that targeted training—whether through active or passive engage­ ment—can effectively enhance different dimensions of creativity. The findings also suggested an interaction effect between cognitive style and training strategy, although these effects varied across creativity tasks and specific indices, implying the necessity of differentiated instructional approaches in creativity training.

4.1. Theoretical implications

In the current study, for the AUT that emphasize creativity indices such as flexibility and originality, FD participants benefited more from passive engagement than active engagement. This aligns with prior research suggesting that FD learners often exhibit lower efficacy in self-directed learning and require more effort to assimilate what they have learned (Chen & Macredie, 2002; Klahr & Nigam, 2004). Passive engagement in our study may have provided FD participants with structured opportunities to review and internalize creative strategies of video content after the learning phase (Endres et al., 2020). However, this finding contradicts with Gu et al. (2022b), who observed that FD university students performed better under active engagement. This discrepancy may be attributed to university students’ higher prior knowledge, cognitive abilities, and metacognitive skills compared to primary school students (Brod, 2021b), making them more suited for knowledge construction through active engagement. Primary school students, on the other hand, may still be developing these abilities, and thus passive engagement better supports their cognitive processing. In contrast, FI par­ ticipants had no significant difference in the AUT performance between training strategies, which is consistent with previous studies that found FI participants to be relatively unaffected by training methods due to their strong autonomy, self-regulation, and cognitive independence (Gu et al., 2022b).

A different interaction pattern emerged in the creative imagination task, which requires both divergent thinking and coherent image construction, an opposite interaction pattern emerged. In this task, FD participants showed higher flexibility and originality under active engagement, while FI participants performed better under passive engagement. Creative imagination task relies on the integration of prior experiences and the generation of novel mental representations (Wang et al., 2023). Active engagement may provide FD participants, who are typically more sensitive to external cues and may rely more on structured guidance (Witkin et al., 1977; Yang & Chen, 2020), the necessary stimulation to broaden their idea space. Meanwhile, FI participants likely benefitted from the more focused and less divergent nature of passive engagement, which could provide them with the cognitive space to generate internally coherent and novel images (Lei et al., 2021).

This explanation may also indicate why in the scientific design task, FI participants outperforming FD participants under passive engagement in generating significantly more useful ideas, as well as outperformed themselves relative to the active engagement. The scientific design task emphasizes practicality and goal-directed creativity, requiring students to generate solutions that are not only original but also applicable and effective. Passive engagement may allow FI participants to consolidate knowledge and align it with functional constraints more effectively. Given their high cognitive autonomy, FI participants are capable of internalizing problem structures and engaging in evaluative thinking without external prompting (Benedek et al., 2014). In contrast, active engagement may have introduced excessive cognitive load or encouraged unfocused ideation, detracting from the practical value of the designs (Skulmowski, 2024).

In the drawing tasks, which requires open-ended and free exploration, the results revealed no interaction effects for FD participants. However, FI participants exhibited greater openness under active engagement, possibly due to the exploratory freedom it afforded. Nevertheless, this heightened openness appeared to enhance only the dispositional tendency toward open-mindedness rather than improving cognitive indicators of creativity, such as originality and elaboration. On the other hand, for FD participants, the openended and unstructured nature of the task may have limited the influence of engagement strategy, as they tend to prefer more goal-directed or externally scaffolded environments (Li et al., 2023).

Interestingly, no significant interaction effects were found on the RAT, a convergent thinking task, or on creative self-efficacy.

4.2. Practical implications

Blended learning, which integrates the flexibility of digital tools with the interactive classroom activities, offers a promising environment for fostering student creativity. This study underscores that effective creativity training in blended settings requires more than just combining online and offline elements—it must also be responsive to the type of creative task and individual differences in cognitive style.

Our findings suggest that educators should tailor engagement strategies based on students’ cognitive profiles and the specific creative goals of the training. For example, passive engagement such as reviewing materials may help FD learners in acquiring creative cognitive skills better by reducing cognitive load and offering structured review opportunities. In contrast, when the goal is to stimulate imaginative thinking, FD learners may benefit more from active engagement, such as hands-on tasks, group discussions, or open-ended challenges, which offer external scaffolding and stimulate exploration. FI learners, who are more cognitively autonomous, tend to benefit from passive engagement when working on complex, goal-oriented tasks like scientific design, where reduced external input can create space for reflection and integration of ideas.

Teachers can use these insights to design flexible instructional strategies within a blended format. For example, a creativity lesson might begin with a video to build foundational knowledge, followed by interactive activities aligned with the targeted creative out­ comes and tailored to learners’ cognitive styles.

4.3. Limitations and future directions

The current study has some limitations and provokes new considerations. First, the study did not show interaction effects on all aspects of creativity tasks. Future research should further validate the current findings and optimize the training environment. This includes extending the duration of online video learning or entirely adopting online training formats to minimize distractions from group training. Moreover, prior research indicates that individuals with different cognitive styles have different preferences for control strategies (Deimann & Keller, 2006). However, the current study required participants to follow the training according to structured procedures. This lack of control over training may lead to mismatches between participants’ actual training pace and preferred learning pace, thereby influencing training outcomes. Therefore, future research is needed to further explore the impact of control strategies on creativity training outcomes for individuals with different cognitive styles.

In this study, only the frequency of participation was recorded, while individual students’ real-time engagement during the creativity training sessions was not assessed. This limitation may have influenced the observed learning outcomes. Prior research has shown that when course design effectively fosters student engagement, students tend to become more motivated and innovative, leading to improved learning outcomes (Lin et al., 2019). Future research should address this gap by employing more precise and dynamic methods to capture individual engagement within group-based interventions.

Another potential limitation concerns the timing of creativity assessments. Participants were required to complete each creativity task within predetermined time limits, based on protocols from prior research (e.g., three-minute AUT tasks as implemented in Gu et al., 2021; Ritter et al., 2020). Although this standardized approach facilitates comparisons across studies, it may restrict the expression of participants’ full creative potential. As emphasized by Said-Metwaly et al. (2020), longer time are associated with increased originality in divergent thinking tasks, while fluency and flexibility remain relatively stable. Future studies should explore the impact of extended or open-ended assessment durations to more accurately capture the breadth and depth of creativity following training.

5. Conclusions

This study highlights the critical role of aligning cognitive style, engagement strategy, and task type in creativity training within blended learning environments. FI learners showed stable performance across engagement types involving creative cognition and autonomy like the AUT. However, they derived greater benefit from passive engagement when completing tasks that required mental representation and evaluative thinking, such as creative imagination and scientific design. FD learners also benefited from passive formats when learning cognitive skills in more structured contexts, but performed better with active engagement during tasks that encouraged imaginative exploration. These differentiated patterns suggest that a one-size-fits-all approach to creativity training is insufficient. Instead, instructional strategies should be adapted not only to learners’ cognitive styles but also to the specific cognitive demands of the creative tasks involved. The blended training model developed in this study—combining online video content with classroom activities—offers a practical and adaptable framework for creativity instruction. It enables educators to personalize learning experiences, accommodate diverse cognitive styles, and foster a more inclusive and responsive learning environment. By considering the interplay between learner traits and task characteristics, educators can more effectively support the development of creativity across varied educational contexts.
