1. Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) technology has the poten­ tial to generate educational content, improve student engagement and interaction, and provide personalized learning experiences, given its capability to generate texts, images, and videos (Chiu, 2023; Kasneci et al., 2023). ChatGPT, a popular GenAI technology, has been integrated into a range of tasks for diverse disciplines, such as translation and essay writing for language learning and computational thinking tasks for computer education, thereby supporting the development of literacy and problem-solving skills (Bilal Khallel, 2024; Kasneci et al., 2023). Its impact on student engagement is multifaceted, with evidence suggesting both positive and negative effects. Student engagement is viewed as multidimensional, including behavioral, cognitive, and emotional components (Chiu, 2021, 2022; Christenson et al., 2012; Fredricks et al., 2004; Sinatra et al., 2015), which is associated with student learning attention, academic performance, and emotional wellbeing. Generative artificial intelligence, such as ChatGPT, has been increasingly integrated into education to change student learning experience. Current empirical studies have mixed results on how ChatGPT impacts student behavioral, cognitive, and emotional engagement. This systematic literature review and meta-analysis explores whether and how ChatGPT impacts student behavioral, cognitive, and emotional engagement. We used the PRISMA method to select, analyze, and report the results. We screened 766 articles from four databases and identified 17 empirical studies with 1735 students for analysis. We compared the effect on student engagement between ChatGPT-based and non-ChatGPT learning. We found a medium effect size on overall student engagement in ChatGPT-based learning in the random effects model. Our analyses further suggest that ChatGPT- based learning is more effective in fostering student behavioral (medium effective size), cognitive (large effective size), and emotional engagement (medium effective size) than non-ChatGPT learning. Our findings revealed ChatGPT is an effective tool for engaging students in learning. We also suggested three roles ChatGPT plays in fostering student engagement: personalized tutoring, programming and technical assistance, and content gen­ eration and collaboration. Our systematic literature review revealed potential risks and results in student disengagement, such as over-reliance.

2. Literature review

2.1. ChatGPT in education

Chatbots as text-based communication agents were first developed in the early 2000s for language learning, but they tended to provide vague and inaccurate responses that sometimes mislead students (Fryer et al., 2019; Yin et al., 2021). Therefore, students had relatively limited engagement with the chatbots due to their limited educational values. With the advance of GenAI, an AI-empowered chatbot with enhanced conversational capability built on a GPT-3.5 large language model, ChatGPT, was launched to support natural language processing that allows humans to interact with it in conversational language (Chiu, 2023, 2024). Unlike old-generation rule-based chatbots, which can only provide predefined and fixed responses based on students’ responses, ChatGPT can flexibly handle more complex and adaptable contexts (Ng et al., 2024). Relevant studies highlighted the potential of ChatGPT in supporting group interactions in the online learning environment by facilitating group discussions and providing explanations on certain topics (Bilal Khallel, 2024; Javaid et al., 2023). It handles interactions with multiple users spontaneously. Some educators applied ChatGPT as a personalized feedback tool to guide students through the learning activities, while some educators treated ChatGPT as an intelligent tutoring system to provide relevant information and materials to pro­ mote adaptive learning and self-regulated learning. Therefore, ChatGPT could impact student engagement.

2.2. Student engagement and ChatGPT

Student engagement has been studied by researchers to understand the relationship between coursework and student learning (Axelson & Flick, 2010), the amount of student effort and knowledge, and the im­ pacts from environmental and individual factors (Bakker et al., 2015). Student engagement refers to students’ active involvement in the learning activities and commitment to fulfilling the learning outcomes (Christenson et al., 2012). 2.3. The need for this systematic literature review and meta-analysis

Some meta-analyses have examined the effects of ChatGPT on stu­ dent learning. For example, a meta-analysis of 12 early studies in 2022–2023 conducted by Rabiu and Nuhu (2024) showed positive im­ pacts of ChatGPT on the teaching and learning process; other metaanalysis studies also revealed a significant effect of using ChatGPT on learning in language (Wang et al., 2024), medicine (Levin et al., 2024), and education (Li et al., 2024). Li et al. (2024) argued that students require teacher support to engage ChatGPT in learning, supported by an empirical study conducted by Chiu et al. (2024). Moreover, Zhao et al.’s (2023) systematic review suggested how ChatGPT could impact student engagement remain unclear.

To the best of our knowledge, no meta-analyses have examined the impact of ChatGPT on student engagement. This gap is notable given the increasing interest in ChatGPT, which has implications for its applica­ tion in education to enhance students’ learning experience. The absence

3.1. Research questions

This systematic literature review and meta-analysis study is to syn­ thesize the available evidence on ChatGPT’s impact on student engagement across different educational contexts. We computed the effect size to reveal whether there is a statistical significance between ChatGPT and student engagement. Screening and inclusion criteria were adopted to select articles for the primary analysis. First, 39 duplicate records were removed. Two independent raters then examined the titles and abstracts of the articles to identify articles related to ChatGPT and student engagement. The full texts of the remaining 66 articles were then read to exclude further papers irrelevant to AI or not empirical studies or missing data. Finally, 17 articles were included in the meta-analysis (See Fig. 2 and Table 1). This number of the articles offer reliable analysis which is supported by studies such as 14 articles in Zhan et al. (2022) and 13 articles in Benavides-Varela et al. (2020).

We used Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta- Analyses (PRISMA) to identify articles for analysis and present the re­ sults (Tricco et al., 2018).

3.2. Article selection process

To identify articles for the analysis, we retrieved four widely used digital academic databases related to education: Web of Science, Scopus, Proquest, and ERIC. ChatGPT and engagement are two key components in our research questions, which allowed use to retrieve the relevant articles for this study. Therefore, the search query was “ChatGPT and engagement” in all fields including title, abstract and keywords. During the search process, the inclusive selection criteria are (a) journal articles, (b) written in English, (c) empirical studies, (d) educational research, and (e) published between 2021 and 2024. The exclusive criteria are (a) the intervention is not related to ChatGPT and (b) empirical studies miss key data. The initial and revised search was completed on August 24, 2024. The first three inclusive selection criteria are supported by related meta-analysis studies such as Benavides-Varela et al., 2020 and Zhan et al. (2022).

3.5. Computing effect size

We adopted the Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software version 4.0 to compute the effect sizes of the selected studies (Borenstein, 2022). All p-values reported were two-tailed. Heterogeneity was tested with the I2 test, of which heterogeneity can be considered unimportant when it falls between 0% and 40%. In contrast, it is moderate at 30%–60%, signifi­ cant at 50%–90%, and substantial when it falls between 75% and 100% (Higgins & Thompson, 2002). The random effects were used as we

We computed the effect size for each eligible study to fit the assumption of independence that the data of different outcomes were derived from different samples (Hedges et al., 2010). It could be prob­ lematic to consider the outcomes of the same set of samples as inde­ pendent, as this could lead to an erroneously small standard error and a narrow confidence interval for the point estimates computed across outcomes. Nevertheless, if a study involved different sets of samples without overlap, we computed one effect size for each sample set. If a study had multiple dimensions of student engagement reflected from the same set of samples, we treated their sub-studies as distinct studies to allow for a more nuanced understanding of the effects being measured (Borenstein & Higgins, 2013). For instance, while reporting impacts on students’ learning, the measurement was chosen across three di­ mensions of student engagement: cognitive, behavioral, and emotional (Fredricks et al., 2004).

3.6. Publication bias

Publication bias arises from the tendency of researchers, reviewers, and editors to favor the publication of positive research findings (Turner, 2013). Publication bias is reflected when the potential distor­ tion of treatment effects was assessed with Funnel plots using Hedges’s g with random effects (Duval & Tweedie, 2000). In an unbiased scenario, the plot should display a symmetrical inverted funnel. Conversely, if publication bias is present, the plot will exhibit asymmetry and skew­ ness. Suppose the plot appears somewhat asymmetric, with more studies on the right side of the mean effect size (positive side). In that case, it implies a potential publication bias, as negative or non-significant studies might be underrepresented.

To address the research questions regarding student engagement, the included studies’ findings and discussions were analyzed thematically using an inductive thematic approach by two independent raters. The general framework for the thematic analysis followed the threedimensional model of student engagement (Fredricks et al., 2004), involving behavioral, emotional, and cognitive engagement.

This indicated that students in ChatGPT-mediated learning activities had gained significantly more engagement levels than those involved in non-ChatGPT learning activities.

4.2.2. Behavioral engagement Six studies involving 435 participants were analyzed to investigate the effects of ChatGPT and non-ChatGPT interventions on student behavioral engagement. Six independent effect sizes were calculated. The Q statistic value indicated the presence of significant heterogeneity among the studies (Q = 26.142, I2 = 81%, p < 0.001). Using a random effects model (Fig. 5), an overall medium effect size of Hedges’s g was 0.454 (95% CI = 0.055 to 0.853) (Sullivan & Feinn, 2012), indicating that students participating in ChatGPT-mediated learning activities exhibited significantly greater behavioral engagement compared to those engaged in non-ChatGPT learning activities.

Despite the overall positive findings, the funnel plot analysis (Fig. 6) appeared relatively asymmetric, suggesting potential publication bias (Duval & Tweedie, 2000). Kendall’s Tau calculation (tau = 0.194, pvalue = 0.44) indicated a weak positive correlation with a nonsignificant p-value, implying no strong statistical evidence for publica­ tion bias, although visual inspection suggests caution.

Among the six studies that examined the impact of ChatGPT on student behavioral engagement, five reported a statistically significant positive effect (Dindorf et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Yilmaz et al., 2023), while one study indicated that ChatGPT did not significantly improve students’ behavioral engagement compared to a control group at certain points in time (Saritepeci & Yildiz Durak, 2024). However, their findings still showed evidence of overall improvement in reflective thinking for both the experimental and control groups over time. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that ChatGPT has enhanced students’ help-seeking behaviors in science education (Ng et al., 2024), promoted more proactive behaviors in learning, and improving student behavioral engagement in self-regulation

4.3. How does ChatGPT foster behavioral, cognitive, and emotional engagement?

A summary of student engagement reported in the collected studies can be synthesized into three dimensions, see Table 3: (i) behavioral engagement (n = 20, 30%), which encompasses students’ participation, attention, and effort; (ii) cognitive engagement (n = 30, 45%), which reflects the level of investment in learning and mastering content; (iii) emotional engagement (n = 17, 25%), which relates to the emotions and reactions that facilitate task completion (Christenson et al., 2012; Fre­ dricks et al., 2004; Kahu, 2013).

We identified three main roles of ChatGPT, see Fig. 11, in supporting student learning, namely personalized tutoring (PT) (n = 10, 59 %), programming and technical assistance (TA) (n = 5, 29%), and content generation and collaboration (CG) (n = 2, 12%). Specifically, ChatGPT supports personalized tutoring by facilitating engaging dialogues, of­ fering informative responses, and delivering personalized supplemen­ tary materials that cater to each student’s diverse learning needs (Dindorf et al., 2024). For example, it has been shown to increase stu­ dent engagement by providing instant feedback and clarification on a diverse range of tasks, including decision-making (n = 1), English writing (n = 1), problem-solving tasks (n = 2), constructing program­ ming conceptual knowledge (n = 1), visual design tasks (n = 1), providing science learning instruction and recommendations (n = 3), and mathematics learning tasks (n = 1).

5. Conclusion, limitations, and future directions

5.1. Overall implications

We analyzed 17 articles to synthesize their data to understand better how ChatGPT-incorporated learning activities impact student behav­ ioral, emotional, and cognitive engagement. The analysis extends the discussion of existing frameworks of student engagement by incorpo­ rating insights specifically related to the use of ChatGPT in K12 and higher education (Fredricks et al., 2004; Zhao et al., 2023). Our findings from the meta-analyses revealed learning with ChatGPT fosters greater student behavioral, cognitive, and emotional engagement than learning without ChatGPT. The results from the systematic review suggest the major roles ChatGPT plays in promoting student engagement: PT, TA,

Our systematic literature review revealed potential risks and results in student disengagement, such as over-reliance. The findings contribute to our understanding from both a quantitative and qualitative perspec­ tive. They encourage more teachers to integrate ChatGPT into learning and teaching. They can also serve as a foundation for developing instructional activities and evaluating student learning outcomes in ChatGPT-supported learning environments in future research.

Overall, the findings suggest that ChatGPT could be an effective learning tool for enhancing student engagement. The current educa­ tional dilemma is that the student adoption rate of ChatGPT in education is faster than that of teachers. These findings encourage teachers to use ChatGPT in teaching and learning to keep up with the student paces. Although this meta-analysis suggests ChatGPT has positive effects on student engagement, most of the selected articles are set in an inter­ vention setting. The learning environments were under researcher control. These findings are driven by an experimental condition, which may not reflect the authentic learning environments. Therefore, we still need to be aware of the engagement effects, i.e., student disengagement brought by ChatGPT (Chiu, 2023, 2024).

5.2. Limitations and future research directions

This study has four limitations. First, to ensure the quality of the studies included in our analysis, we collected published empirical studies on applying ChatGPT interventions from journals. However, it is possible that papers from other databases, as well as grey literature (e.g., which include research or information that is not commercially pub­ lished or peer-reviewed, such as conference proceedings, government reports, theses, dissertations, technical reports, and other unpublished works), or papers missed due to limitations in the search strings, may have been excluded from our search. In future research, the inclusion and exclusion criteria for selecting studies could be expanded to encompass other types of literature, allowing for the inclusion of rele­ vant data from more diverse resources. Second, the number of included studies was relatively small for the meta-analysis due to the limited number of empirical studies that match the inclusion criteria. Therefore, future researchers are expected to collect more empirical studies on adopting different versions of ChatGPT or related large language models by focusing on its pedagogical applications. Third, this study compared the effects of student engagement in ChatGPT-incorporated learning activities to those of non-ChatGPT. However, some empirical studies have compared the effects of different disciplines or learning groups. For instance, Bilal Khallel (2024) compared the results of ChatGPT inte­ gration in independent, peer, and group learning, group. In future research, scholars can conduct meta-analyses with moderator analysis to compare the effects of individual and other learning groups. Fourth, this study analyzed the data from articles conducted at different educational levels due to the insufficient number of studies. The way K-12 students engage with ChatGPT should be different from university students. Therefore, we suggest future studies should focus on one educational level.
