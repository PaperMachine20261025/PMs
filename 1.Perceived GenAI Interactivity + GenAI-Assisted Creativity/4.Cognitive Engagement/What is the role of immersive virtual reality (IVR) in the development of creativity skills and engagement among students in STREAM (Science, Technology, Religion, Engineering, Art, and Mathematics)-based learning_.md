What is the role of immersive virtual reality (IVR) in the development of creativity skills and engagement among students in STREAM (Science, Technology, Religion, Engineering, Art, and Mathematics)‑based learning?

Melese Astatke1 · Cathy Weng1 · Abebayehu Yohannes1

Received: 20 December 2023 / Accepted: 5 May 2025 / Published online: 22 May 2025 © The Author(s) 2025

Abstract With the growing availability of immersive technologies, such as Immersive Virtual Reality (IVR), students are now afforded greater opportunities to explore diverse educational domains, including Science, Technology, Religion, Engineering, Arts, and Mathematics (STREAM). However, there remains a paucity of research investigating the efficacy of IVR-based STREAM approaches in enhancing students’ creativity and engagement. The present study seeks to address this gap by designing and evaluating an IVR-based STREAM approach tailored specifically for religious studies in middle school education. The primary objective of the research is to assess the impact of this innovative approach on students’ creativity and engagement. A sample of 40 seventh-grade students was recruited and randomly assigned to one of two groups: the experimental group, which engaged with the religious studies lesson using Head-Mounted Displays (HMD), and the control group, which utilized desktop-based virtual reality (DVR) to interact with the same content. To evaluate the outcomes, preand post-intervention questionnaires were administered to measure student engagement, while creativity was assessed using a standardized rubric. The findings of the study indicate that students in the experimental group, who interacted with the lesson through IVR, exhibited significantly higher levels of creativity and engagement compared to their counterparts in the control group. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of IVR-based STREAM approaches in religious education and highlight the benefits of immersive technologies in formal education. The study offers valuable insights for educators and policymakers, advocating for innovative, interactive teaching methods that enhance creativity and active student engagement.

Keywords  Immersive virtual reality (IVR) · STREAM · Creativity skills · Engagement

1  Introduction

The traditional model of single-discipline education and productivity-focused learning is increasingly inadequate in equipping students with the ability to synthesize knowledge and address complex, real-world challenges, failing to meet contemporary societal needs (Huang, 2020). In response, Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) education has gained prominence by dismantling the boundaries between conventional academic subjects and integrating these fields into a unified curriculum, aimed at better preparing students for the demands of the global economy (Yakman, 2008; Yakman & Lee, 2012).

A more inclusive and interdisciplinary education paradigm, known as STEAM education, combines art with STEM subjects, promoting creativity and offering a balanced approach (Hsiao & Su, 2021; Kim & Bolger, 2017). STEAM activities have shown promise in capturing students’attention and fostering their creativity (Hong et al., 2020; Khamhaengpol et al., 2021), which is a crucial twenty-first-century skill for complex problem-solving.

The STREAM education model is gaining international attention for promoting holistic and creative learning (Dzyna, 2023). By integrating religion and the arts with science and technology, STREAM offers a more inclusive and culturally relevant approach than traditional STEM. Educators are embracing it to help students connect logical thinking with creativity and navigate a complex world.

Globally, countries like Finland, Singapore, and the U.S. have adopted STREAM to foster critical thinking, collaboration, and innovation—often supported by technologies like VR and digital design tools (Dyulgerova et  al., 2023; Watanabe & Uemura, 2023). Regionally, STREAM is adapted to reflect cultural and spiritual values, especially in African, Asian, and Middle Eastern contexts, where religion and traditional art are integral to education (Sismanto et al., 2024).

Numerous studies have highlighted the positive outcomes of implementing STEAM-based learning, including improved concept mastery and creativity (Wandari et  al., 2018), enhanced self-efficacy and motivation (Bedar & Al- Shboul, 2020; Conradty et al., 2020), and increased academic achievement (Tan et  al., 2020). Integrating technology, particularly virtual reality (VR), into the STEAM concept has further been shown to enhance learning effectiveness and students’engagement (Su, 2019).

While strong empirical evidence remains limited, several studies have begun to explore the impact of IVR on learners’skills and competencies. For instance, Yang et al. (2018) found that the immersive nature of IVR enhances skill transfer, with participants in IVR settings achieving higher post-test scores compared to traditional methods. Similarly, Miguel-Alonso et  al. (2023) emphasize that IVR promotes higher levels of engagement, which is critical for effective learning. However, these studies also acknowledge the need for further research to confirm these findings. IVR experiences have been found to boost information retention and knowledge assimilation in contrast to conventional instructional approaches (Krokos et al., 2019), demonstrating the potential for VR-based education to enhance learning outcomes (Jensen & Konradsen, 2018; Molina-Carmona et al., 2018).

Despite the recognized benefits of STEAM education, certain disciplines, such as religious education, are frequently overlooked. To cultivate well-rounded individuals capable of addressing societal challenges and contributing meaningfully, there is a need to integrate religious elements within the STEAM framework. Religious education can complement STEAM by fostering empathy, enhancing critical thinking, and promoting spiritual, moral, social, and cultural development (Engebretson et al., 2010). Additionally, it can instill values of diversity and self-confidence in learners, broadening their perspectives and strengthening character (Kowino et al., 2011).

Although religion significantly contributes to the enhancement of science learning and creativity, there is limited research on the impact of IVR on students’creativity and engagement in STREAM-based religious education. To address this gap, this study aims to integrate IVR through HMDs into the STREAM classroom and examine its effects on middle school students’creativity and engagement during religious studies lessons. By exploring the intersection of IVR, STEAM, and religious education, this research seeks to expand the existing literature on this topic and shed light on the potential benefits of this innovative approach. In line with this, the following research questions are at the heart of the current investigation.

1.	 Does the creativity skill of students differ significantly between those who learn with an IVR-based STREAM approach and those who use the DVR-based STREAM approach? 2.	 Does the degree of engagement between students using the DVR-based STREAM approach and those using the IVR-based approach differ noticeably?

2  Literature review

2.1  Immersive virtual reality

VR technology, introduced by Sutherland in the 1960 s, has undergone significant development over the years. VR creates computer-generated simulations of threedimensional (3D) images or environments, aiming to immerse users in a sense of presence (Steuer, 1992). This capability allows users to experience realistic scenarios within a virtual world displayed on the screen, making VR an increasingly prominent and common part of our daily lives (Suh & Prophet, 2018).

In the educational realm, various advantages of incorporating VR in educational settings have been acknowledged by researchers. Firstly, VR provides 3D virtual worlds with enhanced interactions that greatly motivate learning and encourage active participation rather than passive observation (Kim & Song, 1997; Oigara, 2018). Secondly, VR offers students unique educational experiences by enabling them to interact with various scenarios from anywhere and at any time. Thirdly, VR facilitates learning experiences that are difficult to replicate in traditional classroom settings, making it a valuable tool for relevant and engaging education (Bailenson, 2018). Furthermore, Bonner and Reinders (2018) have pointed out that VR has the capacity to minimize distractions in the classroom.

VR is commonly classified into non-IVR (desktop VR) and IVR, as outlined by Buttussi and Chittaro (2018). Non-IVR displays the simulated environment on the display of a desktop computer, where interactions commonly involve the utilization of a mouse and keyboard (Lee & Wong, 2014). On the other hand, IVR involves higher levels of interaction and often requires costlier external equipment, such as HMDs, which can enhance realism and immersion within the virtual setting (Liu et al., 2020), leading to more immersive and effective learning experiences compared to non-IVR setups.

Researchers have investigated the application of VR across diverse educational contexts, including ecosystems science, geography, geometry, and history. Prior researches have highlighted the positive effects of VR on experiential learning, spatial knowledge representation, motivation, and overall learning performances (Dickes et al., 2019; Yildirim et al., 2018; Hwang & Hu, 2013). To gain further insights into the influence of immersive virtual environments in fostering students’creativity skills and engagement during religious study lessons, this study employed a HMD-based VR on STREAM learning approach. Despite some drawbacks, such as motion sickness in HMD usage, learners generally express satisfaction with IVR experiences (Sun et al., 2017). Additionally, IVR has been found to enhance memory recall in contrast to VR conditions on desktop computers (Krokos et al., 2019), highlighting its potential benefits for educational purposes.

2.2  STEAM/STREAM education

The philosophical aspect of integration within a STREAM (Science, Technology, Religion, Engineering, Arts, and Mathematics) framework is grounded in the belief that knowledge is inherently interconnected and that education should reflect the holistic nature of human experience (Mani & Sharma, 2023). Rather than treating each discipline as an isolated subject, STREAM emphasizes the unity of knowledge and encourages learners to explore the relationships between different fields. This integration supports a more meaningful and relevant learning process, where students not only acquire information but also understand how to apply it in real-world contexts that are complex, cultural, and multidisciplinary (Meier, 2018).

At a deeper level, this philosophical approach values the development of the whole person—intellectually, emotionally, morally, and spiritually. By including religion and the arts alongside science and technology, STREAM recognizes the importance of ethical reasoning, spiritual reflection, and creative expression in shaping responsible and well-rounded individuals. It acknowledges multiple ways of knowing, such as analytical thinking, artistic intuition, and cultural wisdom, thereby fostering respect for diverse perspectives. Ultimately, the integration within STREAM prepares learners not only to solve technical problems but also to engage thoughtfully and ethically with the world around them, making education both transformative and purposeful.

STEAM education plays a crucial role in shaping the future world and is expected to serve as the basis of the rapidly growing economy (Leahy et al., 2019). However, numerous communities are currently facing a STEAM skill crisis (Jesionkowska et al., 2020), making it imperative to promote STEAM education to equip learners

for the demands of the twenty-first century and equip them to address practical challenges (Thuneberg et al., 2018). It has been suggested that STEAM is designed to improve students’critical thinking, creativity, collaboration, innovation, and interpersonal communication skills, all of which are essential for success in the modern era (Perignat & Katz-Buonincontro, 2019).

In addition to higher-order thinking skills, character development is also crucial in education. Religious education can play a supportive role in shaping morals and ethics, reinforcing the foundation of STEM identities (Rodriguez et al., 2019; Scheitle & Ecklund, 2018). Furthermore, integrating religion into STEAM has shown positive associations with various educational outcomes for adolescents (McMorris, 2016; Mubarok et al., 2020). McMorris (2016) revealed that increased religious involvement is related to positive academic gains and overall schooling improvement.

The development of STREAM skills can foster metacognitive abilities, enabling individuals to consciously think about problems, select appropriate strategies, and make effective decisions for successful problem-solving and task execution. Adopting a STREAM approach reflects the interconnectedness of disciplines in real life and encourages innovation and the discovery or design of new things among students. STREAM-based learning enhances problem-solving, logical thinking, and information technology mastery (Devi et al., 2018).

In line with the need for meaningful and engaging connections and applications, the present research was intended to examine the impact of immersive virtual environments on students’creativity skills and engagement. By designing a STREAM- based religion study lesson, the study seeks to explore the possible benefits of immersive virtual environments in enhancing students’learning experiences and preparing them for the challenges of the twenty-first century.

2.3  Creativity

In any field, creativity refers to the capacity to generate innovative and valuable concepts and products (Amabile, 1996). Divergent thinking, an essential component of creativity (Madden et al., 2013; Sousa & Pilecki, 2013), enables individuals to generate multiple solutions to a problem, making it a valuable skill. Creativity goes beyond mere"thinking"; it involves"creating something original"(Sousa & Pilecki, 2013) and culminates in the production of unique outcomes. Nurturing creativity in the classroom is crucial and can be achieved through various activities. VR technology, utilizing three-dimensional display tools, offers dynamic presentations and interactions (Kwon et  al., 2020; Yang et  al., 2019), thereby promoting creative flexibility (Fleury et al., 2020; Fröhlich et al., 2018). For the present study, creativity is defined as the novelty of the response students present, accompanied by multimedia supplements. The creative products align with the elements of STREAM aspects. These aspects encompass students’scientific knowledge (S), the use of technology to address questions (T), the identification of religious meanings attached to diagrams in their own words (R), the design of temples (E), the artistic techniques employed in drawing (A), and the application of geometry knowledge to describe the temple (M).

2.4  Students’ engagement

Active participation in the classroom is a crucial factor in determining teenagers’academic success, college major choices, and potential careers in STEM fields (Wang & Degol, 2014). Engaged students devote their attention and energy to mastering subjects, persevere through challenges, build supportive relationships with peers and professionals, and maintain a strong connection with their school community (Wang & Eccles, 2012). Therefore, active student engagement is a crucial element of effective learning. (Appleton et al., 2008).

In accordance with the theoretical framework of self-system motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000), engagement includes both visible and hidden aspects of how students interact with learning activities. While scholars acknowledge the multidimensional nature of student engagement, there is no unanimous agreement on the specific dimensions to be delineated (Fredricks et al., 2016). In the present study, we concentrated on three categories of engagement: cognitive, behavioral, and emotional. These dimensions of student engagement are intricately woven into individuals and operate across different levels, encompassing subject areas, school environments, and moment-to-moment actions.

Our study delved into engagement within social science classroom settings, as we sought to investigate the impact of IVR on STREAM-based instruction and its effect on student engagement. IVR has been gaining significant interest in recent times (Checa & Bustillo, 2020; Radianti et al., 2020) due to its ability to immerse students in diverse environments, providing them with first-hand experiences and encouraging active involvement in various learning tasks. The three interrelated engagement dimensions (cognitive, behavioral, and emotional) are explored further in the following sections.

2.4.1  Behavioral engagement

As per Fredericks et  al. (2016), behavioral engagement is evident through good behavior, the lack of disruptive behaviors, and active participation in class and academic tasks. Past studies have evaluated behavioral engagement using measures like attention, involvement, concentration, completion of assignments, and adherence to classroom norms (Fredricks & McColskey, 2012). A research carried out by Liu et al. (2020) discovered that students in the IVR classroom had abundant opportunities for active engagement in collaborative activities.

2.4.2  Emotional engagement

Emotional engagement involves cultivating positive emotional responses towards peers, teachers, and classroom activities, coupled with a genuine appreciation for learning and a keen interest in the subject matter (Conner, 2011). Typically, emotional engagement is measured through students’emotional reactions, including enjoyment, curiosity, and their perceived value of the learning experience (Fredricks & McColskey, 2012). A research undertaken by Liu et al. (2020) revealed that the integration of IVR in the classroom led to heightened engagement and motivation among students in science studies, consequently enhancing emotional engagement.

2.5  Cognitive engagement

Cognitive engagement entails using deep learning techniques, the practice of selfregulated learning, and the utilization of cognitive processes to grasp intricate concepts (Zimmerman, 1990). To assess cognitive engagement, items related to persistence, the application of deep and shallow learning techniques, and self-regulation were employed (Greene, 2015). In the IVR learning environment, students demonstrated increased effort in acquiring science knowledge and memorizing the displayed information through interactive manipulation of 3D objects, resulting in enhanced cognitive engagement (Liu et al., 2020) Figs. 1, 2.

The use of 360-degree video allowed students to explore their surroundings from all angles, providing a spherical vision that enabled movement of the film in different directions, granting them the freedom to choose where to direct their attention (Reyna, 2018). 3  Methodology

This section covers the research design, participants, measurements tools and experimental procedure.

3.1  Research design

A quasi-experimental design was employed to investigate the impact of IVR on STREAM-based instruction. Two classes were designated, where one is designated to the experimental group and the other to the control group. In the experimental group, religious studies lessons were conducted using IVR technology, while the control group received identical content through conventional classroom methods using desktop VR. Following the experiment, students engaged in STREAM-based activities to evaluate their creativity skills, and questionnaires were administered to assess their levels of engagement.

3.2  Participants

The subjects in this experiment comprised 7 th-grade students attending a middle school in Taipei City, Taiwan. The current study involved 40 students in total from two classes. One class was randomly selected to be the experimental group, comprising 20 students who utilized the IVR-based STREAM learning strategy. The other class, also consisting of 20 students, served as the control group, utilizing

the desktop VR-based STREAM approach. Both classes were taught by the same teacher, received the same learning content, and completed identical tasks within the same learning timeframe. The teaching subject for this study focused on the contents of tourism in Grade 7 religious studies.

3.3  Experimental procedure

Figure 4 presents a detailed flowchart of the experimental process used in this study. The experiment began with an introductory session for both groups of students, where they were introduced to the fundamental knowledge relevant to the day’s lesson content. This was done to ensure that all participants had a basic understanding of the subject matter before engaging in the learning activities.

Next, both groups completed a pre-questionnaire to assess their initial levels of engagement. This questionnaire was aimed at measuring how engaged students felt before interacting with the different types of learning materials.

Following the questionnaire, students were provided with various learning materials. These included conventional VR videos as well as 360-degree IVR videos, both of which were centered around the Guanshan Tianhou Temple, Taiwan a key subject of the study (as shown in Fig. 4). These materials were identical in content, ensuring that any differences in learning outcomes would be attributable to the mode of delivery rather than the subject matter itself.

3.4  Teacher’s role during the application of STREAM based activities

3.4.1  S – Science

•	 Guide students in identifying and discussing scientific concepts such as symmetry, stability, sound, light, and environmental orientation in temple architecture. •	 Provide examples or diagrams showing how scientific principles like gravity or solar alignment are applied. •	 Facilitate group experiments or demonstrations, such as using flashlights to simulate sunlight entering a temple at specific times.

3.4.2  T – Technology

•	 Introduce and demonstrate the use of digital tools (e.g., 3D modeling software, virtual tours, or educational apps) relevant to studying temple structures. •	 Encourage discussion about both ancient and modern technologies used in construction and visualization.

3.4.3  R – Religion

•	 Provide cultural and historical context to explain the symbolic meaning of temple parts. •	 Encourage respectful discussions about religious symbolism and traditions, making connections across various belief systems where appropriate. •	 Support students in conducting research or interviews (if possible) to understand local interpretations of temple architecture.

3.4.4  E – Engineering

•	 Facilitate design-thinking processes, helping students brainstorm, draft, build, and revise their temple models. •	 Ensure that students use correct measurement techniques and understand basic structural principles (like balance and load distribution). •	 Offer feedback on the feasibility and clarity of each design and encourage critical reflection on problems encountered.

3.4.5  A – Arts

•	 Provide visual examples and templates for temple features like carvings, pillars, or domes. •	 Encourage creative expression while linking artistic choices to cultural or symbolic meanings.

3.4.6  M – Mathematics

•	 Guide students in applying mathematical skills such as measuring, estimating, calculating area/volume, and identifying shapes in temple features. •	 Provide tools like rulers, protractors, graph paper, or digital measuring apps. •	 Help students connect their measurements to real-world contexts and validate their calculations through reasoning and discussion.

3.5  Measurements tools

To analyze the influence of IVR on students’ creativity skill and engagement in STREAM based approach, the current study employed engagement questionnaire and rubric for creativity skill.

The engagement scale, created by Wang et al. (2016), comprised 22 items evaluated on a Likert scale consisting of five points, ranging from 5 (strongly agree) to 1 (strongly disagree). Organized into three dimensions, it included eight items focused on cognitive engagement (e.g., I complete the social studies class assignments using IVR.), eight for behavioral engagement (e.g., I don’t give up when something is

difficult.), and six for emotional engagement (e.g., I’m excited for our social science lesson.). The factor loading indicated that all the items used to measure the latent variable grouped under each factor indicating that all items used were measured the expected latent variables (Appendix A). A high degree of reliability was shown by the Cronbach’s alpha scores for all three subscales and overall engagement, which were consistently greater than 0.7.

In addition to this, we assessed students’ performance in STREAM-based activities. The researchers evaluated these activities using four distinct criteria (as outlined in Table 1), which cover the dimensions of science, technology, religion, engineering, art, and mathematics. The rubric designed to assess students’creativity in STREAM activities was developed through a thorough review of existing literature and consultations with experts. This rubric, aligned with the STREAM elements, was validated by expert feedback. Its high interrater reliability (kappa = 0.84) further attests to its effectiveness in accurately evaluating creativity.

4  Results

4.1  Students’ engagement

4.1.1  Analysis of the engagement of the two groups

The students’ engagement before the intervention was analyzed by performing an independent t-test. Prior to engaging in the learning activities, the two groups did not exhibit a great difference from one another in terms of cognitive (t = 1.11, p = 0.27 > 0.05), behavioral (t = 0.32, p = 0.76 > 0.05), emotional engagement (t = 1.59, p = 0.12 > 0.05), and overall engagement (t = 1.15, p = 0.26 > 0.05), as indicated in Table 2. This indicates that students both groups had similar engagement before the intervention.

The researchers utilized one-way ANCOVA to investigate the variations in students’engagement between the two groups. The pre-engagement questionnaire was used as a covariate variable in this analysis. Prior to conducting the ANCOVA, Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances was performed to check whether the assumption of homogeneity of variances was met. The results indicated no violation of this assumption: F (1, 38) = 0.82, p = 0.37; F(1, 38) = 1.53, p = 0.22; F(1, 38) = 0.09, p = 0.76; F(1, 38) = 0.13, p = 0.72. Therefore, ANCOVA was appropriate for analyzing the post-questionnaire of engagement.

Table 3 shows that there were no significant differences between the two groups in cognitive engagement, behavioral engagement, and general engagement. However, a significant difference was found in emotional engagement between the experimental group and the control group, F(1, 38) = 4.97, p = 0.032. This result suggests that students who participated in the IVR-based STREAM approach demonstrated greater emotional engagement compared to those who experienced the desktopbased VR approach.

4.2  Creativity skills

Regarding creativity skills, the students’activities were scored by two researchers and one teacher with over 10 years of teaching experience. They based their assessments on the six elements of STREAM and four evaluation dimensions. The interrater reliability showed high consistency in their scores (kappa = 0.84).

As Table 4 indicates, the means and standard deviations (SDs) of the results in the religion dimension were 2.6 and 0.73 for the experimental group, and 2.10 and 0.45 for the control group. The t-test result (t = 2.75, p < 0.05) demonstrated a significant difference between the two groups. Similarly, for the engineering dimension, the experimental group’s scores were 2.45 and 0.60, while the control group’s values were 2.05 and 0.69.

A notable distinction between the two groups was also shown by the t-test score (t = 2.09, p < 0.05) in this dimension. Furthermore, for the arts dimension, the experimental group’s scores were 2.30 and 1.04, while the control group’s were 1.85 and 0.85. The t-test result showed that there was a significant difference between the two groups in this regard (t = 2.68, p < 0.05). These findings imply that the integration of the IVR-based STREAM approach into religious studies had a notable positive influence on students’creativity skills in religious, engineering, and arts aspects.

In this study, we designed an IVR-based STREAM approach for religious studies lessons using HMDs. Our objective was to investigate the effectiveness of this method in enhancing students’engagement and creativity skills. The experiment, conducted in a middle school setting, revealed several important findings.

First, our results showed that students who learned through the IVR-based approach demonstrated significantly higher emotional engagement compared to those using a desktop VR-based STREAM approach. This aligns with findings from previous research, which highlights the ability of immersive VR environments to enhance emotional involvement (Bouta et al., 2012; Lim et al., 2006). The immersive nature of IVR heightened students’interest and motivation, contributing to deeper emotional engagement during the lessons. Specifically, students participating in the IVR-based STREAM lessons displayed greater enthusiasm and excitement compared to those in desktop-based conditions. This observation is corroborated by Vesisenaho et al. (2019), who emphasize that VR’s immersive experience fosters a strong emotional connection to the learning environment. Despite certain challenges, such as limited control over learning scenes and the availability of IVR devices, students remained consistently emotionally engaged, illustrating the potential of IVR to enhance student engagement even in constrained conditions.

Second, with regard to creativity, students who engaged in the IVR-based STREAM approach demonstrated greater creativity, particularly in the religious, engineering, and artistic dimensions, compared to students using the desktop VR-based approach. These findings are consistent with those of Yang et al., (2019), who reported similar results in the context of VR-enhanced learning. The immersive experience provided by IVR facilitates an environment conducive to creativity by encouraging visualization, manipulation of images, openness to new experiences, and the evaluation of ideas (Hawlader & Poo, 1989). Our study found that the IVR approach not only increased students’ enjoyment and engagement but also led to enhanced creative performance, likely due to the heightened stimulation and relaxation experienced in the IVR environment. This supports Seligman and Csikszentmihalyi’s (2014) findings

that positive emotional states, such as enjoyment and relaxation, contribute to higher levels of creativity and concentration during VR applications.

Moreover, IVR’s ability to give students a sense of control and presence in the virtual environment significantly enhanced their retention and transfer of knowledge (Makransky et al., 2019). This contributed to improved motivation, self-efficacy, and ultimately, more creative outputs. Chang et al., (2020) further affirm that these factors lead to better creative design performance. The opportunity for students to explore innovative ideas in a visually immersive environment not only stimulated their interest but also encouraged them to integrate knowledge in meaningful ways. This process helped shape their knowledge systems and enhanced their creative abilities.

In conclusion, the findings of this study provide valuable evidence of the benefits of incorporating IVR-based STREAM learning in education. Not only does it enhance emotional engagement, but it also supports higher levels of creative performance, which are essential for fostering critical thinking and problem-solving skills. These results suggest that IVR has the potential to transform the educational landscape by offering a more immersive and stimulating learning experience.

5.1  Suggestions for future studies

While the findings of this study provide valuable insights into the impact of IVR- based STREAM learning on student engagement and creativity, several limitations were identified that present opportunities for further research. The following suggestions are proposed to guide and enrich future studies in this field.

1.	 Future research should consider involving a larger and more diverse sample across multiple schools, grade levels, and geographic locations. This would enhance the generalizability of the findings and allow for cross-cultural comparisons. A more representative sample would also help in identifying how different groups of students respond to IVR-based STREAM learning environments. 2.	 This study focused on short-term effects. Future research should adopt a longitudinal design to explore the long-term impact of IVR-based STREAM instruction on creativity, knowledge retention, and emotional engagement. Tracking changes over weeks or months would provide deeper insight into sustained learning outcomes and behavioral shifts. 3.	 Since this study acknowledged limitations regarding device accessibility and classroom control during IVR use, future studies should explore cost-effective solutions, infrastructure readiness, and teacher training programs to support the broader implementation of immersive STREAM instruction. Studies could also examine how to manage technical challenges and optimize the learning flow in real-world school environments. 4.	 Future research should include teachers’ perspectives on the design and delivery of IVR-based STREAM activities. Understanding their experiences, challenges, and instructional strategies would offer practical insights into improving lesson planning, classroom management, and assessment in immersive learning settings. 5.	 To build on the current use of questionnaires and rubrics, future studies should incorporate qualitative methods such as interviews, reflective journals, and

classroom observations. These can capture richer, more nuanced data on student engagement, creativity, and emotional responses, offering a deeper understanding of the learning experience.

These suggestions aim to broaden the evidence base for IVR-based STREAM education and ensure it evolves into a scalable, inclusive, and impactful approach to 21 st-century teaching and learning.
