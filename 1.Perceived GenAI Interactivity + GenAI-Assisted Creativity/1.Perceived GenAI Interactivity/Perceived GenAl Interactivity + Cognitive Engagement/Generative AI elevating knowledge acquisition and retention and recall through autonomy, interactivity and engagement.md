Abstract Purpose – Generative AI (GenAI) in education promises remarkable development changes that can enhance learner experience through the personalised and responsive learning process. In this paper, we investigate how the different features of GenAI – autonomy, interactivity and usability – contribute to student engagement and learning outcomes. Design/methodology/approach – From the lens of the self-determination theory, the study seeks to articulate how these GenAI features influence student engagement and their ability to achieve specific goals through interactive learning experiences delivered by GenAI. Qualtrics, a well-known online data collection tool, was employed in the study to collect data from 488 respondents in the UK region who had been exposed to generative AI-based platforms. The sampling approach used was representative, wherein the platform was asked to randomly share the survey with respondents in the UK region who had some experience interacting with Gen AI platforms. Findings – The paper’s findings show that increased autonomy and interactivity encourage students to engage more in GenAI platforms. Usability functions as a critical mediator with respect to how autonomy and interactivity are incorporated into engagement and skill acquisition. Skill development emerged as a key mediator of the direct effect between GenAI features and student engagement, confirming that it is an important determinant in engaging with educational content. Further, results reveal a significant positive moderation effect of personalisation between autonomy and engagement. Originality/value – The results extend the self-determination theory and offer theoretical suggestions for improving learning through AI-driven educational tools. For educators, the study provides practical insights to motivate the effective integration of GenAI platforms that can provide autonomy, interactivity, usability and personalisation to increase student engagement. Literature review 2.1 Self-determination theory Self-determination theory (SDT) (Ryan et al., 2023; Ryan and Vansteenkiste, 2023) provides a robust framework for understanding motivation within educational contexts. Central to SDT theory are the concepts of autonomy, competence, and relatedness—psychological needs that are essential for fostering intrinsic motivation and effective learning (Rahi et al., 2021). Autonomy in education refers to students’ sense of volition and choice in their learning activities (Johansen et al., 2023). When students perceive that they have control over their learning processes and can decide how they approach their studies, they are more likely to be intrinsically motivated (Patall et al., 2013). Offering students opportunities for self-direction and choice contributes to greater engagement and motivation (Printer, 2023). In the context of GenAI, autonomy is enhanced with AI-driven tools, facilitating learners’ ability to take control of their learning pace, access personalised resources, and discover information individually (Renfeng et al., 2025). Competence is strengthened through AI’s features that support adaptive feedback and interactive learning (Hsiao and Tang, 2024). Furthermore, relatedness may be augmented with AI-powered collaborative tools stimulating engagement with others, which creates a sense of connection in the learning environment (Shen et al., 2024).

Competence refers to the students’ beliefs concerning their capability to achieve goals and overcome challenges (Brauner et al., 2023). According to SDT, students’ intrinsic motivation is boosted when they feel competent in undertaking the required learning tasks (Printer, 2023). The process is encouraged and enhanced by giving proper tasks, timely feedback, and career settings, increasing competence (Alt et al., 2023). When the context developed by educators

permits students to succeed and learn step by step, students feel competent, and their performance improves (Wang and Zhu, 2019). It was highlighted that motivation must be complemented by effective teaching strategies consistent with students’ ability levels and constructive feedback (Ryan et al., 2023). Relatedness involves the learners’ feeling of recognition and acceptance within the learning community, which also plays a key role in determining the learning outcomes (Rahi et al., 2021). Students who experience supportive and caring relationships with peers and educators are likelier to be engaged and motivated (Leenknecht et al., 2023; Leo et al., 2023). The improvement of teacher-student relationships and encouragement of students’ teamwork can increase students’ belongingness, motivation, and achievement (Saggers et al., 2019).

In applying SDT to education, the theory emphasizes the importance of designing learning environments that satisfy these basic psychological needs (Rahi et al., 2021; Torbergsen et al., 2023). For instance, instructional approaches that offer students choices, align tasks with their abilities, and create a supportive classroom atmosphere have enhanced engagement and performance. Furthermore, SDT has implications for understanding various educational motivational phenomena (Evans et al., 2024). For example, the theory helps explain why some students are more intrinsically motivated than others and how external rewards can sometimes undermine intrinsic motivation (Jones et al., 2023). By highlighting the importance of satisfying students’ psychological needs, SDT provides valuable insights into designing educational practices that promote long-term engagement and learning (Ameloot et al., 2024).

Applying the self-determination theory (SDT) to the current study, autonomy refers to the learner’s ability to control the learning process, a critical factor in sustaining engagement (Ryan and Deci, 2000). Interactivity aligns with relatedness and dynamic feedback, which enhances the learner’s sense of connection and responsiveness within the educational environment (Moore, 1989). Usability, a construct grounded in the human-computer interaction literature (Nielsen, 1993), is theorized as a mediator that supports competence by making GenAI platforms accessible and effective. Engagement is conceptualized as a multidimensional construct reflecting behavioral, emotional, and cognitive investment in learning (Fredricks et al., 2004), while skill development represents the learner’s perceived competence, a key psychological need under SDT. Finally, personalization is a moderator that strengthens the autonomy–engagement link by tailoring learning experiences to individual preferences (Kay and Pasarica, 2019). These constructs are tightly integrated into SDT, enabling a coherent and theoretically grounded model to examine GenAI’s educational impacts.

2.2 GenAI GenAI is redefining educational approaches, particularly with progressive enhancements of learning strategies. These technologies apply higher-order computation processes to create, innovate, and share content that alters the approaches applied to the content in teaching and learning to be created and addressed (Moosman and Dryden, 2024). The scope of application of GenAI in education is quite broad after covering the aspects of intelligent learning, teaching, and content creation (Law, 2024). They are appreciated in the literature for their effectiveness in improving learning outcomes, relying on integrating learning processes with students’ needs and preferences (Luo, 2024). The incorporation of GenAI into the learning environment has many valuable prospects. One such is that it can indeed help to achieve the objective of personalization (Laak et al., 2024). Options concerning the content and learning activities that GenAI systems can teach can also correspond to the student’s preferences and needs, where the GenAI systems have the options for educational interactions with the students (Laak et al., 2024).

5. Analysis Partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) was used to examine the measurement and structural model studies.

5.1 Common method bias check In keeping with recent research (Talwar et al., 2020; Tan, 2025), the variance inflation factor (VIF) values were below 5.0, indicating that there is no multicollinearity (Ringle et al., 2020) or common method bias issues in the model (Kock, 2015). We added the marker variable to our measurement model to assess CMB to corroborate our findings better (Podsakoff et al., 2003). The outcomes confirmed that CMB had no appreciable impact on the study’s findings, as the differences in R2 between models with and without the marker variable fell below the acceptable cutoff.

5.2 Measurement model We utilized CFA to evaluate the model’s validity and reliability. To create a robust model, we followed the norms and standards established by earlier authors (Leguina, 2021; Henseler, 2017) and weeded out items with cross-loading difficulties or VIF values beyond the declared permissible limits. This caused us to drop a few items from the initial five-item scale. Cronbach’s alpha for the constructs and composite reliability were both more than 0.70 (Leguina, 2021), confirming the model’s validity and reliability (Table 3). The AVE value was more significant than 0.50, confirming the model’s construct validity (Bagozzi and Yi, 2012). We used the Fornell-Larcker and Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio (HTMT) criteria to evaluate the model’s discriminant validity. We found that the proposed model adhered to the discriminant validity (Henseler, 2017) (see Tables 4 and 5). The Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio (HTMT) is a widely accepted criterion for assessing discriminant validity in SEM. A threshold value of 0.85 for HTMT is considered conservative. It is commonly used to ensure rigorous discriminant validity (Henseler et al., 2015; Leguina, 2021). The HTMT ratio was used to assess discriminant validity with a threshold of 0.85. However, some values were above 0.85. While the values above 0.85 can indicate potential issues with discriminant validity, several scholars have suggested that the threshold can be relaxed to 0.90 in social sciences and behavioural research, especially when constructs are conceptually related. Hair et al., (2017) and Franke and Sarstedt (2019), suggest that HTMT values above 0.85 but below 0.90 are acceptable when constructs are theoretically related without compromising the model’s integrity. Furthermore, Henseler et al. (2015) note that the HTMT threshold is not absolute, and higher values do not automatically imply a lack of discriminant validity, especially if other criteria, such as the Fornell-Larcker criterion and item cross-loadings, also support construct distinctiveness. The moderation analysis extends these relationships by challenging participants’ goals of personalizing and collaboration. This result also suggests that personalization can address the kind of learning that a specific student is comfortable with and thereby make the learning experience meaningful in a way that will make the student more receptive to the information being taught. The moderating effect of personalisation also revealed a significant role in strengthening the link between autonomy and engagement. This aligns with cognitive fit and adaptive learning theories, suggesting that personalized learning experiences help students better align the educational content with their individual learning preferences and needs. As a result, autonomy becomes more meaningful when embedded within a personalized context, further motivating student engagement.

Interactivity was not found to have any interaction with collaboration towards the two factors of skill development or engagement. In other words, GenAI interactivity facilitates the improvement of such skills and engagement levels. However, adding collaborative factors does not seem to bolster these results in GenAI. This result calls for more research on how the collaborative features should be implemented to enhance the student engagement and learning activities achieved in the process. This may be because the current implementation of collaborative features is not ideal for integration with interactivity, or the students are more interested in having individual interactivity than collaborating in GenAI. Further study on other forms of integration that may be compatible with interactivity for improving of student achievement should be conducted in the future.

7. Implications 7.1 Theoretical implications The findings derived from this study are crucial for extending the theory concerning engagement in the digital learning environment outlined by the SDT Theory. Subsequently, this research demonstrates the importance of SDT in examining how different aspects of the educational AI platform, such as GenAI, impact students’ motivation and participation. This study contributes towards extending the use of SDT, showing how autonomy, in combination with GenAI learning platforms, can increase student engagement. Consequently, the students’ perceived autonomy levels revealed positive effects on the students’ depth of educational engagement. In this way, this operationalization of autonomy in digital platforms offers an idea of how the principles of SDT can be enacted in current educational contexts.

Skill development as an engaging variable can be seen as a mediator to engage students, underlining competence’s relevance in the SDT theory. This research demonstrates that

engagement is directly affected by skill development and also that skill development reflects the connection between autonomy and engagement. This supports the earlier argument that educational technologies should enhance content delivery and learning skills. Since skill development is one of the main factors contributing to motivation, platforms driven by GenAI should be more helpful to students in achieving this sense of competence.

The mediation role played here by usability in the interactivity–engagement relationship significantly highlights user-centered design, which is well aligned with SDT. Although usability has been generally examined within the framework of technology acceptance theories, this research contributes to this view by explaining how it plays a mediating role in the relationship between interactivity and engagement. It means that when interactive features are implemented, the design should be natural and easy to use to enhance students’ engagement and preserve what SDT regards as fundamental psychological needs.

The study adds a new dimension to the use of personalization and collaboration as the applied approach of SDT. The current study’s moderating role for personalization in the link between autonomy and engagement supports theories of differently differentiated instruction. The non-significant moderation by collaboration in interactivity and engagement brings into question conducted on interactive engagement and cooperative learning. This implies that the improvement in the usage of collaborative features might be subjected to particular parameters of context or some approaches to the use of collaborative features that were not addressed in the present research. As for theoretical developments in the future, it is necessary to further study in what context collaboration can effectively facilitate interactivity and engagement, and it might be helpful to draw upon research from social learning and cooperative learning theories.

7.2 Practical implications The results of this study have several practical implications for the design and implementation of AI-driven educational platforms like GenAI. The insights gained can guide educators, instructional designers, and educational technology developers in creating more effective and engaging learning environments for students. First, the strong positive relationships between autonomy, interactivity, usability, skill development, and engagement highlight the importance of incorporating features that promote these elements within educational platforms. Educators and developers should prioritise designing systems that give students high autonomy. This could include offering choices in learning paths, flexible scheduling, and personalised feedback, which can empower students and enhance their intrinsic motivation to engage with the material. Providing autonomy in this way aligns with the principles of studentcentred learning, fostering a sense of ownership and responsibility for their learning outcomes. Furthermore, educators can enhance engagement by offering students choices in interacting with content, embedding GenAI platforms into project-based learning, and encouraging inquiry–driven exploration. For example, ChatGPT and Gemini allow students to learn independently and at their own pace by asking questions, generating explanations, and exploring topics. The personalized and adaptive responses provided by these platforms align with the respective learning styles of the different students, which empowers them to take control of their learning and enhances their engagement and motivation (Tewari, 2024).

Second, the findings also highlighted the decisive role of skill development as a mediator in the relationship between platform usage and learning outcomes, which implies that educational platforms should provide comprehensive instruments for skills enhancement. This could include such activities as computer-based tutorials, samplings, modelling exercises, role-plays, or other hands-on activities where students must apply what has been learned in class. A study undertaken by Microsoft, MIT, Princeton University, and the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania has highlighted that developer productivity increased with the use of GitHub Copilot, with a 26% increase in productivity in the developers using the platform (Alford, 2024).

8. Limitations and directions for future research Despite the findings of this study, which provide insight into what may affect students’ engagement on the GenAI platform, there are certain limitations. These limitations suggest possible ways to guide future research to advance from the study’s findings. First, the study was conducted to produce research with a particular sample size and demographic, so it is hard to generalize the findings. It was also important to note that the participants were from a specific educational background. Thus, they may not be a true representation of all the students. Future research should recruit a larger sample size and ensure the participants are from different educational levels, cultural backgrounds, and fields of study. This approach would guarantee generalisability because the results would be extended to a larger population of learners and educational contexts. Certain limitations are inherent with any survey-based study, such as the self-selection bias (those with a more substantial interest in GenAI may have been more inclined to participate), recall bias (as participants reflect on prior GenAI use), and nonresponse bias. While we attempted to mitigate these through random sampling, screening,

and quality control measures, we acknowledge their presence, which can somewhat limit the generalisability of the findings.

Secondly, the study used a cross-sectional research design, which can only provide a singlepoint estimate for the relationships. This research design limits the inference of causality of the findings. Longitudinal studies are necessary in an attempt to describe the dynamics of the students’ interaction with the GenAI tool in detail over a period of time. Such studies would also make it possible for the researchers to determine how the various variables evolve over time and the impact of such evolution on other variables such as engagement, skill development, and usability perceptions. Thirdly, the findings were based mainly on statistical data, which is significant as it offered comparatively rich information concerning students’ attitudes and experiences. If either a direct method of data collection, such as interviews or focus groups, or surveys in question included open-ended questions, there would be more richness in how students interact with GenAI and in their personal understanding of the system. The qualitative findings might also complement the quantitative effects, offering a more comprehensive understanding of the reasons for persons’ levels of engagement and the challenges students encounter.

Fourth, this study looked at the level of engagement created by other specific concepts such as autonomy, interactivity, usability, and skill development. However, some other factors that could influence students’ behaviour and attitude toward the embrace of technology in learning were not considered. These include students’ past experiences, intrinsic motivation, and extraneous factors such as social support and the learning environment. Further research should include these other variables for a broader perspective of students’ engagement with AI-based learning systems. Finally, despite underlining the significance of the personalisation and collaboration priorities, the study did not provide a qualitatively detailed analysis of how these can be most efficiently designed and integrated. Further research should explore various approaches concerning learner-centered learning and methods that promote teamwork. Research could be conducted on different personalization methods or on many tools to discover which of the two ways is beneficial in improving the students’ performance and interest.
