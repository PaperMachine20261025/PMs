ABSTRACT With the increasing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into second language (L2) writing, learners' emotional experiences in AI-­mediated writing contexts have attracted growing scholarly attention. However, limited research has examined how L2 writing enjoyment develops over time or how it is influenced by learner-­ and interaction-­related factors in AI-­mediated environ­ ments. Guided by Complex Dynamic Systems Theory (CDST), this longitudinal study examines the developmental trajectory of L2 writing enjoyment and investigates the predictive roles of AI feedback literacy and perceived learner–AI interactivity. Data were collected from 328 participants at three time points across one semester and analysed using latent growth curve modelling (LGCM) to capture the developmental trajectory of writing enjoyment. Our results revealed a modest but significant upward increase in students' L2 writing enjoyment across the semester. In addition, both AI feedback literacy and perceived learner–AI interactivity significantly predicted the initial level of writing enjoyment as well as the rate of its development over time. These findings provide empirical support for a CDST-­informed perspective on the dynamic development of emotions in L2 writing. Our findings also identify key pathways through which writing enjoyment can be leveraged in AI-­mediated writing contexts, highlighting the roles of learners' AI competencies and their perceived learner–AI interactivity.

2.1   |   L2 Writing Enjoyment

Enjoyment has been conceptualized in L2 research as a cen­ tral positive achievement emotion (Pekrun  2006). Notably, while enjoyment has been extensively investigated in general language learning contexts, research examining enjoyment in specific language skill domains, such as writing, remains relatively limited (Li  2025; Zhang, Pan, and Fan  2025). L2 writing enjoyment, in particular, deserves focused attention as L2 writing is a cognitively demanding, emotionally charged and self-­regulated activity (Torrance and Galbraith  2006). Different from receptive skills, writing requires learners to generate ideas, monitor language form, organize discourse and evaluate their own output, often under conditions of linguistic uncertainty and performance pressure (Torrance and Galbraith  2006). In this sense, L2 writing enjoyment can therefore be understood as the positive emotional expe­ riences learners derive from participating in writing tasks and from navigating the challenges inherent in L2 writing (Zhu et al. 2022).

2.2   |   AI Feedback Literacy in L2 Writing

In L2 writing research, the effectiveness of feedback depends not only on the feedback provided but also on learners' ability to interpret and use it productively (Shen et al. 2023; Sutton 2012). To capture this learner-­related dimension, Sutton (2012) intro­ duced the concept of feedback literacy, referring to learners' ability to read, interpret and make use of feedback in academic contexts. Building on this initial conceptualization, Carless and Boud (2018) further elaborated the construct and proposed a widely adopted framework in which feedback literacy com­ prises several interrelated capabilities, including appreciating feedback, making judgements about work quality, managing affective responses and taking action to improve subsequent performance. Learners with higher levels of feedback literacy are generally better able to engage actively with feedback and translate it into meaningful revisions, which can contribute to improvements in L2 writing (Shi and Aryadoust 2024).

However, the increasing integration of AI in L2 writing has in­ troduced new forms of feedback that can differ from traditional feedback practices (Crosthwaite and Sun  2025). AI-­mediated writing tools, such as automated writing evaluation systems and generative AI assistants, can provide immediate suggestions on language use and textual organization during the writing pro­ cess (Yang et al. 2024). Such feedback has the potential to sup­ port writing performance, language development and revision practices (e.g., Guo et al. 2025; Tran 2025), and it may also en­ hance learners' affective experiences, including writing enjoy­ ment, peace of mind and reduced anxiety (e.g., Yu et al. 2025; Mohammed and Khalid 2025). Notably, however, these benefits are not always guaranteed. Their realization also depends on learners' ability to interpret, evaluate and selectively apply the feedback they receive, since AI-­generated suggestions can some­ times focus on surface-­level corrections or require careful judge­ ment (Tran  2025; Wang, Derakhshan, and Ghiasvand  2025; Wang, et al. 2026). Empirical studies (e.g., Yan and Zhang 2024) also confirm that learners vary in how they engage with AI feed­ back, with some adopting suggestions automatically, while oth­ ers critically assessing and incorporating them strategically into their revisions. These findings indicate that, as with traditional feedback, the effectiveness of AI-­generated feedback also hinges on learners' feedback literacy (Jin et al. 2025).

In response to this emerging need, recent studies have begun to look directly upon AI feedback literacy in higher education con­ texts. Liu and Deris (2025) were among the first to conceptualize AI feedback literacy (AIFL), introducing the construct and devel­ oping a psychometric scale to measure learners' ability to engage with AI-­generated feedback. Their findings indicated that AIFL significantly predicts students' uptake of AI feedback, both di­ rectly and indirectly through motivational factors. Building on this conceptual foundation, Zhan and Yan (2025) conducted an empirical study of how students engage with ChatGPT feedback, showing that engagement involves cognitive, metacognitive, af­ fective and behavioural processes, although it may sometimes remain superficial. Despite these emerging insights, as AI feedback literacy is still at its nascent stage, it is therefore unsurprising that research to date has primarily focused on conceptual development and its influence on learners' revision behaviours. Far less is known about how AI feedback literacy may shape learners' emotional experiences in AI-­mediated writing environments (Crosthwaite and Sun  2025; Liu and Fan  2024). In particular, it remains unclear whether and how learners' AI feedback literacy influ­ ences the development of positive emotions, such as writing enjoyment, over time. Addressing this gap, the present study ex­ amines the role of AI feedback literacy in predicting the devel­ opmental trajectory of L2 writing enjoyment in an AI-­mediated writing context.

2.3   |   Perceived Learner–AI Interactivity

In AI-­supported learning environments, interaction is no lon­ ger limited to exchanges between learners and teachers or peers but increasingly involves interactions between learners and AI systems (Guo and Li 2025; Memarian and Doleck 2024). In this context, the concept of perceived learner–AI interactivity has at­ tracted growing scholarly attention. Perceived learner–AI inter­ activity refers to learners' subjective perception of the extent to which AI systems respond to their inputs, provide personalized support and enable active engagement in the learning process (Liu, Fan, and Xia 2025; Liu, Lu, and Yan 2025; Ren et al. 2025; Shi et al. 2026; Wang, Cheung, et al. 2025). Similar to interac­ tion in traditional educational settings, learner–AI interactivity emphasizes not only the existence of technological interaction but also learners' perceptions of responsiveness, controllability and meaningful engagement during their interactions with AI systems (Guo and Li 2025).

To operationalize this construct, Wang et  al.  (2025) devel­ oped and validated the Perceived Interactivity of Learner–AI Interaction Scale, which conceptualizes perceived interactiv­ ity across four dimensions: responsiveness, personalization, learner control and learning engagement. Responsiveness refers to the timeliness and appropriateness of AI responses, personal­ ization concerns the extent to which AI systems adapt to learn­ ers' needs, learner control reflects learners' perceived ability to regulate the pace and direction of interaction, and learning engagement captures the degree of attention and involvement stimulated by AI tools (Barut Tugtekin and Dursun 2022; Wang, Pang, et al. 2024). Subsequent studies have adopted this multi­ dimensional perspective and examined factors associated with perceived AI interactivity. For example, Ren et al. (2025) found that digital competence positively predicts learners' perceived interactivity through self-­efficacy mechanisms, whereas digital stress may weaken this relationship. Empirical research further suggests that perceived learner–AI interactivity is associated with a range of learning behaviours and experiences. Taken together, these studies suggest that perceived learner–AI interactivity plays an important role in shaping learners' en­ gagement and learning experiences in AI-­supported environ­ ments. However, existing research has primarily focused on its behavioural and performance outcomes (Guo and Li 2025), with limited attention to learners' emotional development. It remains unclear how perceived learner–AI interactivity contributes to the development of writing-­related emotions, such as writing enjoyment, over time. To address this gap, the present study examines the role of perceived learner–AI interactivity in pre­ dicting the developmental trajectory of L2 writing enjoyment in AI-­mediated writing contexts.

2.4   |   Complex Dynamic Systems Theory

Traditional research on positive emotions in L2 writing has often treated emotions as relatively stable, trait-­like constructs, neglecting the ways in which learners' affective experiences fluctuate in response to changing linguistic, cognitive and so­ cial demands (Wang, Ganushchak, et al. 2024; Zhang, Pan, and Fan  2025). In response, CDST has increasingly been adopted as a metatheoretical framework for understanding such devel­ opments, including affective experiences, as non-­linear, emer­ gent and contextually situated processes (van Dijk et al. 2025). From this perspective, language learning is the result of con­ tinuous interactions among multiple interdependent subsys­ tems, which adapt, reorganize and co-­evolve over time (Piniel and Albert 2025). Consequently, learners' emotions are not fixed traits but emergent outcomes arising from the interplay be­ tween individual competencies and environmental conditions (Ranjbar et al. 2025).

3.2   |   Instruments

Background information was first collected, including partici­ pants' gender, age, major and their last four digits of their student ID. These variables were used to provide a general description of the sample.

L2 writing enjoyment was measured using an adapted L2 Writing Enjoyment Scale developed by Zhang, Pan, and Fan (2025). Participants rated their agreement with each item on a five-­point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). A sample item includes ‘I feel a sense of accom­ plishment when writing in English’. The scale was administered at three time points during the semester to capture changes in students' writing enjoyment over time. In the present study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency across the three waves, with Cronbach's α coefficients of 0.913, 0.892 and 0.932, respectively.

AI feedback literacy was measured using the AI Feedback Literacy (AIFL) Scale developed by Liu and Deris (2025). The instrument consists of 16 items and comprises two dimensions: attitude and practices, assessing learners' dispositions toward AI-­generated feedback and their ability to interpret, evaluate and apply such feedback in writing tasks. Participants responded to all items using a five-­point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The scale demonstrated satisfac­ tory reliability in the present study, with a Cronbach's α coeffi­ cient of 0.842.

Perceived learner–AI interactivity was assessed using the Perceived Interactivity of Learner–AI Interaction Scale devel­ oped by Wang et al. (2025). This instrument contains 17 items measuring learners' perceptions of AI responsiveness, person­ alization, learner control and engagement during interaction with AI tools. Responses were also recorded on a five-­point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). A sample item is ‘I can decide how to interact with AI according to my own needs’. The scale demonstrated good in­ ternal consistency in the present sample, with a Cronbach's α coefficient of 0.867.

The first wave of data collection took place in the second week of the semester. All participants completed questionnaires mea­ suring L2 writing enjoyment, AI feedback literacy and per­ ceived learner–AI interactivity. This initial survey served as the baseline measurement of the focal constructs before systematic

The second wave of data collection was conducted in the eighth week of the semester after several weeks of regular course in­ struction. The same questionnaires and procedures were used to examine short-­term changes in the target variables. Shortly after completing the survey, students carried out a structured AI-­assisted writing task as part of the course. They were asked to compose a short essay, use AI tools to obtain feedback and revise their drafts, and submit both the original and revised versions. Throughout the activity, the instructor provided pro­ cedural guidance and support. After completing the task, some students voluntarily shared their writing and their reflections in class, which further encouraged peer discussion and engage­ ment with AI-­mediated writing.

The third and final wave of data collection took place in the last week of the semester (Week 14). At this stage, participants once again completed the same set of questionnaires under identical conditions. This final survey captured students' end-­of-­semester levels of writing enjoyment, AI feedback literacy and perceived learner–AI interactivity, allowing the modelling of both intra-­ individual change and inter-­individual differences across the semester.

Although AI feedback literacy and perceived learner–AI in­ teractivity were measured across all three waves, only base­ line (Wave 1) scores were used as predictors in the conditional LGCM to examine their effects on the developmental trajectory of writing enjoyment. Throughout the data collection process, participation was voluntary, informed consent was obtained prior to the study, and all responses were securely stored.

3.4   |   Data Analysis

Data analysis was conducted in several stages. First, the data­ set was screened and prepared prior to the main analyses, and responses from the three survey waves were matched using the last four digits of participants' student ID numbers to en­ sure accurate longitudinal tracking across time. Missing data were handled using Full Information Maximum Likelihood

To address the research questions, LGCM within the struc­ tural equation modelling framework was conducted using Mplus 8 to examine the developmental trajectory of L2 writing enjoyment across the semester. In the unconditional LGCM, writing enjoyment measured at the three time points was used to estimate two latent growth factors: the intercept factor rep­ resenting the initial level of writing enjoyment and the slope factor representing the rate of change over time, with slope loadings fixed at 0, 1 and 2 to represent linear change across the three measurement occasions. Subsequently, a conditional LGCM was estimated in which baseline (Wave 1) scores of AI feedback literacy and perceived learner–AI interactivity were specified as time-­invariant predictors of both the inter­ cept and slope factors. Model fit was evaluated using multiple goodness-­of-­fit indices, including the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), with CFI and TLI values above 0.90 and RMSEA and SRMR values below 0.08 indicating ac­ ceptable model fit (Hu and Bentler 1999; Kline 2023).

5   |   Discussion

5.1   |   Developmental Trajectory of Writing Enjoyment in AI-­Mediated Writing (RQ1)

First, our study advances research on L2 writing enjoyment by examining its development in an AI-­mediated writing con­ text through a longitudinal design. Although previous studies have increasingly emphasized task-­specific emotions and a growing body of work has examined writing enjoyment, most

has either relied on cross-­sectional designs (Tabari et al. 2024) or been conducted in traditional writing classroom contexts (Derakhshan and Fathi  2025; Zhu et  al.  2022). By tracing students' emotional experiences across a semester, we pro­ vide new empirical evidence that writing enjoyment devel­ ops dynamically in AI-­supported writing environments. The observed pattern is consistent with previous research (either quantitative or qualitative) in traditional writing classrooms, where writing enjoyment tends to increase as learners gain experience and become more comfortable with writing ac­ tivities (e.g., Castro and Shelton-­Strong 2024; Elahi Shirvan et  al.  2021). Also noteworthy is that the magnitude of the growth rate in our study was relatively modest, which is not unexpected given the nature of achievement emotions in lan­ guage learning. Emotional experiences such as writing en­ joyment typically develop gradually as learners accumulate learning experiences and progressively evaluate their com­ petence and task value (Guo and Wang  2025). In addition, our study was conducted within the time frame of a single academic semester, which may limit the extent of observable emotional change. Because students already reported a mod­ erately positive level of writing enjoyment at the beginning of the course, the subsequent increase tended to be incremen­ tal rather than dramatic. From a complex dynamic systems perspective, such gradual development is consistent with the notion that learner emotions evolve through continuous in­ teractions between learners, tasks and the learning environ­ ment rather than through abrupt shifts (van Dijk et al. 2025). Also interestingly, our study revealed differences in the rate at which students' writing enjoyment developed, with some

students experiencing faster growth than others. This find­ ing has important pedagogical implications (see Section  6) and resonates with research showing substantial intra-­ and inter-­individual variability in learners' emotional trajecto­ ries, particularly in relation to L2 writing enjoyment (Piniel and Albert 2025). Our study adds new insights into previous studies by demonstrating that across both traditional and AI-­ mediated writing contexts, writing enjoyment is a dynamic rather than static construct, with learners potentially follow­ ing different developmental trajectories.

One possible explanation for this developmental trajectory is that students gradually became more familiar with the writ­ ing tasks as they engaged in the writing process, a mechanism widely discussed in emotion research on traditional writing classrooms (Hu and Zhang 2025; Wang and Zhang 2025). As learners gain more experience with planning, drafting, and revising their texts, they tend to develop greater confidence and a stronger sense of control and competence in writing, which can further enhance their writing enjoyment (Tabari et al. 2024; Zumbrunn et al. 2019). Building on this line of re­ search, our study extends existing explanations by introducing context-­specific factors in shaping the development of writing enjoyment. In AI-­mediated writing contexts, students must not only become familiar with writing tasks but also learn to interact with AI tools and interpret AI-­generated feedback. As learners gained experience using AI to generate ideas, revise drafts and evaluate their writing (Hwang et al. 2025), they likely became more confident in integrating AI support into their writing process (Mills et al. 2025; Wang 2025). 5.2   |   The Role of AI Feedback Literacy and Learner–AI Interactivity in L2 Writing Enjoyment (RQ2 and RQ3)

Second, our study highlights the important role of learners' com­ petencies and interactional perceptions in shaping their emo­ tional experiences in AI-­mediated writing environments (Shi et al. 2025). Specifically, our results show that both AI feedback literacy and perceived learner–AI interactivity positively pre­ dicted not only students' initial levels of writing enjoyment but also the rate at which their enjoyment developed over time. The findings extend previous research which has primarily exam­ ined the behavioural and performance outcomes of AI feedback literacy and learner–AI interactivity (Guo and Wang 2025; Kim et al. 2026; Rad et al. 2024), introducing new evidence that these factors are also closely related to learners' emotional develop­ ment in AI-­mediated writing contexts.

From the perspective of feedback literacy, this finding comple­ ments earlier research emphasizing the importance of learn­ ers' ability to interpret and utilize feedback effectively (Carless and Boud  2018). Learners with stronger AI feedback literacy are more likely to engage with AI-­generated feedback in a con­ structive and strategic manner (Liu and Deris 2025). Instead of perceiving AI feedback as confusing or overly technical, they may treat it as a useful learning resource that helps them iden­ tify weaknesses in their texts and refine their writing (Zhan and Yan 2025). Through such engagement, learners are more likely to make meaningful revisions and observe improvements in the quality of their writing (Shen et al. 2023). These improve­ ments may strengthen their sense of competence and achieve­ ment, which can foster more positive emotions toward writing (Jin et al. 2025). Similarly, from the perspective of learner–AI interaction, when students perceive AI systems as responsive, personalized, and supportive, they may feel more engaged and confident in the writing process (Guo and Li 2025). In such situa­ tions, AI tools may be experienced less as impersonal evaluation technologies and more as collaborative partners in learning (Li et al. 2025). This interpretation also aligns with earlier research showing that learners tend to report more positive learning ex­ periences, such as motivation and engagement, when AI tools are perceived as interactive and supportive companions rather than purely evaluative technologies (Mills et al. 2025).

5.3   |   Theoretical Contributions to Complex Dynamic Systems Theory

One ongoing discussion in the CDST literature concerns whether the framework can generate empirically testable hypotheses rather than remaining a broad metatheoretical perspective (Han et al. 2023; van Dijk et al. 2025). Our study contributes to this discussion by translating CDST's assumptions about change and system interaction into concrete, testable propositions regarding the development of writing enjoyment. By modelling individual trajectories of writing enjoyment and examining how learners' competencies and interactional perceptions predict both the initial level and rate of change in enjoyment, we illustrate how CDST-­inspired perspectives on dynamic development can be op­ erationalized through longitudinal research designs (Hiver and Al-­Hoorie 2016).

Additionally, our findings enrich CDST discussions of initial conditions and subsystem interconnectedness. CDST empha­ sizes that language development emerges from interactions among multiple subsystems and that early system conditions can shape subsequent developmental trajectories (Verspoor et al. 2021). Previous CDST research has largely focused on in­ teractions within linguistic subsystems, such as the dynamic relationship between complexity and accuracy in L2 production (Yu and Lowie  2020). Our study extends this perspective by examining a broader constellation of subsystems that includes learners' competencies (AI feedback literacy), interactional con­ ditions (perceived learner–AI interactivity) and emotional expe­ riences (writing enjoyment), suggesting that these factors jointly shape learners' emotional trajectories in AI-­mediated writing environments.

CDST research calls for longitudinal approaches that capture developmental trajectories and indi­ vidual variability rather than relying solely on static snapshots of learner variables (van Dijk et al. 2025). 6   |   Implications, Limitations and Future Directions

Our findings can offer several pedagogical implications for AI-­ mediated L2 writing instruction. First, the gradual increase in students' writing enjoyment suggests that AI-­mediated writing environments can support the development of positive emo­ tional experiences when learners are given sufficient time to be­ come familiar with both writing tasks and AI-­assisted writing practices. For teachers, this highlights the importance of pro­ viding structured guidance at the early stages of AI integration, such as demonstrating how AI tools can be used for idea gen­ eration, revision and feedback interpretation (Wang 2025). The results further indicate that AI feedback literacy plays a crucial role in shaping learners' emotional engagement with writing. Therefore, rather than assuming that students naturally know how to use AI-­generated feedback effectively, instructors should explicitly scaffold learners' abilities to interpret, evaluate and strategically apply AI feedback during the writing process (Liu and Deris 2025). In addition, the significant role of perceived learner–AI interactivity suggests that students' emotional ex­ periences are influenced not only by their individual compe­ tencies but also by how they perceive their interaction with AI systems. For course designers and teachers, this implies that AI-­ supported writing tasks should encourage iterative interaction with AI tools, reflective activities and opportunities for learners to critically engage with AI-­generated suggestions. Such prac­ tices may help learners view AI not merely as an automated cor­ rection tool but as an interactive partner in the writing process, thereby fostering more sustained writing enjoyment over time (Yu et al. 2025).

Despite these implications, several limitations should be ac­ knowledged. First, the AI-­mediated writing activities were im­ plemented within structured classroom interventions, including AI training and guided writing tasks. While this design ensured that students developed a shared understanding of AI-­supported writing practices, it may differ from informal digital learning contexts in which learners interact with AI tools autonomously outside the classroom (Liu et al. 2024). Future research could therefore examine how writing enjoyment develops when learn­ ers engage with AI tools in more self-­directed environments beyond formal instruction. This is also a direction we plan to explore in the next phase of our research.

Second, although the longitudinal design and LGCM analysis captured semester-­level developmental trajectories, this ap­ proach cannot fully reveal how moment-­to-­moment interactions with AI tools shape emotional fluctuations during specific writ­ ing activities. Future research could adopt designs with finer temporal resolution to better capture the short-­term dynamics of emotional experiences in AI-­mediated writing.

Finally, because the participants were recruited from a sin­ gle grade level at one university, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Future research could include more diverse samples across multiple institutions and educational settings to further examine the broader applicability of the findings.
