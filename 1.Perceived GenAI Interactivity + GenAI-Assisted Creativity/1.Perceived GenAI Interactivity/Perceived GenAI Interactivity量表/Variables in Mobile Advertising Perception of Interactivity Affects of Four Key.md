There are indications that interactivity could benefit the effectiveness of mobile advertisements. But there are few guidelines on how to design interactive mobile advertisements. This study investigated the influences of various design features of mobile advertisements on perceived interactivity and the relationship between perceived interactivity and attitude toward mobile advertisements. An experiment consisting of 2 sessions was conducted to test 5 proposed hypotheses with 40 participants. Results showed that the number of user control options and the customizability of advertisements have significant influences on users’ perception of interactivity. The more options a message advertisement provides, the more interactive the participants perceive the advertisement; a customizable game advertisement is perceived as more interactive than an uncustomizable game advertisement. But no significant effects of presenting instructional information of mobile Web advertisements and including humorous elements were found. Finally, perceived interactivity was found a strong predictor of attitude toward mobile advertisement.

1. INTRODUCTION

Mobile advertisements experienced a tremendous growth in the past decade, driven by the rapid development of adoption of mobile devices, services and networks. In China, the number of cell phone subscribers reached 394 million at the end of 2005. On average, every 100 Chinese own 30.3 cell phones (Ministry of Information Industry of the People’s Republic of China, 2006). The convergence of Internet and mobile services has already changed the way people interact and the way the business is done (International Telecommunication Union, 2002; Lunder, Taylor, Kluge, Larsen, & Holmes, 2000) and people become increasingly interested in using cell phones for data services in addition to voice services. Despite the great amount of attention given to the buzz of interactive mobile campaigns and services and the belief of many advertisers that the interactivity is crucial to the success of mobile advertising, however, to date scant scholarly effort has been endeavored to specify what influence users’ perception of interactivity of mobile advertisements and to provide validated guidelines for designing for mobile interactivity. Researchers in marketing and communications argued that interactivity should not be measured by analyzing the process or counting the features, but by observing how users perceive the interactivity during the communication (Lee, 2005; McMillan & Hwang, 2002; Wu, 1999).

Compared with desktop computers, mobile devices have the following characteristics (Barnes, 2002; Kannan et al., 2001):

• Ubiquitous availability: Users carry handsets like cell phones everyday and everywhere and handsets are almost always “on.” • Personal usage: Handheld devices often carry their user identity and are often used in the personal context. • Context-aware ability: Mobile application can utilize contextual information to provide customized information and services at the point of need. • Easy to respond: It is difficult to input long texts on a cell phone. However, short and fast responses are greatly facilitated. For example, it is often easier to make use of an M-coupon sent to one’s cell phone than to a print-to-use coupon sent to one’s e-mail box.

The importance of interactivity will increase in mobile advertising since it is one of the most salient characteristics of mobile communications (Barnes, 2002; Kannan et al., ; Retti & Brum, 2001). The results of a two-way short message service (SMS) campaign for a youth brand at Kingston University in the United Kingdom showed that when the same invitation to a prize draw was announced through different media, the response rate of the SMS group was 10 times higher than that of the e-mail group, and the response speed of the SMS group was dramatically faster than that of the Web group (Stone, Briggs, & Smith, 2002). It was implied that not only are interactive communications on mobile phones possible, but users are more likely to take part and get involved. Lee (2005) also found that tIt he perceptions of interactivity have a positive effect on customer trust in mobile commerce environment. Furthermore, synergies between the mobile medium and traditional mass-communications environments (e.g., TV) can be leveraged to lend interactivity to those previously non-interactive media (Davis & Yung, 2005).

However, the poor screen resolution, the limited computing capacity, the slow network speed, and the awkward input restricts the “media bandwidth” (Hoffman & Novak, 1995) for information exchange, and consequently influences the form and quality of interactions. Thus, it is not appropriate to apply the interactivity concept developed for Internet advertising to mobile advertising. To design interactive mobile advertisements, both the representation design and the distributing approach of mobile advertisements should be considered carefully.

There have been a number of studies discussing mobile interactivity; however, it is still unclear what interactivity is for mobile applications. In different studies

on mobile applications the word “interactive” often refers to different characteristics and behaviors of the mobile applications. Broadbent and Marti (1997) referred the term interactivity to whether the information is pushed to or pulled by the user. Brown and Jones (2001) defined interactive as the situation where the user directly issues a request to retrieve relevant documents, and proactive as the situation where documents are presented to the user automatically. Based on a survey of context-aware applications, Chen and Kotz (2000); defined active context-awareness as applications that change their content autonomously according to discovered contexts; on the contrast, passive context-aware applications merely present the updated context to the user and let the user specify how the application should change. Based on Chen and Kotz’s model, Barkhuus and Dey (2003) added an extra level of interactivity as personalization which refers to customizing and tailoring. These definitions of levels of mobile interactivity stem from classifications of interactions rather than a clear understanding of the interactivity construct. Second, these studies focus on technological features but not users’ real interactions with the system. Such an approach cannot tell whether the user can perceive the utility of these features in the way designers expect and whether such features meet user expectations for interactive mobile systems. Regarding the effects of interactive applications on users, users’ perception of interactivity is indispensible.

Perceived interactivity was found a more distinguishable variable than structural interactivity in predicting the impact on user experience with interactive media. It was regarded as a crucial element of successful online marketing (Deighton, 1996; Hoffman & Novak, 1995). Wu (1999) measured participants’ attitudes toward particular Web sites and their perceived interactivity of those sites and found a strong correlation between these two variables. Cho and Leckenby (1999) found positive relations between intention to interact with the advertisement and attitudes toward the advertisement, attitudes toward the brand, and purchase intention. Yoo and Stout (2001) also achieved similar results in their study. McMillan and Hwang’s (2002) study demonstrated that interactivity and involvement with the subject of a site were two predictors of positive attitude towards the Web site, and perceived interactivity accounts for more of the variance in attitude than does involvement. In this study we adopt the perceptual perspective of interactivity.

2.2. Dimensions of Interactivity

Interactivity is generally believed to be a multidimensional construct (Ha & James, 1998; P. Liu & Shrum, 2002; Steuer, 1992; Wu, 1999), but there are no general agreements regarding the nature and the content of the dimensions. Based on constructs identified in previous Internet interactivity studies and analysis of the characteristics of mobile communication, we constructed a model of interactivity for mobile advertisements (Gao, Rau, & Salvendy, 2009), comprising six components, as stated below:

User control. This construct refers to the extent to which an individual feels in control of his/her interaction experience. Such control can be obtained by letting users choose the content, timing and sequence of a communication (Dholakia, Zhao, Dholakia, & Fortin, 2000). Two constructs identified by Steuer (1992), range and mapping, actually describe two aspects of control. The former refers to the number of options the environment provides the user to modify the task flow and the environment, and the latter refers to the extent to which the controls and manipulations in a mediated environment are similar to controls and manipulations in the real word. User control is also associated with minimizing effort in the achievement of a task and ease of adding information (Heeter, 1989).

Two-way communication. An interactive communication should allow reciprocal communication and the messages in a sequence should relate to each other (Rafaeli, 1988; Rafaeli & Sudweeks, 1997). In other words, the environment should be capable for facilitating the user to provide feedback based on received messages cit_bf(Day, 1998; Ha & James, 1998)cit_af ref_bf(Day, 1998 ref_num269/ Ha, 1998 #243)ref_af. Mobile communication on handheld devices, especially cell phones, is expected to provide abundant possibilities, because cell phones are originally designed for two-way communication and voice service is in nature two-way communication.

Synchronicity. The importance of synchronicity in the conceptualization of interactivity has been long recognized (Dholakia et al., 2000; Ha & James, 1998; P. Liu, 2003; McMillan & Hwang, 2002). It refers to the speed at which the message can be delivered and at which persons can process messages. The faster the response is, the less inhibited the user is, and the more interactive he or she perceives. The time duration between sending a message and receiving a response is often shortened in mobile communication. On one hand, voice and instant message services provide an opportunity for instant response. On the other hand, the fact that the user always carries the device and the device is nearly always on makes synchronized conversation always possible, regardless where and when the conversation takes place.

Connectedness. This construct is broadened in the context of mobile communication on the base of construct proposed by Ha and James (1998), referring to the feeling of being linked to more resources related to the company and the product outside the specific environment (e.g., a real-world campaign, technical support information). There are numerous possibilities in mobile communication for providing diverse connections between the customer and the company, including links to more related topics, alerts to ongoing events, and opt-in newsletters for community members.

Playfulness. Playfulness was empirically proved to be a salient predicator for Web site popularity (Chen & Yen, 2004; Eighmey, 1993). Unlike the previous

constructs emphasizing interaction between persons, the playfulness dimension of interactivity emphasizes communication within oneself, since playful devices can provide players an enjoyable inner talk with oneself (Ha & James, 1998). Handheld devices are often used for entertaining needs, and people often intentionally seek playfulness on their handheld devices. In a review of interviews focusing on mobile marketing, half of the experts noted that providing entertaining content encourages participation and help attracting people (Scharl, Dickinger, & Murphy, 2005).

Interpersonal communication. This construct refers to the degree to which a media system resembles and facilitates interpersonal communication. In interactivity literature, interpersonal communication has long been defined as the ideal type or the standard of interactive communication (DeFleur & Ball-Rokeach, 1989; Durlak, 1987; Heeter, 1989; Williams, Rice, & Rogers, 1988). The more the technologically mediated communication resembles interpersonal communication, the more interactive the communication is (Heeter, 1989). The user perception of interactivity will be improved if the company can utilize the device features to build certain kinds of communication taking on some characteristics of interpersonal communication.

3. RESEARCH FRAMEWORK

Internet research has found that some structural features influence Web users’ perception of interactivity significantly. Such features may differ across various types of mobile advertisements. For example, push-type message advertisements can be of less user control, but providing a reply option gives customers a convenient channel to respond; mobile Web advertisements are less intrusive compared with push-advertisements, but they might be neglected or assumed as decorative images. To design interactive mobile advertisements properly and effectively, the effects of possibly “interactive” features should be tested empirically against real users. Reciprocity and two-way communication are of great importance for interactivity (Ghose & Dou, 1998; P. Liu & Shrum, 2002; Steuer, 1992). People are getting used to customizable Web contents on the Internet. H5: Perceived interactivity has a positive influence on the attitude toward the

mobile advertisement.

Studies on Internet advertising interactivity yield conflicting findings regarding the influence of interactivity on user experience (Bezjian-Avery, 1998; Cho & Leckenby, 1999). By distinguishing structural interactivity and perceived interactivity (P. Liu & Shrum, 2002), it is found that there is a strong correlation between perceived interactivity and advertising effectiveness in terms of attitude toward brand, attitude toward advertisements, and purchase intention (Cho & Leckenby, 1999; McMillian & Hwang, 2002; Yoo & Stout, 2001). Previous study also suggested that higher interactivity help the customer experience “flow” during the interaction (Hoffman & Novak, 1995), and the consequence of “flow” experience is positive subjective experience. The similar effect is expected to take place for mobile advertisement. Therefore high perceived interactivity of a mobile advertisement is hypothesized to engender affective attitude toward the advertisement.

4.3. Independent Variables

Presence of clicking instructions of mobile Web banners, with two levels as clicking instruction presence and absence, was manipulated by either presenting pictorial signs/textual statements suggesting the click behavior or not. Eight mobile Web banners were designed, with four presenting control instructions, and four others without such information, as shown in Figure 2. In the first case, the advertisements contained a pictorial sign (a hand over the advertisement, similar to a hovering cursor on a hyperlink displayed on PC) or a textual statement (“click here to know more”) suggesting the participant to click on the advertisement to get more information.

Number of user options of message advertisements was manipulated by including different number of user control options in the message. Four groups of message advertisements were designed, providing no options to three options. Each group included four message advertisements. In the no options group, only commercial information about the product and/or the ongoing event was provided. In the one option group, a reply-to-the-message option was provided. In the two options version, participants were offered a Web site link besides the reply option. They could visit the Web site to get more information about the product and the event. In the three options version, participants were further enabled to call back besides reply and visit a Web site. Because the PDAs used in the experiment were not capable for voice communication, the call back function was only demoed. A bubble saying “sorry the function is not available in the demo ad” would appear if the participant selected to call back (see Table 4).

4.5. Apparatus

Two ASUS personal digital assistants (A620C) with built-in PocketPC 2002 as the operation system were used. Each was equipped with a 3.5-in. TFT-LCD with a resolution of 240 × 320 pixels. All mobile advertisements and games were developed with Macromedia Flash. A third-party flash player—FlashAssist v1.3—was installed to run the flash file independently and to enable full screen display of the flash.

Two entertaining Web sites were constructed for the first session. The content of the two Web sites was identical, but one Web site included banner advertisements with instructions, and another Web site included banner advertisements without instructions. Besides experimenting Web ads, the same four filler ads appeared on both sites. Both Web sites were built with Macromedia Flash and were displayed in full screen. Forty participants were randomly assigned to the two Web sites with exactly 20 in each group. The PDA program automatically traced and timed (to the nearest one-thousandth second) the participants’ actions.

Two slot machine games and two action games were developed to test Hypotheses 3 and 4. The details were previously described.

Two independent sessions were conducted to test the five hypotheses. The first session was to test Hypotheses 1 and 2. Forty participants were randomly assigned to the two Web sites with exactly 20 in each group. Because there were more male participants than female, special attention was paid so as to ensure that equal number of male and female participants were included in each group. They were asked to follow the on-screen instruction to finish eight evaluation tasks. For each task the participant was asked to browse a single category of the Web site (e.g., “entertainment news”) for 5 min and put down the title or keywords of his or her favorite content on a response form. The system would go to the page showing the introduction to the next task automatically after 5 min. The order of tasks was randomized.

When a participant was browsing the site, six message advertisements appeared on the PDA screen at a predetermined time interval, including four experimenting advertisements and two filler advertisements. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four treatments of experimenting message advertisements (with no options, with one option, with two options, and with three options) with exactly 10 in each group. They were informed before the experiment that they could respond or not respond to the advertisements as they will.

Upon the completion of all tasks, each participant was asked to fill out a demography questionnaire followed by a questionnaire measuring his or her attitude toward mobile advertisements in general and involvement with the Web site. After that, the participant completed perceived interactivity measures and attitude measures for each banner advertisement and each message advertisement.

Session 2 was to test Hypotheses 3 and 4. Each participant was asked to play a slot machine game and an action game, each for four rounds. They were randomly assigned to one of the two treatment conditions of the action game— customizable and uncustomizable—and were randomly assigned to one of the two treatment conditions of the slot machine game, with humors and without humors in the game. The only constrain of the assignment was that there should be 20 participants in each treatment condition for each game and the number of male and female participants should be equal across different groups. Finally, participants completed the measures of playfulness, perceived interactivity, and attitude toward each game advertisement.

4.7. Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic information of participants, perceived interactivity, attitude toward the advertisement, attitude toward mobile advertisements in general, and user involvement with the Web site. Then the demographic information, attitude toward mobile advertisements in general,

and user involvement with the Web site were compared across groups, to identify if there were other factors that may cause the variance in dependent variables.

Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) tests were run to test the first four hypotheses. Prior to each test, the data adequacy for the test was examined. The normality and the homogeneity of variance were first checked. Then the linear relationship between two mediators, attitude toward mobile advertisements in general and involvement with Web sites, and perceived interactivity were examined; if a linear relationship did exist, then the homogeneity of regression coefficients was tested. If all requirements were satisfied, ANCOVA tests were run with attitude toward mobile advertisements in general and involvement with Web sites as two covariates. In addition, click-through rate for banner advertisements and playfulness for the slot machine game advertisement were also compared. Wherever assumptions of ANCOVA were violated but assumptions for analysis of variance (ANOVA) were kept, ANOVA tests would be run instead. Nonparametric tests were carried out when assumptions of ANOVA were violated.

To test Hypothesis 5, the Pearson correlation coefficients between perceived interactivity and attitude toward the advertisement were computed and the significance of the coefficient was examined. Linear regression was also conducted to determine to what extent perceived interactivity can predict attitude toward the advertisement.

5.3. Testing Hypothesis 3

It was hypothesized that mobile advertisements that include humorous dialogues and graphics in the gaming process would be perceived as more interactive than mobile game advertisements which do not include such elements. The ANOVA test result of perceived interactivity shown in Table 6 indicates that no significant differences in perceived interactivity were found between mobile game advertisements including humorous elements and those including no such elements. Hypothesis 3 was not supported.

5.4. Testing Hypothesis 4

It was hypothesized that a customizable mobile game advertisement will be perceived as more interactive than an uncustomizable mobile game advertisement. The correlation between the dependent variable (perceived interactivity of game ads) and the attitude toward mobile advertisements in general was found significant (r = .43, p = .005). Therefore attitude toward mobile advertisements in general was taken as a covariate in the test. The result of the ANCOVA test shown in Table 6 revealed that whether a mobile game is customizable or not has a significant influence on perceived interactivity, F(1, 37) = 4.6, p = .04. Users perceive a customizable mobile game advertisement (M = 4.6, SD = 0.04, LSM = 5.03) as more interactive than an uncustomizable mobile game advertisement (M = 4.48, SD = 0.78, LSM = 4.53) 11.04% increase of perceived interactivity was found. Hypothesis 4 was supported.

5.5. Testing Hypothesis 5

To assess the influence of perceived interactivity on attitude toward the advertisement, a Pearson correlation test was conducted. A significant positive correlation (r = .65, p < .001) between perceived interactivity of a mobile advertisement and the attitude toward the advertisement was found. Further, to explore whether perceived interactivity can predict attitude toward the advertisement, a linear regression was carried out, with attitude toward the advertisement as the dependent variable and perceived interactivity as the independent variable. The result showed that perceived interactivity is a strong predicator of attitude toward advertisement since it explains much of the variance in attitude toward the advertisement (R2 = 0.42), F(1, 38) = 312.5, p < .001.

No significant differences were found between two groups, either in click behavior or in perception of interactivity (shown in Figure 5). Several plausible reasons may explain this phenomenon. A very likely reason may be the transfer of the banner-blindness effect from the internet. Users’ prior experiences and knowledge gained from the Internet transfer to their mobile browsing behavior, as well as their tendency of ignoring banner advertisements and maybe dislike toward these attention grabbing tools. All participants in the experiment were extremely experienced Internet users (5.2 hr per day on the Internet), thus such a transfer was very likely to take place. Second, the device used in the experiment was a PDA that was operated with stylus. To click-through a banner, the user can move the stylus fast to the point and then click with a continuous motion. But for most mobile phones that can only be operated by pressing keys, the user need to press the direction key several times to move the focus and then switch to the confirm key to click. This discrepancy in the interaction style leads to different expectation of possible actions.

Hypothesis 2 was supported by the significant differences in perceived interactivity. Although the increase in the number of response options of mobile advertisements does not lead to increase in behavioral responses, users do perceive advertisements providing interaction opportunities more interactive than advertisements without such opportunities, as shown in Figure 5. Although in all cases in the experiment participants had no control over the initiation of mobile advertisements (i.e., advertisements were pushed to participants), with more options

provided by the unsolicited message advertisements participants had more choices to change their following advertisement viewing experiences. They perceived such advertisements as more interactive even when they actually did not perform more interactions with such advertisements. The result is consistent with previous internet interactivity literature where user control is believed to be a core component of interactivities (Bezjian-Avery, Calder, & Lacobucci, 1998; Steuer, 1992; Williams et al., 1988).

Though playfulness was found an important underlying construct of perceived interactivity and humorous graphics/dialogues were considered contributing to playfulness, our manipulation of humorous graphics/dialogues did not lead to differences in perceived interactivity. To explore the reason, the playfulness of the two mobile games was checked. It was found that including humorous elements or not did not impact the perceived playfulness of the game (Wilcoxon two sample test: Z = –0.31, p = .76). A possible reason may be that the game was designed to balance the player’s account after 1 min and then award certain bonus according to the amount of deposit. This design seems motivating participants to expedite their gaming process in order to acquire as much as possible in the given time. Therefore many of them skipped the congratulation screen containing the humorous elements by fast clicking away. The correlation relationship was tested, and it was found playfulness was positively correlated with perceived interactivity (r = .42, p = .007). This indicates that increasing playfulness of the game does promote users’ perception of the interactivity of mobile game advertisements, but attention should be paid to the design for playfulness. Though it is believed humorous statements or animations can evoke playful feeling (Eighmey, 1993), the design of game rules should ensure that enjoying such entertainments would not impede players pursuing the game goal.

The hypothesis that customizable games were perceived as more interactive was supported. Although in either case (customizable or uncustomizable) no real human–human interaction occurs, a customizable game enables more humandevice interactions and lends the user certain power to change the game environment to suite to his or her preference to a certain extent. This empowerment, perceived as an interactive feature, is expected to help increase users engagement and immersion with the game and therefore better learning of the advertising information contained in mobile game advertisements.

7. CONCLUSION

Although advertisers are increasing their investment in mobile advertising, users may become more and more sensitive to advertisements clutter and spam on their personal cell phones. As happened in the Internet advertising era, the connection between the customer and possible beneficial offers are sometimes obstructed rather than facilitated by Internet advertisements because of the habituated banner blindness. The new channel offers brilliant possibilities to provide users tailored information at a proper time and a proper place, but the opportunities must be used with great attention to avoid overloading or irritating

the user with overwhelming irrelevant information jammed on his or her cell phone; interactions with mobile advertisements should be designed with cautious to avoid grabbing the user’s attention rudely or interrupting the user at anytime anywhere. One feasible approach to achieve this is to design interactive mobile advertisements with which users are empowered to select what he or she would like to experience. This is beneficial for both companies and users. For companies, customers are voluntarily connected to the campaign and the company, which will lead to more responses and more affective attitude toward the brand and the company; for users, they can have more control over their own experiences and can be better connected to beneficial offers which are personal relevant by proper designed advertisements. However, how to develop such interactive mobile ads? Empirical studies of various design features are needed.

The goal of this study is to investigate the influence of various design features of mobile advertisements on users’ perception of interactivity. The presence of instructional information was manipulated for mobile Web advertisements, the number of user control options was manipulated for message advertisements, and the inclusion of humorous elements and the customizability were manipulated for mobile game advertisements. In addition, it was expected that high levels of perceived interactivity would lead to positive attitude toward the advertisement.

Based on the findings of the current study, two design guidelines for interactive mobile advertisement design are proposed.

1. Provide abundant response options in message advertisements: On one hand, the customer feels the advertisement more controllable with more options; on the other hand, these options often reveal several distinct channels to communicate with the company, which would increase the “touching area” between the customer and the company and thus more opportunities for long-term contacts. Besides the response options used in the experiment, more interaction opportunities can be initiated through contextual offerings of advertisements and synergies between mobile campaigns and other media marketing. 2. Make mobile game advertisements customizable: Mobile game advertisements are recognized as less obtrusive and highly impressive because of the immersive and interactive gaming environments and are gaining their market share rapidly. The result of the current study shows that mobile users should be able to change the game settings according to their needs. Some customizations can be automated by using information of users’ identity, location, and physical environment that can be specified by mobile networks. Moreover, making embedded product information customizable (e.g., select a product as the top award of the game) may help the user actively process the advertising information and increase brand awareness effectively.3. Include humorous devices in advertisement in a proper way: There is a clear relationship between perceived playfulness and interactivity. However, cautious should be exercised when the playfulness of a game is to be increased. Although certain humorous devices are considered being able to evoke

playful feelings, simply inserting such devices into the advertisement might not result in the expected effect. It is necessary to allow users to enjoy such entertainments without hurting their performance of the current task they are doing.

Finally, it is found that there is a significant correlation between perceived interactivity and attitude toward the advertisement. Further regression analysis showed that perceived interactivity can explain 42% of the variance in attitude toward the advertisement. This finding reinforces the essentiality of interactivity in mobile advertisements design.

A limitation of the study lies in the sampling. Graduate students in Tsinghua University have been recruited for testing the hypotheses empirically. This nonprobabilistic sampling method and the small sample size may limit the generalizability of the result. Also the experimental condition might make participants more involved than usual. Another limitation of the study is that the gender was not balanced in participants. The number of male participants (n = 28) was much larger than that of female participants (n = 12). Traditional media research suggests that male and female participants differ in belief, attitude, and behavior media effect (Prakash, 1992); in Internet advertising research, it is found that male respondents exhibit more positive beliefs about Web advertising, and they are more likely to purchase from the Web than female respondents (Wolin & Korgaonkar, 2003). To reduce the possible biases caused to experiment results, equally number of male and female participants were included in different experimental groups. However, in the future study, equal number of female and male participants should be recruited to eliminate the biases. It is also interesting to investigate the gender differences in the perception of mobile interactivity.

This study is an attempt to extend the scope of conventional perspectives on the concept of interactivity to mobile advertising. Several representation features of mobile advertisements were examined regarding their influences on perceived interactivity. However, the range of “ubiquitous interactivity” of mobile advertisements goes beyond design of representations. Further research may address further issues such as proper utilization of pull–push advertising strategy, the interaction between interactivity and context-awareness, and synergy between various media to build a highly interactive sphere. Also, further research may refine the current understanding of what interactivity is in the mobile communications environment and how it influences users’ experiences. A possible approach is to analyze the type of interactions enabled in this environment and adjust or develop principles for specific contexts.
