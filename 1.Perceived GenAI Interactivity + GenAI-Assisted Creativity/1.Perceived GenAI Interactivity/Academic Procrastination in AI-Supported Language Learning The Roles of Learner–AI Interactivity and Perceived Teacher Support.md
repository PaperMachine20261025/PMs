Academic Procrastination in AI-­Supported Language Learning: The Roles of Learner–AI Interactivity and Perceived Teacher Support

ABSTRACT As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly integrated into language learning, AI–supported environments may shape learners' academic engagement and learning behaviours. Among these behaviours, academic procrastination is particularly im­ portant because it reflects learners' tendency to delay academic tasks in technology–mediated contexts. However, little is known about how perceived interactivity in learner–AI interaction and perceived teacher support jointly influence academic procrasti­ nation. Guided by the Technology Acceptance Model, this study examined the relationships among perceived interactivity, per­ ceived teacher support and academic procrastination through Pearson correlation analysis and structural equation modelling. The findings indicated that perceived interactivity of learner–AI interaction was a significant negative predictor of academic pro­ crastination and a significant positive predictor of perceived teacher support. Meanwhile, perceived teacher support significantly and negatively predicted academic procrastination. Furthermore, perceived teacher support partially mediated the relationship between perceived interactivity of learner–AI interaction and academic procrastination. This study deepens the understanding of the relationships among learners' subjective perceptions, external support and academic procrastination and provides insights for optimising AI-­supported language learning experiences and learning practices.

Perceived teacher support refers to students' subjective percep­ tion or evaluation of the care, help and support provided by teachers (Wong et al. 2018). In the process of L2 learning, teach­ ers are not only transmitters of knowledge but also important providers of learning support, capable of offering academic, in­ strumental and emotional support to learners (Liu and Li 2023), alleviating their negative emotions (Liu, Shen, et  al.  2025) to reduce emotional barriers in digital environments (Huang and Zhu 2025), thereby enhancing learners' emotional intelligence, willingness to communicate (Li and Pan 2025) and learning mo­ tivation (Derakhshan et al. 2025). Previous research has indicated that aca­ demic procrastination is not merely a problem of poor time management, but also reflects learners' fear of failure as well as difficulties in self–regulation, motivation maintenance and emotion management (Zhang et al. 2025). This phenomenon has been shown to be closely associated with decreased aca­ demic performance (Su et al. 2025), increased stress and neg­ ative emotions (Huang and Zhu 2025; Zhang et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2026), thereby gradually becoming an important topic in educational research. Existing research has largely focused on general learning contexts, with limited attention to AI–medi­ ated L2 learning environments. Therefore, this study draws on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) to examine how learners' perceived interactivity with AI tools influences aca­ demic procrastination and whether perceived teacher support mediates this relationship.

Although existing studies have recognised the positive role of AI in L2 learning and have separately discussed the importance of perceived teacher support and academic procrastination, the intrinsic relationships among these three constructs remain largely unexplored. Specifically, in AI–supported EFL contexts, current research has yet to fully explain how learners' percep­ tions of interacting with AI are associated with students' ten­ dency to delay academic tasks, nor is it clear whether perceived teacher support plays a mediating role in this process. By investigating this mediating mechanism, this study may clarify how aca­ demic procrastination develops in AI–supported EFL learning and offer practical guidance for teacher support in reducing stu­ dents' procrastination.

2   |   Literature Review

2.1   |   Theoretical Background

The TAM explains technology use by emphasising users' per­ ceptions of a system. Davis (1989) defined perceived useful­ ness as the belief that a system can improve task performance and perceived ease of use as the belief that using it requires little effort. Prior studies have shown that perceived ease of use can enhance perceived usefulness, improve attitudes towards AI and increase users' intentions to adopt AI tools (Huang et al. 2024). In AI–supported EFL contexts, TAM is therefore useful for explaining how students' perceptions of AI tools may shape their engagement with technology–medi­ ated learning.

In AI–supported EFL learning, perceived interactivity in learner–AI interaction represents learners' judgements about the quality of their interaction with AI, including whether it is controllable, responsive, meaningful and engaging. Positive AI interaction experiences may reduce learning uncertainty, strengthen learners' control over academic tasks and promote learning engagement, thereby helping to reduce academic procrastination. However, this process may also be shaped by teacher support, as teachers guide students' AI use and help them interpret AI–generated feedback. Thus, perceived teacher support may serve as an interpersonal and contextual mecha­ nism through which positive perceptions of AI interaction are translated into lower academic procrastination.

Perceived interactivity of learner–AI interaction refers to learn­ ers' subjective perception of their ability to control the interac­ tion process during meaningful and engaging exchanges with AI, as well as their perception of the extent to which AI responds to their interactive behaviours (Wang et al. 2025). In AI–sup­ ported learning environments, this perception is particularly important because learners' input not only influences the qual­ ity of the content generated by AI but also shapes their subjec­ tive experience of the learning process (Zhao and Yang 2026), achievement emotions (Yao et al. 2025) and level of engagement (Liu 2026). When learners perceive AI interaction as controlla­ ble, responsive, meaningful and engaging, they may experience stronger task control, lower learning uncertainty and more sus­ tained engagement in L2 learning.

Previous research has shown that AI technology can provide personalised learning and immersive experiences (Yang 2026; Zhao and Yang 2026), enhance learners' engagement (Liu 2026) by satisfying their basic psychological needs (Derakhshan and Park  2026a) and fostering positive psychological traits and achievement emotions (Wang  2026) and ultimately lead to the improvement of language skills (Moorhouse et  al.  2025). However, existing research has largely focused on generalised attitudes towards technology acceptance, AI literacy and the effects of AI on learners' emotions and behaviours, while pay­ ing relatively less attention to learners' subjective perceptions in L2 learning contexts. In addition, studies have shown that teachers play an important role in AI–empowered learning en­ vironments by guiding learners to use AI effectively and pro­ viding scaffolded support (Derakhshan et  al.  2026; Yang and Derakhshan 2026). Therefore, learners' perceptions of AI inter­ action do not exist independently of interpersonal support, but are instead embedded in instructional contexts in which teacher support remains highly important.

2.3   |   Perceived Teacher Support

Perceived teacher support is commonly understood as stu­ dents' subjective evaluation of the care, assistance and support they receive from teachers (Liu and Li 2023). Teacher support is generally regarded as a multidimensional construct, com­ monly encompassing academic, instrumental and emotional support (Wong et al. 2018). In other words, teachers not only help students accomplish learning tasks by explaining knowl­ edge, providing resources and offering feedback, but also alleviate learning stress and sustain students' confidence through encouragement, understanding and emotional reas­ surance (Yao et al. 2025). However, in AI–supported and tech­ nology–enhanced environments, the importance of teacher support has not been diminished by technological develop­ ments. Although AI and digital tools can provide personalised learning paths, immediate feedback and abundant resources (Yang 2026), students may still need teachers to guide their use of AI tools, screen and evaluate AI–generated informa­ tion, provide pedagogical feedback and regulate their AI–sup­ ported learning processes. In EFL contexts, teacher support not only helps facilitate learners' positive emotions and alleviate negative emotions (Derakhshan et al. 2025) but also enhances their emotional in­ telligence (Li and Pan 2025) by fostering empathy (Liu, Shen, et  al.  2025), thereby influencing learning outcomes through emotional variables (Wong et al. 2018). Existing evidence sug­ gests that perceived teacher support can directly reduce aca­ demic procrastination and indirectly weaken it by promoting academic self–efficacy (Huang and Zhu 2025). Teacher support may reduce academic procrastination by strengthening students' confidence in task completion and discouraging avoidance or delay behaviours. Nevertheless, previous studies on teacher sup­ port and procrastination have mainly been conducted in general education or traditional learning contexts. It remains unclear how perceived teacher support affects academic procrastination in AI–supported EFL settings, where learners engage not only with teachers but also with AI tools.

2.4   |   Academic Procrastination

Academic procrastination is the intentional delay of academic tasks despite awareness of possible negative consequences (Balkis et al. 2024). It is not merely a time–management prob­ lem; rather, it is closely linked to motivational, emotional and self–regulatory difficulties (Solomon and Rothblum  1984). In L2 learning contexts, academic procrastination may manifest as learners' delay or avoidance of language learning tasks, such as vocabulary review, reading practice, writing assignments, oral communication activities, grammar learning or AI–supported learning activities. Because these tasks often require sustained effort, repeated practice and autonomous engagement, learn­ ers' task avoidance and delayed participation may hinder the gradual development of their language proficiency. Existing research consistently shows that academic procrastination has a significant negative impact on learner development (Kurker and Surucu 2024). It is not only associated with lower academic achievement (Balkis et al. 2024; Liu, Liu, et al. 2025) but is also often accompanied by numerous negative emotions and a de­ cline in overall psychological well–being (Huang and Zhu 2025; Ma et al. 2022).

It is particularly noteworthy that in digital learning envi­ ronments, academic procrastination often exhibits greater complexity (Liu, Liu, et al. 2025). Although online and tech­ nology–enhanced learning transcend temporal and spatial constraints, granting learners greater flexibility and auton­ omy, such environments typically lack face–to–face exter­ nal supervision and immediate guidance (Ma et  al.  2022). 2.5   |   Research Questions

Nevertheless, existing research on academic procrastination remains predominantly focused on general educational con­ texts, with insufficient attention given to AI–supported L2 learning scenarios. As AI becomes increasingly embedded in foreign language learning, learners may benefit from im­ proved convenience, but they may also be more vulnerable to procrastination as a result of reliance on technology or re­ duced outside supervision. Yet the relationships among learn­ ers' perceptions of interacting with AI, teacher support and academic procrastination in this context have not been fully examined. To address the above research gap, this study aims to address the following questions:

RQ1.  Are perceived interactivity of learner–AI interaction, perceived teacher support and academic procrastination signifi­ cantly related to one another?

RQ2.  To what extent do perceived interactivity of learner–AI interaction and perceived teacher support significantly predict academic procrastination?

RQ3.  Does perceived teacher support mediate the relationship between perceived interactivity of learner–AI interaction and academic procrastination?

3   |   Method

3.1   |   Participants

This study adopted a quantitative design and was conducted at five universities in northern China. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling and completed an online sur­ vey by scanning a QR code generated through Wenjuanxing. To be eligible, respondents had to satisfy two criteria: they had to be undergraduate students enrolled at one of the five par­ ticipating universities and have previous experience with AI– assisted English learning. The final sample consisted of 613 undergraduates, all of whom submitted valid questionnaires. Among them, 326 were male, representing 53.18% of the sam­ ple and 287 were female, representing 46.82%. Most participants were aged 18–20 years (N = 420, 68.52%), while the remaining 193 were aged 21–23 years (31.48%). With regard to year level, first–year students constituted the largest group (N = 269, 43.88%), followed by second–year students (N = 146, 23.82%), fourth–year students (N = 120, 19.58%) and third–year students (N = 78, 12.72%). 3.2   |   Instruments

A quantitative approach was used in this study, with data ob­ tained from three questionnaire scales. In terms of measure­ ment quality, a reliability coefficient of at least 0.70 was regarded as indicating acceptable internal consistency (Taber 2018). To assess construct validity, the study adopted the model fit criteria recommended by Kline (2023): a χ2/df value between 3 and 5 was regarded as acceptable, CFI and TLI values above 0.90 in­ dicated an acceptable fit and RMSEA and SRMR values below 0.08 were considered to reflect a satisfactory model fit.

3.2.1   |   Perceived Interactivity of Learner–AI Interaction Scale

Learners' perceptions of their interaction with AI were measured using the Perceived Interactivity of Learner–AI Interaction Scale (Wang et al. 2025). The instrument uses a six–point Likert scale, with response options ranging from 1 (‘Strongly disagree’) to 6 (‘Strongly agree’). It comprises four dimensions, namely responsiveness, learner control, learning engagement and per­ sonalisation. A representative item is: ‘AI processes my response quickly.’ In this study, the scale demonstrated high internal consistency, with an overall Cronbach's α of 0.976. The scale showed good internal consistency, with Cronbach's α coeffi­ cients of 0.925, 0.871, 0.934 and 0.885 for the four dimensions, respectively. Its construct validity was also supported by ac­ ceptable model fit indices, including χ2/df = 2.145, CFI = 0.988, TLI = 0.985, RMSEA = 0.043 and SRMR = 0.016.

3.2.2   |   Perceived Teacher Support Scale

Students' perceived teacher support was assessed using the Perceived Teacher Support Scale developed by Wu et al. (2024), which includes four dimensions: instrumental support, emo­ tional support, informational support and appraisal support. Items were rated on a six–point Likert scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 6 (Strongly Agree), with ‘My teachers trust me’ as a sample item. In this study, the scale showed high reliability, with an overall Cronbach's α of 0.981 and dimension–level coeffi­ cients of 0.935, 0.923, 0.934 and 0.920, respectively. Its construct validity was supported by satisfactory fit indices: χ2/df = 1.336, CFI = 0.994, TLI = 0.993, RMSEA = 0.023 and SRMR = 0.014.

3.3   |   Procedure and Data Analysis

Data were collected over one month during the spring semes­ ter of 2026. Before completing the questionnaire, participants were informed of the study purpose, ethical issues, the vol­ untary nature of participation and their right to withdraw at any time.

The collected quantitative data were analysed using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 24.0. Descriptive statistics (means, standard devia­ tions, skewness and kurtosis) and reliability analyses were com­ puted using SPSS 27.0. To address Research Question 1, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the intercor­ relations among the three variables. Construct validity was as­ sessed by conducting confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) on the overall measurement model, with model fit evaluated using χ2/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA and SRMR. For Research Questions 2 and 3, structural equation modelling (SEM) was performed to examine direct and indirect effects. Mediation effects were tested using bootstrapping with 1000 resamples and 95% confidence intervals under both percentile (PC) and bias–corrected (BC) estimations.

Furthermore, this study also found that perceived teacher sup­ port plays a partial mediating role between perceived interac­ tivity of learner–AI interaction and academic procrastination. From the perspective of TAM (Davis 1989), perceived interac­ tivity of learner–AI interaction reflects learners' positive judge­ ments regarding the usefulness, ease of use and supportiveness of AI tools, viewing them as important resources that help com­ plete learning tasks. In this context, AI not only assists learners in solving basic and procedural problems but also optimises their overall learning experience. Positive AI interaction experiences may further encourage learners to participate more actively in the learning process. As learners engage more deeply with AI– supported tasks, they may become better able to distinguish the problems that AI can address from those requiring teacher guid­ ance, such as strategic direction, deeper feedback and emotional support, thereby strengthening their perceived teacher support. Second, from a supplementary CVT perspective (Pekrun 2006), when learners perceive a higher level of teacher support, they are more likely to reduce uncertainty when facing complex tasks, strengthen their sense of control over tasks and enhance their recognition of task meaning and the value of learning engage­ ment. Accordingly, perceived interactivity of learner–AI interac­ tion appears to decrease academic procrastination both directly and indirectly through enhanced perceived teacher support. This finding is supported by Zhao and Yang (2026). Their study points out that in AI–mediated language learning contexts, learners' emotional and attitudinal evaluations significantly in­ fluence their learning engagement as well as their judgements regarding the balance between AI support and human instruc­ tion. This suggests that AI-­related experiences not only affect learning outcomes directly but also shape how learners perceive the relative value of AI support and human support in the learn­ ing process. In this sense, the present study further extends their view by showing that perceived interactivity of learner–AI inter­ action can indirectly reduce academic procrastination through enhancing perceived teacher support, indicating that positive AI interaction experiences may ultimately make learners more aware of the unique value of teachers' in-­depth guidance.

The second direct path shows that perceived teacher support negatively predicts academic procrastination, indicating that learners who perceive stronger teacher support tend to report lower levels of procrastination. As a supplementary theoretical perspective, control–value theory (CVT) (Pekrun 2006) may help explain this relationship. Specifically, teacher support can strengthen learners' sense of control over academic tasks by providing clear guidance, timely feedback and emotional encouragement throughout the learning process. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that perceived teacher support can reduce academic procrastination (Huang and Zhu 2025) and alleviate learners' negative learning expe­ riences as an important social–contextual factor (Liu, Shen, et al. 2025). In the present study, teacher support may reduce academic procrastination by enhancing learners' sense of con­ trol and task value when they face academic tasks, thereby weakening avoidance tendencies triggered by task difficulty, unclear goals or fear of failure.

Notably, the negative predictive effect of perceived teacher sup­ port on academic procrastination is slightly stronger in absolute terms than that of perceived interactivity in learner–AI inter­ action. This difference may be related to the different types of support provided by AI and teachers. AI interactivity mainly offers immediate and basic support, helping learners solve sur­ face–level problems and reduce initial barriers when starting ac­ ademic tasks. However, after these initial obstacles are reduced, learners may still need deeper teacher guidance, including strategic direction, personalised feedback and emotional sup­ port. From a supplementary CVT perspective (Pekrun  2006), teacher support may have a more direct influence on learners' sense of control and task value than the immediate convenience provided by AI interactivity. Teachers' guidance and feedback can reduce learners' uncertainty when they face complex tasks, thereby strengthening their control over learning and weaken­ ing avoidance tendencies. This interpretation is largely consis­ tent with Xu and Zheng (2025), who found that learners tend to use AI for lower–order tasks such as translation and vocabulary substitution, while higher–order tasks still depend on learners' own agency. From a pedagogical standpoint, this study provides several teaching implications for AI–supported EFL learning. First, schools and teachers should help students develop positive traits (Wang 2026; Yang and Zhao 2024) and guide them to engage appropriately with AI–mediated informal digital learning of English (AI–IDLE) (Liu et al. 2024). In this way, students may learn to view AI tools as supportive resources for learning, prob­ lem solving and task initiation, rather than as substitutes on which they passively rely. This may further promote more positive learning behaviour patterns. Second, teachers need to improve their AI literacy and learn how to integrate AI effectively into teaching and feedback practices (Yang and Derakhshan 2026). More importantly, teachers should recognise that their role is not weakened in AI–supported learning environments. Instead, they are expected to serve as deeper guides, learning facilitators and providers of emotional support. Through proactive interac­ tion, targeted strategic guidance, personalised feedback and af­ fective support, teachers can strengthen students' self–efficacy and academic engagement while reducing communication ap­ prehension, thereby helping them cope with negative outcomes such as academic procrastination (Yao et al. 2025). Finally, cur­ riculum design should give greater attention to students' basic psychological needs, including autonomy, competence and relatedness. This means that AI–supported learning activities should not only provide technological convenience, but also create learning conditions that support students' confidence, agency and interpersonal connection.
