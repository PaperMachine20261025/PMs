Development and validation of the perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction scale

Feifei Wang1 · Alan C. K. Cheung1 · Ching Sing Chai2 · Jin Liu3

Received: 28 March 2024 / Accepted: 7 August 2024 / Published online: 31 August 2024 © The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2024, corrected publication 2025

Abstract As learners are able to perceive interactivity when interacting with instructors or peer learners in traditional learning environments, learners are similarly able to per­ ceive interactivity when interacting with artificial intelligence (AI) in AI-supported learning environments. Advancements in AI, such as generative AI including Chat­ GPT and Midjourney, enhance learners’ perceived interactivity, thereby facilitating learning through AI-enabled interaction. However, there is no scale in education for measuring perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction. This study develops a 17-item scale to assess the extent to which a learner perceives interactivity with AI from four dimensions: responsiveness, personalization, learner control, and learn­ ing engagement. The sample group included 422 Chinese university students for the first application and 306 university students for the second application. Both the exploratory factor analysis and the confirmatory factor analysis verified the factor structure of the scale. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the whole scale was 0.948, whereas the Cronbach’s alpha values for the four dimensions ranged between 0.820 and 0.915. Results suggested that this scale was a reliable and valid instru­ ment. This study also found that perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction was significantly associated with AI tools, learners’ behavioral intentions to use AI in learning, months of using AI in learning, and average duration of using AI in learning each time, and not associated with ages, genders, education levels, and fields of education. Finally, theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

However, this interdisciplinary concept has mostly been investigated in fields other than education, such as business (Gao et al., 2010; Liu, 2003), computer science (Borsci et al., 2021), and commu­ nication and media (Sohn & Lee, 2005). After Sims (1997) proposed the question, “Is interactivity a forgotten art?” in education, an increasing number of educational researchers turned their attention to this art (Kettanurak et al., 2001; Kwastek, 2008). The research in education mainly focused on interactivity in learner-instructor inter­ action, learner-learner interaction, learner-content interaction, and learner-self inter­ action (Sun & Hsu, 2013). Meanwhile, the advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) has seen more and more technological developments, such as generative AI. Gen­ erative AI relies on deep learning models to produce diverse simulated interactions with learners, as exemplified by ChatGPT for generating human-like conversations and Midjourney for creating text-based images (Chien et al., 2024). With the popu­ larity of AI tools and advancements in generative AI, a recent surge of research on learner-AI interaction has highlighted opportunities for traditional learning environ­ ments, showcasing AI’s potential to provide unlimited flexibility in space and time, personalized learning experiences, and increased interactivity (Chiu, 2023; Zhai et al., 2021). Thus, interactivity in learner-AI interaction has become a noteworthy topic in AI-supported learning environments (Chiu, 2023; Memarian & Doleck, 2024; Petersen et al., 2022).

Research indicates that increased interactivity in learner-AI interaction means offering learners more choices, control, and feedback, thus producing better learning outcomes (Patwardhan & Murthy, 2015; Wang et al., 2023c). However, “perceived interactivity”, an under-researched concept, is far more important than “interactiv­ ity” in learner-AI interaction (Sun & Hsu, 2013). Less is known about perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction. Current research on perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction has suggested two further research avenues. First, given that no scale in education has been designed to measure perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction, a scale on this noteworthy topic is needed to help researchers, educators, AI developers, and other relevant stakeholders to identify the relationship between perceived interactivity and other variables that influence learners’ perceptions, behav­ iors, and achievement when learners use AI in learning (Shin et al., 2016). Perceived interactivity is likely to be associated with variables such as behavioral intention to use, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, attitude, actual use, self-efficacy, trust, collaborative learning, and learning performance (Liu & Ma, 2023; Patwardhan & Murthy, 2015). Second, since there remains a lack of consensus on the definitions of interactivity and perceived interactivity, the inconsistent usage of the definitions in empirical studies complicates the comparability of relevant studies (Petersen et al., 2022). Therefore, there is a need for such a scale to measure perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction, based on clear definitions of interactivity and perceived interactivity that are appropriate for AI-supported learning environments.

To address these needs for future research, this study aims to develop and vali­ date the Perceived Interactivity of Learner-AI Interaction Scale to measure learn­ ers’ perceived interactivity when interacting with AI in learning. To this end, this study takes the following steps of scale development and validation: (1) identifying clear definitions of interactivity and perceived interactivity in AI-supported learning

environments, (2) constructing a theoretical framework of perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction, (3) developing and validating the scale, and (4) testing the relationship between perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction and other rel­ evant personal and AI usage factors (e.g., behavioral intentions to use AI in learning and demographic characteristics).

1  Literature review

AI expands the learning style applied in traditional education by offering advan­ tages such as personalized learning, immediate feedback, and unlimited flexibility in space and time within interactive learning environments (Zhai et al., 2021). With the increasing accessibility and popularity of AI tools, especially advancements in generative AI, learners are experiencing enhanced interactivity with AI in learning (Chiu, 2023). A recent surge of research on the under-researched topic of perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction has provided new opportunities and challenges; thus, perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction has become a crucial issue in AI-supported learning environments (Memarian & Doleck, 2024).

Current research indicates that perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction demonstrates considerable potential compared to traditional forms of learning. Some studies highlight that perceived interactivity is a key feature of interactive educa­ tional activities that enables learners to acquire knowledge while interacting with AI (Chien et al., 2024; Koć-Januchta et al., 2020). Perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction acts as a scaffolding mechanism that not only enhances learners’ engage­ ment and reduces anxiety during activities, but also facilitates the flow status of the learning process (Wang et al., 2023b). Other studies indicate that high levels of per­ ceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction are associated with positive learning effects for offering learners more choices, control, and feedback (Chiu, 2023; Wang et al., 2023c).

Despite the considerable potential of perceived interactivity of learner-AI interac­ tion, there has been a challenge in research on this topic. The challenge is that the two concepts, interactivity and perceived interactivity, have been used interchange­ ably due to a lack of consensus on their definitions (Girish et al., 2022; Petersen et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023a). To address the challenge, the following sections of the literature review will present three parts: an overview of research related to the definitions, a theoretical framework for perceived interactivity of learner-AI interac­ tion conceptualized from the literature, and an exploration of the literature regarding personal and AI usage factors potentially associated with perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction.

1.1  Definitions of interactivity and perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction

In AI-supported learning environments, interaction, interactivity, and perceived inter­ activity are similar concepts that are often used interchangeably, but there are distinc­ tions worth noting among them (Petersen et al., 2022; Wagner, 1997). Interaction

refers to the process in which two objects or events reciprocally influence each other through synchronous and/or asynchronous communication carried out by means of responses, feedback, or technology (Koć-Januchta et al., 2020; Wagner, 1997). Inter­ activity has been defined as the connection between subsequent messages and the extent to which earlier messages refer to even preceding messages in a series of com­ munication exchanges (Petersen et al., 2022; Rafaeli, 1988). Perceived interactivity is the perception that learners have about their control over the interaction process or about responses from their communicative partners regarding their communicative behaviors in meaningful and engaging interaction (Shao & Chen, 2021; Sims, 1997). In this sense, interactivity emphasizes real and observable communication exchanges in interaction and thus implies real human behavior (Petersen et al., 2022), or sys­ tems’ functional features and actions (Plass & Pawar, 2020). Perceived interactivity is subjectively experienced by learners and can be measured by asking learners their perceptions during the use of technology (Shao & Chen, 2021). Detailed distinctions of interaction, interactivity, and perceived interactivity are shown in Table 1, includ­ ing their definitions, key attributes, measurements, and examples.

The relationship between interactivity and perceived interactivity, as actual system function/real learner behavior and perception, is complex. Perceived interactivity can be high even when only a single valued feature of technology is available, and can be low even when many interactivity features of technology are present if participants do not use them or do not find them helpful (Rodríguez-Ardura & Meseguer-Artola, 2016). Perceived interactivity has attracted attention in research on AI-supported learn­ ing environments. With advancements in AI’s interactive capabilities and prolifera­ tion of AI tools in education, learner-AI interaction has been generated to improve such learners’ abilities as creativity, critical thinking, and self-regulation that can affect their performances and perceptions (Zhai et al., 2021). Perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction has shown considerable potential compared to traditional ways of learning. Higher levels of perceived interactivity are associated with better instructional effects and enhanced learning achievement (Wang et al., 2023a; Zhang et al., 2021). Moreover, perceived interactivity helps to improve learners’ perceived enjoyment, participation intention, satisfaction, and perceived learning (Benvenuti et al., 2023; Wang & Cheung, 2024).

1.2  A theoretical framework of perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction

After clarifying and establishing the definition of the concept, the next important step in developing a reliable measure is to identify its theoretical dimensions (Churchill, 1979; Yim et al., 2023). According to previous studies that have analyzed perceived interactivity and characteristics of learner-AI interaction, perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction is grounded in constructivist principles, where learners assimi­ late new knowledge by connecting it with their existing understanding and actively engaging in the co-construction of knowledge with AI (Robertson et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023c). The interaction process consists of AI-initiated and learner-initiated inter­ action, with learner engagement as the main component to reflect the active involve­ ment of learners (Alanne, 2021). Therefore, the Engagement Control Model (Sims, 1997) is deemed appropriate to be the conceptual basis of the theoretical framework of perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction, for this model is grounded in con­ structivism with emphasis on learners’ engagement, involvement, and motivation in interaction with technology. This model proposes two key dimensions of the concept regarding learner-initiated interaction: learner control and learning engagement. Two other dimensions that are frequently highlighted in the literature include responsive­ ness and personalization (Alimamy & Gnoth, 2022; Gao et al., 2010; Liu, 2003; Xu et al., 2011), both of which fall under AI-initiated interaction. Taken together, the current body of research indicates that the theoretical framework of perceived inter­ activity of learner-AI interaction consists of the four dimensions, as shown in Fig. 1.

Specifically, in the framework, learning engagement and learner control are typi­ cally associated with learner-initiated interaction (Sims, 1997), and responsiveness and personalization are generally associated with AI-initiated interaction (Chandra & Rahman, 2024). In situations where restrictions of interaction (e.g., time, place, and skill levels in using AI) regarding learner control and responsiveness are minimized, higher levels of learning engagement and personalization may be enhanced (Lee, 2005; Xue et al., 2023).

1.2.1  Responsiveness

Many researchers emphasized the importance of responsiveness in the concept of perceived interactivity (e.g., Shao & Chen, 2021; Voorveld et al., 2011). Respon­ siveness refers to interactive communication with fast, appropriate, and relevant messages to sustain a thread of continuity (Johnson et al., 2006). When a learner per­ ceives that the temporal delay between messages decreases, and that the appropriate­ ness and relevance between messages increase, the coupling of learner-AI interaction becomes closer and more intense, so interactivity grows (Walther, 2020). In learner- AI interaction, a message is perceived as highly responsive if it is fast, appropriate, and relevant, and maintains the continuity of interaction.

1.2.2  Personalization

Personalization was found to be a saliant factor of perceived interactivity (Plass & Pawar, 2020; Song & Zinkhan, 2008). Personalization has been defined as the ability to provide learners with content, paths, or scaffolds customized to their prior knowl­ edge, needs, preferences, or contexts (Zhang et al., 2020). Personalization of AI’s recommendation systems consists of three categories (Huang et al., 2023): contentbased (i.e., grouping items according to content similarity), collaborative (i.e., pro­ viding recommendations based on similar attributes of learner groups), and hybrid recommendations (i.e., both content-based and collaborative). Personalization places

learners at the center of learning, given AI’s adaptive capabilities that facilitate learn­ ers’ interaction with the learning environment.

1.2.3  Learner control

Learner control originates from the Engagement Control Model (Sims, 1997), which focuses on the extent to which learners are making learning-related or navigational decisions while using technology. As an essential part of perceived interactivity, learner control over interaction reflects self-efficacy or confidence in performing activ­ ities with technology (Petersen et al., 2022). Learner control means allowing learners some control over AI with voluntary and instrumental actions that directly influence learning experiences (Liu & Shrum, 2002). In contrast to control by AI, learners may control learning content, sequence, or progress (Berendt et al., 2020). When learners perceive active control, their interactivity tends to be high because they are con­ stantly controlling their experiences in interaction (Petersen et al., 2022).

1.2.4  Learning engagement

Learning engagement is another centered dimension of the Engagement Control Model (Sims, 1997), which emphasizes learners’ involvement in interaction in a way designed to facilitate learning. Learning engagement supports deep cognitive pro­ cessing in interaction. Learning engagement refers to the perception of learners that results from interacting with AI in learning and that generates involvement with the content studied (Shao & Chen, 2021). Engaged learners have high levels of involve­ ment that prepare them better for active learning. Therefore, learners perceive more interactivity when they feel involved in learning experiences and engage in appropri­ ate cognitive processes (Shin et al., 2016).

1.3  Potential associations of perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction with personal factors and AI usage factors

1.3.1  Personal factors

Previous research has shown that the four personal factors might be associated with perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction: ages, genders, education levels, and fields of education. As for ages, younger age groups tended to have a higher level of perceived interactivity with highly interactive technologies compared to older age groups (Fahmy & Ghoneim, 2019), since younger “digital natives” were likely to be more satisfied with the interactive digital products and older “digital immigrants” were generally more satisfied with static digital products (Kirk et al., 2015). In terms of genders, research indicated that females exhibited more positive perceptions of interactivity with technology than males (Shao & Chen, 2021). Females were more likely to exhibit interactive behaviors as more social and process-oriented, and males tended to display independent behaviors as more pragmatic and task-oriented (Lin et al., 2017). Regarding education levels, students at lower education levels perceived more interactivity with technology compared to students at higher education levels

(Fahmy & Ghoneim, 2019). As to fields of education, learners from scientific majors experienced higher levels of perceived interactivity with highly interactive technolo­ gies than learners from non-scientific majors (Fahmy & Ghoneim, 2019). However, since these findings were not revealed in the context of AI-supported learning envi­ ronments, the relationship between the four personal factors and perceived interactiv­ ity of learner-AI interaction is far from conclusive.

1.3.2  AI usage factors

Besides the four personal factors, four other AI usage factors might be related to perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction: AI tools, average duration of using AI each time, months of using AI in learning, and behavioral intentions to use AI. As AI technologies advance, AI tools are building stronger interactive capabilities to enhance the interactive experience for users according to user habits and preferences (Yin et al., 2021). Accordingly, different AI tools may exhibit different degrees of interactivity, and learners may have different levels of perceived interactivity depend­ ing on the tools used (Patwardhan & Murthy, 2015). Among AI tools used in educa­ tion, generative AI increases perceived interactivity in digital learning environments to foster positive learning experiences (Chiu, 2023). Intelligent tutoring systems may also enhance perceived interactivity, which in turn improves learners’ motivation, cognitive skills, and engagement (Ahuja et al., 2022). Various AI tools with interac­ tive capabilities are also likely to improve learners’ perceived interactivity with AI (Wang et al., 2023a). Furthermore, the duration that learners interact with AI may influence learners’ perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction since learners have various experiences in navigating AI resources, finding what they are looking for, and creating content that is understandable to them (Chou et al., 2022). There­ fore, learners’ average duration of using AI each time and months of using AI in learning should be taken into account. Additionally, learners’ behavioral intentions to use AI are associated with their perceptions of the interaction process (Chou et al., 2022). While these studies analyzed the four AI usage factors that are potentially related to perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction, they have not yet quanti­ fied the potential associations and confirmed the effects.

Therefore, to examine the potential associations, after developing and presenting the Perceived Interactivity of Learner-AI Interaction Scale, we assessed its relation­ ship with the aforementioned personal and AI usage factors. The findings may shed light on further understanding of this topic.

1.4  Existing survey instruments and need for this study

Given the interdisciplinary nature of perceived interactivity, existing scales for mea­ suring perceived interactivity have been developed and validated in fields other than education. For instance, scales on perceived interactivity were developed in business for mobile advertisements (e.g., Gao et al., 2010) and for websites (e.g., Liu, 2003; Wu, 2006), in computer science for usability in interaction with chatbots (e.g., Borsci et al., 2021), and in communication and media for web interaction (e.g., Sohn & Lee,

2005). However, in education, no scale has been found to measure perceived interac­ tivity of learner-AI interaction.

As perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction increasingly receives attention in educational research, the current instrument of measuring perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction has relied mainly on a three-item instrument developed by Pituch and Lee (2006) to explore system interactivity of a web-based learning system in relevant studies (e.g., Girish et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023a, b). Moreover, some studies (e.g., Sun & Hsu, 2013) drew on several items developed by other studies (e.g., Kettanurak et al., 2001) to measure separate dimensions. While use of several items has built a solid foundation for further research, a well-developed multi-item scale including all potential dimensions will provide advantages of accuracy, validity, and reliability (DeVellis & Thorpe, 2021). Therefore, there is a need for a compre­ hensive scale whose validity and reliability can be verified for measuring perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction.

Furthermore, there is another need to use clearly and consistently defined con­ cepts of interactivity and perceived interactivity in educational research (Petersen et al., 2022). Interactivity and perceived interactivity are sometimes used interchange­ ably. For example, interactivity refers to observable system functions in some studies (e.g., Alam & Mohanty, 2023; Sun & Hsu, 2013), but in other studies, interactivity means learners’ subjective perceptions of their participation in interaction (Girish et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023a). The inconsistent usage of the definitions of interactiv­ ity and perceived interactivity has complicated the comparability of empirical studies in the burgeoning field (Petersen et al., 2022; Quiring & Schweiger, 2008).

To summarize, a comprehensive, valid, and reliable scale for measuring perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction has yet to be developed and validated, based on clear definitions of interactivity and perceived interactivity and consistent usage of the definitions in AI-supported learning environments. To address these needs, this study aims to develop and validate the Perceived Interactivity of Learner-AI Interaction Scale, in line with the definitions of interactivity and perceived interac­ tivity presented in the section of the literature review. Additionally, this study tested and applied the scale to examine the relationship between perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction and eight factors suggested in the literature: four personal fac­ tors (i.e., ages, genders, education levels, and fields of education) and four AI usage factors (i.e., AI tools, average duration of using AI each time, months of using AI in learning, and behavioral intentions to use AI in learning).

2  Method

This study aims to develop a measurement tool to examine learners’ perceived inter­ activity of learner-AI interaction. To this end, a scale development method is appro­ priate because a scale is to measure phenomena that are believed to exist in theory but that cannot be assessed directly (DeVellis & Thorpe, 2021). We followed the procedure suggested by Churchill (1979) for developing and validating a scale (Yim et al., 2023): specifying the theoretical dimensions of the concept in literature review, generating an item pool, collecting data, purifying measure by the exploratory factor

analysis (EFA) and the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and refining and validat­ ing the scale. In the process, the EFA aims to identify the underlying factor structure in the dataset that aligns with theoretical dimensions, thereby facilitating the under­ standing of the construct, and the CFA tries to test the factor structure during the EFA in terms of whether it can be confirmed or not (Hair et al., 2010). Since the first step of specifying the dimensions of the concept has been completed in the above litera­ ture review, the remaining steps are implemented below.

2.1  Generation of an item pool

An item pool was generated at four stages. First, the relevant literature was reviewed, and the scales measuring similar properties were investigated to generate an initial item pool. In particular, the Engagement Control Model (Sims, 1997) was employed to identify the theoretical infrastructure. Based on this model, we carefully examined other scholars’ studies on perceived interactivity and selected items in education. The literature review produced an initial item pool of 20 items, measuring responsiveness (Gao et al., 2010; Liu, 2003), learner control (Liu, 2003; Wu, 2006), learning engage­ ment (Fredricks et al., 2004; Gallini & Moely, 2003; McMillan & Hwang, 2002), and personalization (Alimamy & Gnoth, 2022; Xu et al., 2011). The item pool was measured on a 6-point Likert-type scale with the options: “strongly disagree,” “dis­ agree,” “somewhat disagree,” “somewhat agree,” “agree,” and “strongly agree.” The even-numbered 6-point scale was adopted because an even number tends to force respondents to choose a definite option, potentially reducing the likelihood of select­ ing a neutral option without careful consideration (Brown, 2000) and an expression of “don’t know” (Raaijmakers et al., 2000). Moreover, the 6-point Likert scale has the advantages of minimizing risks associated with deviations in personal decisionmaking and enhancing discrimination and reliability of responses in self-reported surveys (Chomeya, 2010).

Second, we interviewed 16 students who had the experience of using AI in learn­ ing about their thoughts on perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction. The tran­ scripts of the interviews were analyzed, and the main content was added to the item pool appropriately. These students were from four universities, each located in one of the four cities in China: Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Guangzhou. The four cities were also where this study collected surveys. The first criterion for determining the four cities is that these cities have sustained advantages in AI development and application in China, as suggested by their top rankings in “China AI City Competi­ tiveness,” including AI infrastructure, products, developers, users, etc. (TMT, 2024). The widespread development and adoption of AI in the cities may give learners broad access to diverse AI tools. Another criterion is that, in these cities, AI education was initiated relatively early (Huang, 2021), and AI has been broadly integrated into edu­ cation (Knox, 2020). This offers a practical foundation for learners from different backgrounds to engage with AI across various educational settings. Together, the cri­ teria for selecting the four cities were designed to access a broad range of university students with extensive experience in using AI in education.

The 16 students in the interview included eight undergraduate students, five mas­ ter’s students, and three doctoral students who majored in economics, psychology,

law, linguistics, visual arts, physics, electrical engineering, and clinical medicine. Their mean age was 22.1, including seven females and nine males. The interviews were analyzed by three researchers. Considering that the interviews captured items that were not covered in the initial item pool, we added three additional items, result­ ing in a total of 23 items.

Third, concerning the content validity, we sent the 23 items to seven experts to obtain their feedback on whether the items were suitable for evaluating the antici­ pated dimensions and whether the expressions of the items were clear and accurate. All the experts possessed doctoral degrees. Three of them had publications on AI in education, two on education measurement and evaluation, and two on linguistics. In addition, the experts were provided with a form that introduced the purpose of the scale, target participants, items, and anticipated dimensions. They were asked to evaluate each item with the options: “suitable,” “requiring improvement,” and “unsuitable.” Their feedback suggested the removal of six items and the improve­ ment of two items. We made improvements to the two items in line with the experts’ feedback, obtaining an item pool of 17 items.

Fourth, a preliminary test of the scale was conducted with 20 students among a sample of our target participants. Participants shared opinions on whether the items were clear and appropriate in relation to their learning experience. Their feedback indicated no need to revise the items. The final 17-item scale included five items on responsiveness, three on learner control, six on learning engagement, and three on personalization.

2.2  Data collection

Data were collected in China via a widely used professional online survey platform, Wenjuanxing, in January 2024. This survey started with detailing the purpose and protocol of the study. University students who had the experience of using AI in learning were invited to participate. Participants provided informed consent before answering the questions, and they could withdraw at any point.

This study involved two distribution phases for data collection. Initially, 600 sur­ veys were collected for the EFA phase to identify the factor structure of the 17 items. Subsequently, 400 surveys were collected for the CFA phase to test and confirm the factor structure of the items derived from the EFA. In the total of 1000 surveys, there was no missing value, given that the surveys could be submitted only after all the questions were answered on the platform. Filters were used to exclude data via three criteria: (1) if the total length of using AI in learning was less than two months, (2) if the respondents were not students from universities of the four cities in China: Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Guangzhou, and (3) if the surveys were completed within 90 s. After applying the three exclusion criteria, this study obtained 422 partic­ ipants for the EFA dataset and 306 participants for the CFA dataset. In scale develop­ ment studies, it is recommended to determine the proper sample size by multiplying the number of items by ten or twenty, and studies may employ a parsimonious and adequate sample size, guided by conceptual and practical considerations (Hair et al., 2010). When evaluated together, it is found that the sample sizes for both the EFA and the CFA are sufficient for further analyses.

For the EFA dataset, 56.9% were male, 66.1% were aged between 17 and 20 years, and 96% were undergraduate students. For the CFA dataset, 60.1% were male, 66% were between 17 and 20 years old, and 93.8% were undergraduate students. Regard­ ing the AI tool most frequently used in learning, participants filled in the survey according to their personal experiences. Based on the taxonomies of student-focused AI tools in education by Holmes and Tuomi (2022) and by Zhang and Aslan (2021), we modified and classified the tool into five categories: generative AI (e.g. ChatGPT and Midjourney), intelligent tutoring system (e.g. Spark and LAIX), automatic forma­ tive assessment (e.g., iFLYTEK scoring and Zuoyebang), personalized learning sys­ tem (e.g., course-specific expert system and self-developed system for teaching and learning), and writing assistant (e.g., Grammarly and Baidu Translate). For the EFA dataset, the most frequently used AI tool in learning was generative AI (37%), fol­ lowed by personalized learning system (19.2%), intelligent tutoring system (17.5%), automatic formative assessment (13.5%), and writing assistant (12.8%). For the CFA dataset, the predominant AI tool used in learning was also generative AI (40.8%), with personalized learning system (19%), intelligent tutoring system (14.1%), auto­ matic formative assessment (13.7%), and writing assistant (12.4%) in usage. It is noteworthy that participants were reminded during the survey to specifically refer to their interaction with the AI tools they used most frequently, which ensured that their subsequent responses were directly related to these tools mentioned. 2.3  Factor structure identification by the EFA and CFA

In order to identify the factor structure of the Perceived Interactivity of Learner- AI Interaction Scale, both the EFA and CFA were conducted with Mplus Version 8.3. The EFA was performed to reveal the factor structure of the scale and identify which items were clustered within a common factor. The purpose of the analysis is to provide construct validity of the scale and to find a small number of conceptually meaningful variables (i.e., factors or dimensions) by gathering interrelated variables (Field, 2018). In the EFA, Maximum Likelihood Robust was employed as the param­ eter estimator to adjust for the asymmetric distribution of a few items in the dataset with skewness higher than |1| and kurtosis higher than |2| (Noar, 2003). We used the oblique GEOMIN rotation allowing for correlations between factors (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). When determining the value of factor loading (i.e., the correlation of an item with its associated factor), considering that the factor loading of 0.4 and above is considered substantial (Floyd & Widaman, 1995), we retained the items with the factor loading of higher than 0.4 on one factor (Hair et al., 2010). We assessed the EFA model fit by comparing multiple model fit indices (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988). The suggested model is “good” when the fit values of the model fit indices are within the range: 0.00 < χ2/df < 3.00, 0.00 < RMSEA (i.e., the root mean square error of approximation) < 0.05, 0.00 < SRMR (i.e., the standardized root mean square residual) < 0.05, 0.95 < TLI (i.e., the Tucker-Lewis Index, also known as the nonnormed fit index: NNFI) < 1.00, 0.95 < CFI (i.e., the comparative fit index) < 1.00, and “acceptable” within the range: 3.00 < χ2/df < 5.00, 0.05 < RMSEA < 0.10, 0.05 < SRMR < 0.10, 0.90 < TLI < 0.95, 0.90 < CFI < 0.95 (Yurdakul et al., 2012).

2.4  Scale refinement and validation

In the scale refinement, validity and reliability of the scale were assessed with the SPSS Version 26. The convergent and discriminant validity of the scale was assessed by examining composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) val­ ues of the scale (Hair et al., 2010). For convergent validity, the CR should be above 0.7, and the AVE should be above 0.5. For discriminant validity, the square root of each factor’s AVE should be greater than the factor’s correlation with other factors in the model and should be above 0.5 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). To evaluate the reliability of the scale, internal consistency tests include Cronbach’s alpha reliability tests. A reliability coefficient higher than 0.70 is considered “good”, and when it is close to 1.00, it is considered “very good” (Fraenkel et al., 2012).

After these, utility tests of the scale were performed to examine the relationship between the scale and the eight related variables. First, to compare mean perceived interactivity scores within the five documented categorical variables: genders, edu­ cation levels, fields of education, AI tools, and average duration of using AI each time, independent samples t-tests and ANOVAs were conducted. Second, to exam­ ine the relationship between mean perceived interactivity scores and the three docu­ mented ordinal and continuous variables: ages, months of using AI in learning, and behavioral intentions to use AI in learning, a correlation analysis was performed. Behavioral intentions to use AI in learning were measured by the adapted behavioral intention measures of using AI in learning by Liu and Ma (2023) and of using edu­ cational technology by Sun and Mei (2022). The six adapted items are presented in Table 3. Third, the strength of these relationships was assessed together with a mul­ tiple regression analysis. Results of the regression analysis reveal criterion validity of the scale, indicating the potential of using the scale as a predictive tool and providing insights into the discussion.

3.1  Results of the EFA

The EFA results supported a four-factor model. As Table 4 shows, all the model fit indices consistently showed that when the number of factors extracted increased from one to four, the model fit improved. One-factor and two-factor models had poor fits based on χ2/df > 5, RMSEA > 0.1, TLI < 0.9, CFI < 0.9, despite their SRMR < 0.1. The three-factor model showed a better fit for its SRMR was good (SRMR < 0.05) and χ2/df and RMSEA were acceptable (χ2/df < 5, RMSEA < 0.1), but its TLI and CFI were poor (TLI < 0.9, CFI < 0.9). However, the four-factor model showed the best fit for its χ2/df, SRMR, and CFI were good (χ2/df < 3, SRMR < 0.05, CFI > 0.95) and its RMSEA and TLI were acceptable (RMSEA > 0.5, TLI > 0.9).

The parallel analysis also showed a similar pattern, indicating that four factors should be extracted. As displayed in Fig. 2, the first four factors had eigenvalues higher than the average and 95th percentile eigenvalues generated from the parallel random data. To be specific, all the first four factors produced actual eigenvalues higher than one (Factor 1 = 7.607, Factor 2 = 1.765, Factor 3 = 1.387, Factor 4 = 1.263), which were greater than their respective average and 95th percentile eigenvalues, as shown in Table 5. Therefore, we decided that a four-factor model should be retained.

4  Discussion

Behavioral research investigating the influence of AI on learners’ behavioral devel­ opment and on their learning of new skills and competences is increasing rapidly (Benvenuti et al., 2023; Chai et al., 2021). A growing body of research indicates that learners who perceive higher levels of interactivity in learner-AI interaction are not only more likely to participate actively in intelligent teaching behaviors (Wang et al., 2023a) and become engaged in the learning process (Zhang et al., 2021), but also exhibit relationships between perceived interactivity and other related aspects, such as motivation, perceived enjoyment, divergent thinking, creativity, academic perfor­ mance, and well-being (Afanasyeva et al., 2024; Benvenuti et al., 2023; Wang & Cheung, 2024). Despite the importance, however, no scale for measuring perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction has been developed in the field of education. Moreover, the inconsistent usage of the definitions of interactivity and perceived interactivity has complicated the comparability of empirical studies in the emerging field (Petersen et al., 2022). To address this noteworthy topic, we developed, vali­ dated, and presented such a scale: the Perceived Interactivity of Learner-AI Interac­ tion Scale.

4.1  Development and validation of the Perceived Interactivity of Learner-AI Interaction Scale

Based on a literature review, student interviews, and expert opinions, four key dimen­ sions were first identified. The instrument was developed by assessing the content and construct validity of items in each dimension. The EFA on a development sam­ ple revealed the same four dimensions, which were subsequently validated through the CFA on another confirmatory sample. The final version of the scale, supported by evidence of reliability and validity, includes four dimensions with 17 items, as shown in Table 12. The “responsiveness” dimension covers items measuring learn­ ers’ perceptions of AI’s responses in terms of speed, appropriateness, and relevance. The “learner control” dimension includes items measuring learners’ perceptions of their control over the learning content, pace, and experiences. The “learning engage­

This study provides a new instrument for the field of education, specifically devel­ oped to measure learners’ perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction using com­ prehensive dimensions grounded in the Engagement Control Model, which sets the instrument apart from other relevant studies from three aspects. First, it is an instru­ ment developed to measure learners’ perceived interactivity in learner-AI interac­ tion in the field of education. Previous studies have developed people’s perceived interactivity, but many were developed for other fields, such as computer science (e.g., Borsci et al., 2021) and business (e.g., Gao et al., 2010). Second, this instru­ ment, grounded in clearly defined distinctions between interactivity and perceived interactivity from the literature, clarifies the currently inconsistent usage of these concepts and provides a basis for comparability in future empirical studies (Girish et al., 2022). Third, the instrument has been validated to include comprehensive dimen­ sions grounded in the Engagement Control Model. Some studies have mainly used several items to evaluate separate dimensions of system interactivity (e.g., Sun & Hsu, 2013), but this instrument extends these studies by incorporating all potential dimensions related to the topic, as indicated in the Engagement Control Model.

4.2  Utility of the scale

To examine the utility of the scale and further understand perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction, we tested and applied the scale across eight variables as indicated in literature: ages, genders, education levels, fields of education, AI tools, average duration of using AI each time, months of using AI in learning, and behav­ ioral intentions to use. Results indicated that perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction was associated with four of the variables: AI tools, average duration of using AI each time, months of using AI in learning, and behavioral intentions to use.

Specifically, regarding AI tools, perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction scored the highest with intelligent tutoring systems, followed by personalized learn­ ing systems, generative AI, writing assistant, and automatic formative assessment. The results are in line with many previous studies. One reason why intelligent tutor­ ing systems produced the highest score may be explained by the interaction granu­ larity hypothesis, suggesting that the fine granularity (i.e., grain size) of intelligent tutoring systems reduces reasoning difficulties for learners in learning and enhances the scaffolding provided by the systems during learning (Liu et al., 2024). In other words, intelligent tutoring systems provide learners with many interaction opportuni­ ties during learning by providing detailed learning steps, substeps, and even answers (Du Boulay, 2016). Compared to intelligent tutoring systems, personalized learning systems (e.g., self-developed system for programming) and generative AI need more learner-initiated interaction such as phrasing meaningful questions (Gao et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2020), which may influence the interactive process of personalized learn­ ing. Automatic formative assessment and writing assistant need less learner-initiated interaction for directly providing contextualized and generic feedback on specific issues (Zhu et al., 2020). Another reason may be related to the ICAP (Interactive, Constructive, Active, and Passive) framework (Chi, 2009; Gao et al., 2024). This framework suggests that interactive activities are likely to be better than construc­ tive activities, which in turn may outperform active activities, with passive activities being the last. The explanation predicts that learners working with more interactive systems (e.g., intelligent tutoring systems, personalized learning systems, and gen­ erative AI) are more likely to exhibit interactive behaviors, while those using less interactive systems (e.g., automatic formative assessment and writing assistant) may exhibit guessing and imitation, reflecting active or passive learning behaviors.

As for the duration of using AI in learning, both related variables, including aver­ age duration of using AI each time and months of using AI in learning, produced significant results. To be specific, perceived interactivity scores when lasting for “less than one hour” were found to be significantly lower than two other categories: “between one and two hours” and “more than two hours”. Months of using AI in learning also produced significant results, suggesting that increased months of using AI in learning were associated with higher perceived interactivity scores. The results align with previous research to indicate that the longer duration of interacting with interactive technology is related to higher motivation and more perceived interactiv­ ity (Barut Tugtekin & Dursun, 2022). However, there might be boundary conditions that involve characteristics of learners and learning contexts (Liu & Shrum, 2002; Wang & Cheung, 2024). Given that the numbers of participants in the two categories

of “between one and two hours” and “more than two hours” are much lower than “less than one hour”, the findings need more evidence from future research.

In terms of behavioral intentions to use, it was significantly associated with per­ ceived interactivity of learner-AI intention. This indicates that learners who scored higher on the Perceived Interactivity of Learner-AI Interaction Scale showed stron­ ger intentions to use AI in learning. The results align with the findings of existing research that learners interacting with virtual reality in active-interactivity learning environments have enriched learning experience and enhanced motivation, compared to those in passive-interactivity environments (Roussou & Slater, 2020), thereby showing stronger behavioral intentions to use (Barrett et al., 2021). Similarly, another study by Kim and Kwon (2024) shows that increased interactivity is associated with higher behavioral intentions to use among learners to interact with AI.

Not all the variables we tested showed a significant association with perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction. Results did not show significant associations with perceived interactivity scores across ages, genders, education levels, and fields of education. We offer the findings as supplementary evidence of our scale’s validity and utility. Specifically, our findings support that perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction is not constrained by ages, genders, education levels, and fields of educa­ tion. Perhaps this is because people generally have the ability to perceive interactivity in human-AI interaction (Sundar, 2020). Nevertheless, due to the limited numbers of participants in some categories (e.g., master’s degree, doctor’s degree, and the field of medicine), more research on the categories is expected in the future.

5  Limitations

While this study employed a systematic and step-by-step approach in developing and validating the scale, the following limitations should be acknowledged since they pave the way for future research. First, the participants were all university students, thus representative only of this sample scope. Although university students constitute a majority of learners utilizing AI in learning (Zhai et al., 2021), it is expected that data from diverse education levels (e.g., K-12 students) or learners pursuing profes­ sional development after graduation will further validate the findings of this study. Second, although the scale has been validated with a large sample size, learner distri­ bution was not balanced as expected. Some categories were under-represented, such as the age above 26, master’s degree, doctor’s degree, the education fields of science and medicine, and the average duration of using AI lasting for 1–2 h and more than two hours. Third, while the item generation step incorporated a diverse group of par­ ticipants from China, including undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral students, the sample during the data collection was mainly composed of undergraduate students from China. Therefore, it is suggested that future research may recruit participant groups in similar ratios or even from other countries or cultures to generalize the results.

6  Theoretical and practical implications

This study provides insights into both theoretical and practical implications. Theo­ retically, the framework of perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction that has been conceptualized in this study may be utilized to compare learners’ perceived interactivity when using specific AI tools in terms of the four dimensions: respon­ siveness, personalization, learning engagement, and learner control. This scale pro­ vides an evaluation framework for performing comparative analyses across various AI tools in the four dimensions. Moreover, the scale may be adopted or adapted for research within specific contexts. In line with the literature (Memarian & Doleck, 2024; Petersen et al., 2022; Wang & Cheung, 2024), researchers may use the scale to extend scholarly investigation into relationships concerning the aspects closely related to learners’ use of AI, such as motivated learning behaviors, trust, attitudes, self-efficacy, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, subsequent performance, and collaborative learning. Additionally, future research may use this scale to exam­ ine and develop hypotheses and theories on boundary conditions on perceived inter­ activity that may involve characteristics of learners and learning contexts (Liu & Shrum, 2002; Wang & Cheung, 2024), thereby deepening understandings regarding more effective ways of using AI in education. Finally, learners’ perceived interactiv­ ity of learner-AI interaction might change over time as they accumulate experience of using AI in learning, so longitudinal studies are recommended to examine changes in perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction and learning behaviors in different periods.

Practically, it is crucial to employ a multifaceted approach to analyzing perceived interactivity of human-AI interaction from the perspectives of AI practitioners, teach­ ers, and institutional stakeholders. For AI practitioners, it is important to understand the four different dimensions of perceived interactivity when learners use various AI tools. Instead of focusing solely on the capabilities of AI tools, AI practitioners may use the scale to identify learners who do not perceive sufficient interactivity in one or multiple dimensions, and design the human-computer interface to address the gaps. For teachers, understanding students’ interactions with AI and learning behaviors is necessary because teachers may adjust teaching strategies to leverage or mitigate the technological environment. Through the adjusted teaching strategies, students will get more assistance from teachers and be better prepared for the integration of AI into learning. For institutional stakeholders, the scale may be used for evaluating and choosing appropriate AI tools for specific learning objectives, and assessing students’ learning experiences taken over a period of time. AI tools for learning are expected to expand in the future. This scale, specifically developed for the context of learners’ use of AI, could reveal patterns of the learning process for institutional stakeholders’ considerations to build constructive interactive learning environments.

7  Conclusion

This study is the first attempt to develop and validate the Perceived Interactivity of Learner-AI Interaction Scale to measure how learners perceive interactivity with AI. The study contributes to the existing literature by conceptualizing a framework of perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction consisting of four dimensions, by developing a generic 17-item instrument for measuring perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction, and by proposing potential applications of this instrument.

First, grounded in the Engagement Control Model, the study has conceptualized a framework of perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction, including four dimen­ sions: responsiveness, personalization, learner control, and learning engagement. The framework is based on the clearly defined concepts of interactivity and per­ ceived interactivity, which were inconsistently defined and used in empirical studies (Petersen et al., 2022). The development of the scale pushes theories related to per­ ceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction forward.

Second, the study has developed a generic 17-item instrument for measuring per­ ceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction. The 17 items were developed follow­ ing a rigorous scale refinement and validation process. Regarding validity, both the EFA and the CFA endorsed the instrument’s validity, showing satisfactory results for each type of analysis. Regarding reliability, all four dimensions showed satisfactory psychometric properties, with four Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.820 to 0.915 and an overall Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.948. It can be concluded that this scale is a valid and reliable instrument to measure learners’ perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction.

Third, this scale has many potential applications. In our initial applications, per­ ceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction was significantly associated with learn­ ers’ behavioral intentions to use AI in learning, months of using AI in learning, and average duration of using AI each time. Moreover, learners have demonstrated signif­ icant differences in their perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction when using various AI tools, with intelligent tutoring systems scoring the highest, followed by personalized learning systems, generative AI, writing assistant, and automatic for­ mative assessment. Additionally, this study did not find that perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction was significantly associated with ages, genders, education levels, and fields of education. Future research may also consider exploring boundary conditions for perceived interactivity of learner-AI interaction related to characteris­ tics of learners and learning contexts.

The findings of this study can be applied by scholars and educators in develop­ ing and testing theories that explain and predict learners’ behaviors in using AI in learning, and by AI practitioners, teachers, and institutional stakeholders in apply­ ing AI tools in education. We hope this study will provide information on perceived interactivity, which is an important component of learning quality when learners use AI in learning, for scholars, educators, AI practitioners, teachers, and institutional stakeholders who consider the quality of education in human-AI interaction.
