Comparing AI-Generated Feedback Modes in EFL Speaking: The Role of Interactivity and Embedded Teacher Expertise

Abstract With the integration of generative artificial intelligence into EFL speaking instruction, AI-generated feedback (AIGF) has diversified in terms of interactivity and the extent of embedded teacher expertise. Although the pedagogical value of AIGF is well established, limited research has examined how these two dimensions jointly shape learners’ speaking development and willingness to communicate (WTC). Grounded in the Interaction Hypothesis, this quasi-experimental mixed-methods study investigated the effects of three AIGF modes differing in interactivity and embedded teacher expertise over a fourweek intervention. Sixty-two Chinese university students were assigned to one of three feedback modes: (1) EAP Talk, a low-interactivity, assessment-oriented system integrating teacher-informed feedback through structured scoring and diag­ nostic guidance; (2) Doubao, a high-interactivity, open-ended dialogic system emphasising learner-initiated expression and meaning negotiation; and (3) Doubao Agent, a moderate-interactivity, pedagogically guided dialogic system balancing instructional scaffolding with conversational flexibility. Preand post-tests, together with measures of WTC and technol­ ogy acceptance, showed that EAP Talk was particularly effective in improving grammatical accuracy and in-class WTC. Weekly structured reflective responses further suggested EAP Talk supported a feedback–revision–repractice pattern, char­ acterised by problem noticing, output revision, and repeated practice. In contrast, Doubao supported idea generation and fluency in a low-pressure dialogic environment, while Doubao Agent demonstrated a partial balance between form-focused guidance and interactional flexibility. Overall, the findings suggest that interactivity alone does not determine effective­ ness; rather, learning outcomes depend more critically on the alignment between feedback design, pedagogical structuring, and embedded teacher expertise.

Closely integrated with speech analysis technologies and large language models (LLMs), generative artificial intel­ ligence (GenAI) has become increasingly embedded in EFL speaking contexts, particularly through automated speak­ ing assessment and feedback (Du & Daniel, 2024; Wang & Guo, 2026; Wang et al., 2024). Contemporary GenAI sys­ tems can analyse learners’ spoken performance across mul­ tiple dimensions, including grammar, vocabulary, fluency, pronunciation, and content organisation, and provide imme­ diate feedback to support oral practice (Du & Daniel, 2024; Wang et al., 2024). As these technologies are adopted in both classroom and self-directed learning, the research focus has shifted from whether AI can support speaking practice to how different forms of AI-generated feedback (AIGF) can more effectively enhance learners’ speaking performance. As Li (2013) notes, feedback is a core component of foreign language learning due to its strong pedagogical impact.

Literature Review

AI-Assisted EFL Speaking

With advances in speech recognition, speech synthesis, and automated assessment, AI-supported speaking evalua­ tion systems have become widely used in EFL classrooms to support practice, scoring, and feedback access (Du & Daniel, 2024; Du & Reynolds, 2025; Huang et al., 2026). More recently, LLM-based GenAI has expanded speaking feedback by enabling score explanations, visualised error marking, and follow-up learning suggestions (Huang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024, 2026). Current GenAI-based speaking tools broadly fall into two types: assessmentoriented speech evaluation systems (e.g., Liulishuo IELTS, EAP Talk) and dialogue-based GenAI chatbots (e.g., Dou­ bao, CallAnnie), which differ in feedback format and inter­ actional mechanisms.

In AI-mediated speaking contexts, feedback is deliv­ ered through diverse formats rather than a single model. Assessment-oriented systems emphasise structured scoring and diagnostic feedback, targeting precise measurement of linguistic form and performance dimensions (Wang et al., 2024; Zou et al. 2025a). In contrast, GenAI chatbots pro­ vide feedback through dialogic interaction, offering realtime responses that foreground meaning construction and communicative processes (Du & Daniel, 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Wang & Xue, 2024). These feedback modes dif­ fer in interactional structure and learner agency, ranging from low to high interactivity, and may therefore differen­ tially influence speaking development and affective experi­ ence. However, existing research has rarely examined how such differences in interactivity interact with the degree of embedded teacher expertise in shaping learning outcomes.

Assessment-oriented systems typically draw on estab­ lished speaking assessment frameworks and evaluate dimensions such as grammar, pronunciation, and fluency. Their feedback is usually score-based and highly structured (e.g., scores, annotations, revision suggestions), reflecting teacher-derived criteria and pedagogical routines that sup­ port stability and consistency (Doughty & Long, 2005; Du & Daniel, 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Evidence suggests such systems can promote self-monitoring and repeated practice, aiding speaking development (Huang & Zou, 2024; Zou et al., 2025a); quantified indicators may also help learners track progress and build motivation and confidence (Liu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). Nevertheless, their largely one-way delivery and limited interactivity may constrain contextual meaning-making, sustained interaction, and deeper expression, and effectiveness may vary across cul­ tural and instructional contexts (Linh et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2025a). By contrast, GenAI chatbots generate contextualised feedback through dialogue (Du & Reynolds, 2025). They prioritise interactivity and adaptability, adjusting responses to learners’ input and simulating more authentic communi­ cation (Du & Daniel, 2024; Du & Reynolds, 2025). Studies suggest dialogic feedback can support idea development, content organisation, and discourse coherence, while eas­ ing speaking anxiety (Carless, 2016; Wang et al., 2024). These patterns can be interpreted through the Interaction Hypothesis (Long, 1983). In GenAI-mediated speaking, feedback serves as comprehensible input that helps learners notice gaps and modify output through revision, repeated practice, and clarification seeking, thereby supporting an input–output–reinput cycle. Such interactional processes may include clarification requests, confirmation checks, and modified output, which are central to interactionist accounts of second language development. In the present study, inter­ activity is conceptualised as the extent to which learners engage in iterative, multi-turn exchanges with AI systems, including opportunities for learner control, responsiveness of feedback, and negotiation of meaning. Moreover, inter­ activity is not treated as an isolated variable, but as part of a broader feedback design that also involves pedagogi­ cal structuring and the extent to which teacher expertise is embedded in an AI system.

Despite these affordances, most studies emphasise per­ ceptions or overall effectiveness, with limited empirical comparison of how AIGF modes with different levels of coconstruction and interactivity shape speaking development and affective experience. Moreover, interactivity is often treated descriptively rather than analytically, with limited attention to how it interacts with pedagogical design and assessment alignment in shaping learning experience and outcomes.

Willingness to Communicate (WTC)

WTC refers to learners’ momentary inclination to use the target language with specific interlocutors in a given context (MacIntyre et al., 1998). Rather than a stable trait, WTC is now widely viewed as dynamic and context-sensitive, shaped by affect, perceived competence, interlocutor fea­ tures, and interactional environments (Peng, 2019; Peng & Liang, 2025). Higher WTC increases opportunities for authentic language use, supporting exposure to comprehen­ sible input and chances for output, both central to L2 devel­ opment (Fathi et al. 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Sustained engagement may also foster learners’ tolerance and regula­ tion of negative affect, such as speaking anxiety (Fathi et al., 2024; Teimouri, 2017; Wang et al., 2024). In this sense, WTC functions as a key psychological condition that acti­ vates interaction and sustains the input–output cycle high­ lighted in the Interaction Hypothesis (Long, 1983).

Recent work further stresses the interlocutor-specific nature of WTC: learners’ willingness can vary markedly depending on whom they communicate with (Cao & Philp, 2006; Lee & Hsieh, 2019). In classrooms, WTC may dif­ fer across peers and teachers (Lee & Hsieh, 2019). Peer

With AI increasingly embedded in language learning, WTC research has expanded to WTC with AI (WTC-AI), defined as learners’ propensity to interact with AI tools in the L2 for varied purposes (Peng & Liang, 2025, p. 6). Evi­ dence suggests AI can reduce social-evaluative pressure and provide lower-risk speaking environments, thereby support­ ing engagement (Huang & Zou, 2024; Wang et al., 2024). However, WTC-AI does not simply replace WTC with teachers or classmates; it is strongly shaped by feedback format, interaction depth, and the presence of pedagogi­ cally oriented guidance (Peng & Liang, 2025). Structured, instructionally framed systems may be perceived as quasiteachers, eliciting WTC patterns closer to teacher–student interaction, whereas open-ended conversational systems may resemble peer interaction or self-directed expression. Accordingly, different AIGF modes may shape in-class WTC and WTC-AI differentially by reconfiguring inter­ locutor roles and engagement, making it necessary to exam­ ine how these mechanisms jointly influence communicative willingness and speaking development in AI-mediated contexts.

Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)

Although WTC-AI captures learners’ affective readiness to engage in AI-mediated speaking practice (Peng & Liang, 2025), sustained use of AI feedback systems also depends on perceived usefulness and ease of use (Huang & Zou, 2024; Zou et al., 2025a). Davis’s (1989) TAM identifies per­ ceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEU) as key predictors of users’ attitudes and behavioural intention (BI) to adopt a technology. PU concerns whether the system is judged to enhance learning performance, whereas PEU reflects the effort required to use it (Davis, 1989).

Previous studies have widely applied TAM to examine learners’ acceptance of AI-supported speaking systems, showing that tools perceived as useful and easy to use are more likely to generate positive attitudes and sustained use (Liu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026; Zou et al., 2025c). How­ ever, existing research has largely focused on measuring overall acceptance, with limited attention to how differences in AIGF interaction modes shape learners’ perceptions. In the present study, the three feedback modes differ in inter­ actional structure and instructional design, which may shape learners’ perception of the technology. Methodology

Participants

A total of 62 undergraduate EFL learners were recruited via online and on-site sampling from several universities in southeastern China. They were randomly assigned to EAP Talk group (n = 22), Doubao group (n = 20), and Doubao Agent group (n = 20). The sample included 15 males and 47 females (M age = 20.88, SD = 1.60). Twenty-five partici­ pants were from STEM and 37 from humanities and social sciences, with disciplinary backgrounds balanced across groups to minimise confounding. Most learners were at A2–B1 CEFR and reported over 10 years of formal Eng­ lish study, suggesting a stable basis for oral practice. Writ­ ten informed consent was obtained, and ethics approval was granted by the authors’ university ethics committee.

Instruments

AI-Generated Feedback Systems

The following three AIGF systems were selected as rep­ resentative cases of different feedback architectures. They differed along two theoretically relevant dimensions: the degree of interactivity and the extent to which teacher expertise was embedded in the feedback design.

EAP Talk (www.eaptalk.com) provides a low-interac­ tivity, teacher-informed form of AIGF through a hybrid feedback mechanism. This mechanism draws on a curated dataset of feedback practices from approximately 30 expe­ rienced English-speaking teachers, including feedback

As shown in Fig. 1, the system delivers scores and feed­ back across IELTS speaking criteria, including Fluency and Coherence (FC), Grammatical Accuracy (GA), Vocabulary Range (VR), and Pronunciation (PR). After reviewing the scores, learners can expand each dimension by clicking the corresponding arrow to access more detailed feedback. Compared with feedback generated by untrained LLMs alone, this hybrid feedback offers richer corrective input, more concrete revision suggestions, and illustrative exam­ ples, thereby enabling learners to better identify the sources of their speaking problems and to engage in more targeted oral improvement. In addition, the system demonstrated a high level of scoring accuracy, with an accuracy rate of around 90%, as confirmed through evaluation and calibra­ tion by two former IELTS examiners and three university English-speaking course instructors, each with more than ten years of teaching experience.

The moderateand high-interactivity dialogic AIGF con­ ditions were both implemented via the Doubao platform, ensuring a consistent underlying technological environ­ ment, but differed in interactional control and instructional mediation, corresponding to the Doubao group (learnerconstructed, high interactivity) and the Doubao Agent group (pedagogically guided, moderate interactivity). The Doubao group adopted a highly learner-driven free-dialogue mode. As an LLM-based GenAI chatbot comparable to systems such as ChatGPT, Doubao supports text and voice input and provides responses in text and synthetic speech (see Fig. 2). Learners engaged in open-ended, multi-turn interactions under weekly themes and were required to actively elicit feedback through follow-up questions. This mode priori­ tised learner autonomy in topic development, feedback ini­ tiation, and meaning negotiation.

In contrast, the Doubao Agent represented a more ped­ agogically guided dialogic feedback mode. Built on the same platform, it incorporated pre-set interactional topics and a feedback framework aligned with IELTS speaking criteria (see Fig. 2). Its feedback design was informed by the structured, criterion-referenced approach used in EAP Talk, particularly in terms of diagnostic focus and evalua­ tive logic, while retaining dialogic interaction. Preand Post-questionnaire

Preand Post-tests

The pre-test questionnaire collected participants’ demo­ graphic and background information, as well as measures of in-class WTC (adapted from Lee & Hsieh, 2019) and WTC- AI (adapted from Huang & Zou, 2024). The post-test ques­ tionnaire followed the same structure to enable comparison and additionally included PU, PEU, and BI, adapted from Zou et al. (2025a). All items were rated on a five-point Lik­ ert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree).

Participants completed an IELTS-style oral speaking task before and after the intervention. In both tests, learners responded to three speaking questions administered in the Test mode of EAP Talk. The preand post-test tasks were comparable in difficulty, structure, and closely related topics to minimise task effects. All responses were audiorecorded and automatically scored by EAP Talk using analytic band descriptors aligned with IELTS criteria. The system’s teacher management function stored all recordings and scores. To ensure scoring reliability, two trained IELTS speaking instructors independently reviewed all recordings

Weekly Structured Reflective Responses

At the end of each week, participants completed a set of structured reflective responses via an online questionnaire comprising four open-ended questions. The prompts elic­ ited learners’ perceptions of AIGF, including its perceived strengths and limitations for speaking practice, its contri­ butions to speaking development, newly acquired knowled­ gea, and its perceived effects on WTC.

Research Procedures

This study employed a six-week quasi-experimental design conducted immediately after the completion of a regu­ lar academic term, at the beginning of the summer vaca­ tion, during which participants received no other English instruction. In Week 1, participants were informed of the study aims and procedures, provided written informed con­ sent (see Fig. 3), and completed a pre-test questionnaire and speaking test. They were then introduced to the assigned platform and its functions. From Weeks 2 to 5, participants

engaged in a four-week GenAI-supported speaking inter­ vention using their assigned platform. All groups followed an identical practice schedule, completing three to four speaking sessions per week, each lasting 10–15 min. Prac­ tice tasks were uniformly designed by the research team and increased weekly in complexity, covering university life, work modes, language learning strategies, and innova­ tion. Apart from differences in feedback interaction modes, all instructional conditions were held constant. Participant engagement was monitored throughout the intervention via weekly reminders and participation checks in online chat­ ting groups. At the end of each week, participants completed an online open-ended questionnaire to reflect on their learn­ ing experiences. In Week 6, all participants completed a post-speaking test and post-test questionnaire.

Data Analysis

For the quantitative data, speaking performance and WTC were treated as pedagogical outcomes, while PU, PEU, and BI were analysed as evaluative outcomes. Shapiro–Wilk

tests showed that speaking performance and TAM-related variables violated normality assumptions, whereas in-class WTC and WTC-AI approximated normal distributions. Accordingly, Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for speaking performance, and one-way ANOVA was used for WTC measures to examine baseline equivalence across groups. The results showed no significant pre-test differences across groups (all ps > .05), indicating that the three groups were broadly comparable before the intervention. Intervention effects were then analysed using gain scores (Post–Pre). One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests was applied to in-class WTC and WTC-AI, with η2 and Cohen’s d reported as effect sizes. For speaking performance and TAM-related gains, Kruskal–Wallis tests were conducted, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn post-hoc tests where appropriate, with ε2 and Cliff’s δ reported as effect sizes.

Weekly structured reflective responses were analysed thematically following a hybrid approach (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Deductively, the analysis was guided by the study’s research focus on learners’ perceived interaction experi­ ences, WTC, and language development, with affective responses used as supporting evidence for interpreting sustained engagement with different feedback modes. Inductively, codes were generated from participants’ own language and recurring expressions, such as “score compar­ ison,” “practising until satisfied,” and “like chatting with a friend,” without imposing predetermined categories. These codes were then grouped into candidate themes by identi­ fying shared patterns within and across the three feedback

conditions. For example, codes related to score monitoring, error noticing, and repeated output modification were clus­ tered together, while codes describing enjoyment, anxiety, and sustained engagement were grouped separately. The­ matic boundaries were reviewed iteratively across all four weeks of data, and alternative configurations were consid­ ered before the final categories were agreed upon. To ensure reliability, two independent coders analysed the data sepa­ rately using Word and Excel; discrepancies were resolved through discussion, yielding an inter-coder agreement above 90%. The resulting coding framework, together with representative quotations, is presented in the Appendix.

Finally, learners across all groups demonstrated critical engagement with AIGF, though their perceived limitations and response strategies varied. In the EAP Talk group, learn­ ers identified limitations in speech recognition, grammatical judgement, and pragmatic appropriateness, but they selec­ tively adopted feedback rather than accepting it uncritically. Learners frequently referred to acquiring explicit organisational patterns and discourse markers, such as “starting with ‘Firstly’ and ending with ‘In conclusion’”, or consciously using connectors like “therefore” in subsequent practice. Learners frequently described how the Agent helped transform vague ideas into more precise, logical, and professionally framed responses, particularly when engaging with abstract or academic top­ ics. One learner explained that “Doubao helps me turn a vague idea into something more specific and logical,” while another noted, “When discussing abstract topics, I feel my speech is now more structured.”

Discussion

AIGF Modes and Speaking Performance

The quantitative results are broadly consistent with previous studies on chatbot-based and AI-driven speaking feedback, which show that AIGF can effectively support EFL learn­ ers’ speaking development across feedback formats (Wang & Xue, 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). How­ ever, the significantly larger gains in grammatical accuracy observed in the EAP Talk group indicate that differential effects across AIGF modes are primarily concentrated in form-oriented dimensions, echoing findings by Fathi et al. (2024) and Zou et al. (2025b) that structured evaluative feed­ back yields stronger form-focused gains. By contrast, feedback in the Doubao group was embed­ ded in ongoing dialogue. Although learners did self-regu­ late, revisions were often implicit or delayed and guided by conversational flow rather than explicit evaluation. While this supported idea generation and expressive fluency, the absence of stable evaluative reference points limited sys­ tematic form-focused practice, consistent with Carless’s (2016) observation that dialogic feedback without clear criteria may weaken feedback closure. The Doubao Agent group showed an intermediate pattern: explicit corrections promoted attention to form, but frequent feedback and revi­ sion demands sometimes disrupted engagement, reflecting the tension between dialogic flow and feedback closure noted by Carless (2016).

AIGF Modes and WTC

The quantitative findings show that the EAP Talk group achieved significantly greater gains in in-class WTC than the dialogic feedback groups, whereas no significant between-group differences were found for WTC-AI. This pattern suggests that different AIGF modes exert differ­ entiated effects on learners’ readiness for classroom com­ munication, while their influence on willingness to interact with AI is largely comparable. In doing so, the present study extends prior research that has mainly focused on WTC-AI, with limited attention to interlocutor-specific variation in WTC (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024).

From the perspective of the Interaction Hypothesis, EAP Talk, designed around teachers’ feedback practices, pro­ vided clear instructional signals that appeared to facilitate learners’ noticing of linguistic gaps and repeated attempts at modified output, even without sustained dialogue. Quali­ tative evidence suggested a perceived feedback–revision– repractice pattern, in which learners reported noticing system-flagged errors, revising their output, and repeat­ ing practice until scores improved, reflecting perceived regulatory behaviour that resembles the notice–uptake– modified output process in interactionist L2 development (Long, 1983). In contrast, although Doubao’s multi-turn dialogue offered opportunities for clarification, reformula­ tion, and idea expansion (Du & Reynolds, 2025; Wang et al., 2024), these exchanges were mainly meaning-oriented and lacked stable evaluative signals, which may have lim­ ited clear uptake and form-focused repair (Carless, 2016). The Doubao Agent generated more explicit correction and visible repair opportunities, but excessive feedback some­ times overwhelmed learners and disrupted conversational flow. Similar issues were reported by Zou et al. (2025c), where feedback overload, recognition errors, and correction pacing reduced learners’ enthusiasm for sustained speak­ ing practice. This suggests that, in oral language learning, effective AIGF should support repair without interrupting fluency, meaning negotiation, or learners’ willingness to continue speaking.

The qualitative data indicate that EAP Talk enabled learn­ ers to link speaking confidence directly to visible progress achieved through AI practice. Its clear scoring criteria and exam-aligned feedback helped learners concretely monitor performance improvement, thereby strengthening perceived communicative competence, a key antecedent of in-class WTC (MacIntyre et al., 2002). In contrast, learners using Doubao described the interaction as safe and companion­ able, often likening it to “chatting with a friend.” Consistent with Huang and Zou (2024), this low-pressure environ­ ment reduced anxiety and supported sustained engagement with AI, contributing to increased WTC-AI. However, the absence of explicit evaluative benchmarks and alignment with classroom performance standards made it difficult for learners to judge their readiness for classroom interaction. As a result, gains from AI-mediated dialogue did not con­ sistently transfer to in-class WTC, echoing Carless’s (2016) observation that dialogic feedback may remain open-ended without clear evaluative guidance.

Learners in the Doubao Agent group exhibited more dynamic affective patterns. While pedagogically framed topics and explicit correction supported goal-directed learn­ ing, frequent correction and repeated reformulation imposed cognitive demands and occasionally triggered frustration. This affective fluctuation may have weakened the transfer from AI practice to classroom communication, a pattern also reported in prior research on ChatGPT-supported speaking practice (Zou et al., 2025c).

More importantly, the present findings suggest that inter­ actional opportunities alone may be insufficient to trigger key interactionist processes, such as effective noticing and modified output, in AI-mediated contexts. Conclusions, Limitations and Implications

This study demonstrates the pedagogical value of different AIGF modes. Notably, EAP Talk, which embeds teachers’ feedback expertise and routines, produced clear advantages in grammatical accuracy and in-class WTC. Through diag­ nostic scoring, visualised feedback, and structured analysis, it appeared to support learners’ perceived score-oriented regulatory behaviours, including problem noticing, revi­ sion, and repeated practice. By contrast, Doubao offered a low-pressure, dialogue-driven environment that supported idea generation and expressive fluency but provided lim­ ited evaluative benchmarks. The Doubao Agent occupied an intermediate position, combining pedagogical guidance with conversational flexibility while revealing tensions among feedback intensity, cognitive load, and interactional flow. Overall, AIGF effectiveness depends not simply on interactivity, but on how feedback structure, pacing, and pedagogical alignment support learners’ specific goals.

TAM Across AIGF Modes

Post-test TAM results showed generally high levels of PU, PEU, and BI across all three AIGF modes. No significant group differences were found for PU or PEU, whereas BI approached significance (p = 0.05). Learners using EAP Talk (M = 4.34) and Doubao Agent (M = 4.16) reported slightly higher BI than those using Doubao (M = 3.86). These findings suggest that while all AIGF modes were broadly acceptable, systems with clearer pedagogical orientation and more structured feedback were more likely to foster sustained use intentions. This underscores the advantage of embedding teacher expertise in AIGF, where instructional alignment and assessment-informed design appear more influential than interactivity alone. Although this pattern has been mainly reported in GenAI-assisted writing contexts (Han & Li, 2024), the present findings indicate that similar principles apply to speaking feedback. Weekly structured reflective responses further showed that learners perceived EAP Talk as particularly useful for targeted improvement, exam preparation, and progress monitoring, helping explain its high PU despite relatively low interactivity. Its transpar­ ent scoring criteria and diagnostic feedback closely matched learners’ academic expectations, consistent with prior TAM- based studies on EAP Talk (Huang & Zou, 2024; Zou et al., 2025a). Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively modest sample size drawn from a single instruc­ tional context may limit the generalisability of the findings. Future research is encouraged to include larger and more diverse samples across institutions, proficiency levels, and learning settings to enhance external validity. Second, the short-term pre–post design did not capture the longer-term and dynamic effects of AIGF on speaking development and WTC, suggesting the need for longitudinal investigations. Third, although three representative AIGF modes were compared, specific feedback features were not systemati­ cally manipulated, which limits the ability to draw causal inferences regarding individual design elements. Fourth, the use of in-class WTC as an outcome measure should be interpreted with caution. As the intervention was conducted during the summer vacation rather than within an ongoing classroom setting, this measure reflects learners’ perceived readiness for future classroom communication rather than actual classroom participation. This study also yields important pedagogical implica­ tions. Rather than suggesting that one AIGF mode is uni­ versally superior, the findings highlight the importance of instructional fit between feedback architecture and learning goals. In particular, structured, teacher-informed feedback, as represented by EAP Talk, appears especially valuable when instructional goals involve grammatical accuracy, diagnostic feedback, assessment preparation, and classroom communication. Its clear criteria, multidimensional scor­ ing, and teacher-informed evaluative logic helped learners identify specific linguistic problems, monitor progress, and engage in more targeted practice. Such features are partic­ ularly important in EFL speaking contexts where learners often need explicit guidance, visible evidence of improve­ ment, and feedback aligned with assessment standards. Therefore, EAP Talk may be especially suitable for teachersupported classroom instruction, exam-oriented speaking practice, and contexts where learners require structured feedback to build confidence and classroom readiness.

At the same time, other AIGF architectures may serve different pedagogical purposes. Learner-driven open dia­ logue, as represented by Doubao, may be useful for fluency practice, idea generation, low-pressure communication, and learner autonomy. Pedagogically guided dialogue, as rep­ resented by Doubao Agent, may support tasks requiring both interaction and scaffolding, such as topic expansion, logical organisation, and guided reformulation. However, feedback intensity in such systems needs to be carefully managed so that correction and repair opportunities do not interrupt fluency, meaning negotiation, or learners’ willing­ ness to continue speaking. Overall, teachers should select or design AIGF tools according to task goals, learner needs, and classroom conditions, while embedding teachers’ eval­ uative judgement, instructional sensitivity, and accumulated feedback experience into AI systems to ensure pedagogi­ cally aligned and human-centred AI-supported language education.
