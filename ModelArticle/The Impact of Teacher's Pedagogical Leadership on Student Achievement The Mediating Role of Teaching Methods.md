**The Impact of Teacher's Pedagogical Leadership on Student Achievement: The Mediating Role of Teaching Methods**
<a name="_toc371077877"></a>**Abstract**
<a name="_hlk172042709"></a><a name="_hlk172043615"></a><a name="ole_link10"></a>**Purpose -** Educational accountability and student achievement polarization, which result in high dropout rates, pose significant challenges and pressures on teachers' pedagogical leadership. <a name="_hlk172229194"></a><a name="_hlk172228798"></a>Whether pedagogical leadership, which originates in the Western educational environment, can significantly improve student achievement in Chinese high schools, remains unclear. This concept has not yet been fully explored in the Chinese educational environment, and its direct impact on student achievement and the mediating role of English teaching methods remains to be investigated. However, existing research has concentrated on the effectiveness of principals' pedagogical leadership, with variations in teachers' pedagogical leadership practices. Many reform measures have been implemented in China to improve student achievement, but past educational practices have analyzed the impact on student achievement from a single instructional leadership, school capital, or teaching method perspective. Furthermore, there is a lack of multidimensional and systematic assessments of the direct effects of teacher pedagogical leadership on student achievement and the mediating effects of English teaching methods.
**Design/methodology/approach -** To address this gap, this study analyzed the impact of teachers' pedagogical leadership on student achievement and the mediating effect of English teaching methods with the support of the theory of action for teacher leadership, specifically using pedagogical leadership and English teaching methods models.This study conducted a questionnaire survey of 968 participants in Taian City, China, and quantitatively analyzed the data using SmartPLS structural equation modeling (SEM).
**Findings -** This study revealed that pedagogical leadership has a positive direct effect on student achievement. Meanwhile, among the four mediating factors, the Direct Method, Audio-Lingual Method, and Communicative Language Teaching had significant mediating effects.
**Originality/value** **-** This study shows that the effective use of academic and professional capital allocation in pedagogical leadership, combined with effective measures of using multiple effective English teaching methods, helps achieve high-quality student achievement.
**Keywords**: <a name="_hlk172136645"></a>Pedagogical Leadership, Professional Capital, English Teaching Method, Student Achievement, Teacher Leadership, School Community
**Introduction**
Today, the success of a school is evaluated based on assessable student achievement, and the high dropout rate due to the polarization of educational accountability and student achievement in China poses a great challenge and pressure on teachers (Huan et al., 2015; Loyalka et al., 2013; Xiaoyun & Rozelle, 2020). Previous research has demonstrated that pedagogical leadership is a critical contributor to the practice of effective teaching methods and is positively associated with student achievement (Gamarra-Mendoza & Brito-García, 2023; Grice et al., 2023; Male & Palaiologou, 2017; Palomino Fernández et al., 2023). <a name="_hlk172106649"></a>Various studies on educational leadership have identified pedagogical leadership as the second most influential factor in improving student achievement after classroom teaching (Ahmad, 2013; Palomino Fernández et al., 2023). Robinson et al. (2008) emphasized that pedagogical leadership has, on average, three to four times the impact on student achievement as transformational leadership. Unlike instructional leadership, pedagogical leadership emphasizes teaching methods and is practiced collaboratively by principals, school leaders, and teachers (Fonsén & Lahtero, 2024; Grice et al., 2023; Opazo et al., 2023).
Pedagogical leadership focuses on student learning and teacher empowerment (Chaucono Catrinao et al., 2022; Grice, 2019; Peng & Chudy, 2021). This leadership approach combines leadership and management functions to promote student achievement, and exerts both direct and indirect effects (Ärlestig & Törnsen, 2014; Chaucono Catrinao et al., 2022; Fonsén & Lahtero, 2024; Forssten Seiser, 2020). The direct effect of pedagogical leadership is related to classroom teaching methods, whereas its indirect effect is related to administrative and human resource management (Chaucono Catrinao et al., 2022; Fonsén & Lahtero, 2024; Peng & Chudy, 2021). <a name="ole_link5"></a>Specifically, pedagogical leadership of principals in educational institutions focuses on building social and intellectual capital, whereas pedagogical leadership of teachers includes academic capital related to classroom teaching and professional capital related to teaching methods (Fonsén & Lahtero, 2024; Grice, 2019; Sergiovanni, 1998; Varlık et al., 2021). Previous research has also demonstrated that teaching methods enhance professional capital and are crucial in predicting student achievement (Ärlestig & Törnsen, 2014; Grice, 2019; Varlık et al., 2021). Moreover, teaching methods mediate<a name="_hlk171929722"></a> the effects of professional capital on student achievement <a name="_hlk171929980"></a>(Alonso-Nuez et al., 2024; Cole & Duffy, 2024; Sergiovanni, 1998; Supovitz, 2001).
<a name="ole_link9"></a>Despite extensive research, significant gaps remain in the literature on pedagogical leadership (Berhanu, 2023; Bøe et al., 2022; Okiri & Hercz, 2023; Palomino Fernández et al., 2023). <a name="_hlk172277894"></a><a name="ole_link6"></a><a name="ole_link2"></a><a name="ole_link1"></a>First, pedagogical leadership, which originated in Western educational settings, has not yet been thoroughly explored in the Chinese educational context. There is a lack of research on the direct impact of pedagogical leadership on student achievement and the mediating role of English teaching methods in Chinese educational settings (Na et al., 2016; Shen et al., 2020; Shengnan & Hallinger, 2021). <a name="_hlk172042753"></a>While China has implemented numerous educational reforms to enhance student achievement, previous studies have examined the impact on student achievement from isolated perspectives,  such as leadership (e.g., instructional leadership), school capital (e.g., social or professional capital), and teaching methods (Hou et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2022; Na et al., 2016).
Second, within China's centralized education system, most research has concentrated on the instructional leadership and pedagogical leadership of principals or school leaders. Literature on teacher pedagogical leadership has paid little attention to how teachers demonstrate pedagogical leadership and its impact on student achievement through teaching, and there are differences in teachers' effective pedagogical leadership practices (Peng & Chudy, 2021; Varlık et al., 2021; Xin et al., 2019). For example, teachers constrained by power distance and hierarchical management structures may view pedagogical leadership as an additional task rather than empowerment, which can lead teaching stress and psychological pressure, ultimately limiting student achievement (Bush & Haiyan, 2000; Opazo et al., 2023; Xin et al., 2019). Low student achievement has raised concerns about teachers' pedagogical leadership roles and professionalism (Mo & Jing, 2022; Xin et al., 2019). In addition, educational institutions prioritize the results of teaching methods to improve student achievement, rather than focusing on whether teachers effectively practice pedagogical leadership (Fonsén et al., 2023; Qiao et al., 2017). Although past research has provided useful insights into underperforming schools, there is limited evidence that teachers' pedagogical leadership capital configurations support student achievement (Daly et al., 2014, 2020). <a name="_hlk172278830"></a><a name="_hlk172051350"></a>This practice optimizes teaching methods solely to improve student achievement, ignoring the fact that teaching methods are related to teachers' pedagogical leadership capital configurations (Daly et al., 2020; Fonsén & Lahtero, 2024; Varlık et al., 2021).
<a name="_hlk172551715"></a>Third, existing research lacks analyses that incorporate the effects of all four capital dimensions of pedagogical leadership on student achievement, and the mediating role of English teaching methods in a comprehensive research model (Alp Christ et al., 2022; Fullan et al., 2015; Gamarra-Mendoza & Brito-García, 2023). In particular, teaching methods have not been adequately researched as potential mediating mechanisms through which pedagogical leadership affects student achievement (Alonso-Nuez et al., 2024; Cole & Duffy, 2024; Sergiovanni, 1998; Supovitz, 2001). <a name="_hlk172536823"></a><a name="ole_link7"></a><a name="ole_link8"></a>Student underachievement is a complex issue closely linked to teachers' pedagogical leadership capital configurations. <a name="_hlk172370444"></a>To address this issue,it requires the integration of multiple factors, including pedagogical leadership and its capital, teaching methods, and expectations for improving student achievement (David, 2001; González-Fernández et al., 2021; Sergiovanni, 1998). <a name="_hlk172536927"></a>The integration of teachers' pedagogical leadership capital and teaching methods can be particularly effective when schools and districts attempt to develop teaching reform strategies to improve student <a name="_hlk171936659"></a>achievement (Daly et al., 2020; David, 2001; González-Fernández et al., 2021).
To address this gap, this study analyzed the impact of teachers' pedagogical leadership dimensions of social capital, intellectual capital, academic capital, and professional capital on student achievement, and the mediating effect of English teaching methods in the Chinese high school setting. The significance of this study lies in the proper configuration of teachers' pedagogical leadership capital, and the effective combination of multiple teaching methods that can support the achievement needs of diverse student groups.
**Literature review and hypothesis**
*The theory of action for teacher leadership*
<a name="ole_link12"></a>Several studies have emphasized the crucial role of teacher leadership in the teaching and learning community (Fonsén et al., 2023; Katzenmeyer & Moller, 2009; York-Barr & Duke, 2004). Leadership is defined as the ability to motivate, guide, and influence a school or its members to achieve common goals and achieve new and better accomplishments (Bush & Haiyan, 2000; Northouse, 2021; Qian et al., 2017). Teacher leadership is the process by which teachers play leadership roles inside and outside the classroom to influence colleagues, principals, and members of the school community; improve teaching practices; and promote successful student achievement (Gonzalez Fernandez et al., 2019; York-Barr & Duke, 2004). Teacher leadership focuses on creating school communities that enhance school improvement by fostering collaboration among schools and reducing the administrative burden on principals, empowered by pedagogical leadership in the areas of teaching and learning (Katzenmeyer & Moller, 2009; Llorent-Bedmar et al., 2021; Northouse, 2021).
Teacher leadership is a socially constructed concept that may vary across contexts, cultural backgrounds, and teaching practices (Qian et al., 2017). <a name="ole_link11"></a>In Chinese teachers' practiced leadership, pedagogical leadership and instructional leadership are used interchangeably. Although there are similarities between these two concepts, there are differences (Gamarra-Mendoza & Brito-García, 2023; Mo & Jing, 2022; Shengnan & Hallinger, 2021). This is because pedagogical leadership is encompassed within instructional leadership, which is seldom seen in isolation, but more often in terms of teaching methods (Elo & Uljens, 2024; Na et al., 2016).
Instructional leadership is defined as intentional actions and processes that guide and support teaching planning, delivery, and outcome analysis (Hallinger & Murphy, 2013; Moos, 2011). It encompasses the management and oversight of all aspects related to teaching and learning and is typically held by school leaders who possess both management and supervisory roles (Hallinger & Murphy, 2013; Liu et al., 2022; Qian et al., 2017). By contrast, pedagogical leadership emphasizes empowering teachers to lead and guide teaching methods and promote student achievement (Ahtiainen et al., 2021; Fonsén & Lahtero, 2024; Grice et al., 2023). <a name="_hlk172356751"></a>Some researchers have argued that pedagogical leadership emphasizes the teachers' role as a leader in improving teaching methods and strategies, which is critical for improving the quality of teaching and student achievement (Elo & Uljens, 2024; Grice et al., 2023; Palomino Fernández et al., 2023).
According to the theory of action for teacher leadership (York-Barr & Duke, 2004), pedagogical leadership affects student achievement through three key pathways. First, the traits, types of capital, and working conditions of pedagogical leadership are the basis for the realization of teacher pedagogical leadership. Pedagogical leadership is characterized by a learning orientation and leadership practices. <a name="_hlk172217480"></a>Second, how pedagogical leadership is practiced, its influence, and mediating changes in teaching method practices determine how pedagogical leadership affects student achievement. Teachers influence student achievement through the mediating pathway of implementing effective teaching methods, among other factors (Alonso-Nuez et al., 2024; Cole & Duffy, 2024; Elliott & Oliver, 2016; Sergiovanni, 1998). Third, this process ultimately leads to better student achievement (Elo & Uljens, 2024; Grice et al., 2023; Palomino Fernández et al., 2023).
*Teachers' Pedagogical leadership*
Pedagogical leadership is an emerging concept in the effective school movement proposed by Sergiovanni (1998), who described the four dimensions of pedagogical leadership that influence teaching methods and improve student achievement. Pedagogical leadership has various perspectives. First, pedagogical leadership is centered on student learning and teaching methods, which improve student achievement through teachers' teaching practices (Sergiovanni, 1998). The second perspective emphasizes the need for dynamic processes in pedagogical leadership implementation (Male & Palaiologou, 2015). The third perspective emphasizes that pedagogical leadership must prioritize the establishment of clear educational goals, curriculum planning, and teacher and teaching evaluation, including an assessment framework for pedagogical leadership in schools to align with relevant student-achievement-related external standards and expectations (Fonsén & Lahtero, 2024; Grice et al., 2023; Llorent-Bedmar et al., 2021).
The term pedagogical leadership is often used in research and policy in different contexts, with varying meanings attached (Fonsén & Lahtero, 2024; Gonzalez Fernandez et al., 2019; Grice et al., 2023; Leo, 2015). Pedagogical leadership is defined as leading or directing effective teaching and technological improvements, adapting teaching methods to the diverse contexts of students to improve student achievement, and creating learning communities and relationships (Bøe et al., 2022; Elo & Uljens, 2024; Male & Palaiologou, 2015). It emphasizes the mentoring and supportive roles of leaders, including school leaders, teaching specialists, and teachers (Ärlestig & Törnsen, 2014; Rodríguez & Gairín, 2017; York-Barr & Duke, 2004). <a name="_hlk172285393"></a>In the Chinese context, pedagogical leadership is often viewed as teaching method leadership and is considered a form of teaching method or teaching skill (Fonsén et al., 2023; Hsieh et al., 2024; Na et al., 2016; Xin et al., 2019). Therefore, this study  does not focus on pedagogical leadership as a broad term but rather on English teachers' pedagogical leadership in English teaching methods.
Based on Sergiovanni's (1998) conceptualization of pedagogical leadership, there are leadership roles for principals, school leaders (such as English department directors and grade directors), and teachers in the practice of pedagogical leadership. Pedagogical leadership can arise not only from formal school leaders but also from teachers' professional development and teaching practices (Rodríguez & Gairín, 2017). Generally, pedagogical leadership extends beyond formal positions; teachers without official titles can also act as pedagogical leaders (Gonzalez Fernandez et al., 2019; Grice et al., 2023). For teachers, pedagogical leadership is not optional, but is described as a requirement and responsibility for their teaching job. At the same time, teachers are recognized as playing pedagogical leadership and as having a role as pedagogical leaders within their team of educators (Bøe et al., 2022; Fonsén et al., 2023; Fonsén & Ukkonen-Mikkola, 2019; Heikka et al., 2020).
Based on the above definition, this study conceptualizes pedagogical leadership as a leadership approach in which the school engages teachers as co-participants in the pedagogical leadership practice process while working as a teaching and learning community that builds capital community relationships and empowers high-quality student achievement.
<a name="_hlk172197868"></a>*The Capital Dimension of Pedagogical Leadership*
Researchers have developed various models of pedagogical leadership. Sergiovanni (1998) identified four core dimensions of pedagogical leadership: social capital (community relationships), academic capital (academic culture), intellectual capital (professional knowledge), and professional capital (teaching methods skills). Gento and González (2012) proposed eight dimensions, including charismatic, emotional, and cultural, to assess pedagogical leadership in educational institutions. Robinson et al. (2009) developed a classification tool to characterize the quality of pedagogical leadership in schools, which includes setting teaching and learning goals, providing teaching resources, planning and evaluating teaching, supporting professional training, and shaping a quality teaching and learning environment. In addition, Ärlestig and Törnsen (2014) proposed three interrelated definitional models: goal-oriented, process-oriented, and result-oriented (Alonso-Yanez et al., 2021).
<a name="_hlk172381379"></a>Sergiovanni's (1998) four-capital model offers a thorough framework for pedagogical leadership using both internal and external school resources. However, it demands substantial resources and may neglect the influence of leaders’ personal qualities and style. Gento and González's (2012) eight-dimensional model addresses the broad aspects of pedagogical leadership and allows for contextual adaptation. It emphasizes how personal traits such as charisma and foresight affect leadership. However, its complexity can lead to resource strain and challenges in the management of multiple dimensions. Robinson's (2009) five-dimensional model offers practical strategies to assess pedagogical leadership quality. However, it tends to emphasize technical management aspects over the leader’s personal attributes, teacher support, and external school environment. Ärlestig and Törnsen's (2014) three-dimensional model connects pedagogical leadership directly with student achievement through specific strategies like walkthroughs and feedback. However, it oversimplifies leadership complexity and overlooks the school context, teacher development, and role of fostering school culture and growth. Therefore, we aimed to construct a multidimensional framework that incorporates pedagogical leadership capital, teaching methods, and student achievement to better understand and assess teachers' pedagogical leadership.
<a name="_hlk172198191"></a>According to Sergiovanni's (1998) definition, pedagogical leadership provides social capital and academic capital to support and enhance student achievement, and intellectual and professional capital to support teachers' professional development. Specifically, the social capital of pedagogical leadership involves the development of caring communities, which contributes to the development of academic capital when teaching decisions revolve around the curriculum and teachers as well as educational resources and teaching time (David, 2001; Leo, 2015). Intellectual capital develops schools as communities of inquiry, whereas professional capital fosters reciprocal shared responsibility between teachers and students (David, 2001; Sergiovanni, 1998).
Social capital (SC) is the internal and external relationship network and resources composed of interactions and trust in the school as a relational community (Daly et al., 2020; Lin, 2001; Mayger & Hochbein, 2021; Varlık et al., 2021). Social capital emphasizes trusting and reciprocal relationships within and outside schools that provide students with academic and social support and a sense of belonging (Fullan et al., 2015; Hargreaves & Fullan, 2015). <a name="ole_link3"></a>Academic capital (AC) refers to schools as academic-focused communities that establish and foster an academic culture and scholarly climate through the academic qualifications of teachers, their teaching performance, and their reputation and influence in academic and professional fields (Kholis, 2018; Sergiovanni, 1998; Varlık et al., 2021). Academic capital can motivate students to pursue high levels of achievement through rigorous academic standards, the effective implementation of teaching designs, the availability of academic resources, teaching assessments, feedback mechanisms, and commitment to student achievement (Cheng & Kaplowitz, 2016; Sergiovanni, 1998).
<a name="_hlk172288343"></a>Intellectual capital (IC) refers to the school as an inquiring community, and the expertise and capacity of teachers to promote educational theories of learning and teaching effectiveness, develop teaching and learning resources, and promote student learning strategies (Ibarra-Cisneros et al., 2023; Varlık et al., 2021). Intellectual capital encourages collaborative learning and knowledge sharing among teachers as well as effective communication and teaching practices between teachers and students, thus improving the quality of teaching and learning in schools (Nicolò et al., 2021). Professional capital (PC) refers to the professional practices and skills of schools as a community of practices in which teachers engage in teaching methods, skills, practices, and ethics (Hargreaves & Fullan, 2015; Turin & Davidson, 2022; Varlık et al., 2021; Watts & Richardson, 2020). Collaboration and trust among teachers and the implementation of community best practices help enhance teachers' professional development and student achievement (Fullan et al., 2015; Hargreaves & Fullan, 2015; Watts & Richardson, 2020).
The four capital dimensions of pedagogical leadership cannot simply be viewed as completely separate constructs; there are complex interdependencies and interactions among them that collectively affect student achievement (David, 2001; Sergiovanni, 1998). While these capital dimensions are interconnected, each represents a unique aspect of pedagogical leadership that impacts student achievement through different mechanisms (Côté & Levine, 2000; Daly et al., 2014, 2020; Salloum et al., 2017). Specifically, the social capital dimension of pedagogical leadership focuses on teachers' social interactions with regard to teaching and learning to promote student achievement. Academic capital emphasizes the development of scholarly resources, whereas intellectual capital centers on the accumulation of teaching experience. Professional capital highlights mastery of teaching methods and skills, with the aim of enhancing student achievement. As González-Fernández et al. (2021) state, although these capital dimensions may be interrelated, they may affect student achievement through different mechanisms and pathways. Nonetheless, conceptualizing these dimensions as separate constructs allows for a more nuanced examination of each dimension's unique contribution to student achievement.
While many researchers have analyzed the models proposed by Gento and González (2012), Robinson et al. (2009), and Ärlestig and Törnsen (2014), there is still a need for an empirical analysis of Sergiovanni's (1998) pedagogical leadership model centered on the teaching method-centered pedagogical leadership model for more empirical research (Berhanu, 2023; González-Fernández et al., 2021). <a name="_hlk172551933"></a>Some researchers suggest that further empirical research on capital dimensions of pedagogical leadership is needed to better understand their impact on student achievement (Berhanu, 2023; González-Fernández et al., 2021; Sergiovanni, 1998). <a name="_hlk172290087"></a>Previous research has demonstrated that all four dimensions of pedagogical leadership positively affect student achievement (Cheng & Kaplowitz, 2016; Fullan et al., 2015; Salloum et al., 2017). Therefore, this study utilized Sergiovanni's (1998) pedagogical leadership model because of its close relationship to the mediating variable of English teaching methods. This model facilitates the analysis of both the impact and mediating effects of the four dimensions of pedagogical leadership capital on student achievement (Alonso-Yanez et al., 2021). Therefore, hypotheses were proposed based on a literature review.
*H1*. Social <a name="_hlk172290072"></a>capital has a positive effect on student achievement.
*H2*. Academic capital has a positive effect on student achievement.
*H3*. Intellectual capital has a positive effect on student achievement.
*H4*. Professional capital has a positive effect on student achievement.
*English Teaching Methods*
<a name="ole_link4"></a><a name="_hlk172198511"></a>Pedagogical leadership is a key factor in implementing effective teaching methods and is positively correlated with student achievement (Alonso-Yanez et al., 2021; Okiri & Hercz, 2023; Palomino Fernández et al., 2023). <a name="_hlk172356803"></a>According to <a name="_hlk172134537"></a>Bureković et al.'s (2023) English teaching methods model based on language teaching theory, the four English teaching methods widely used in Chinese high school teaching are the Grammar-Translation Method, the Direct Method, the Audio-lingual Method, and Communicative Language Teaching, along with their connections to teachers' pedagogical leadership capital (Adebileje & Akinola, 2020; Bureković et al., 2023; Khalil & Kholofelo Semono-Eke, 2020; Orfan et al., 2021). These English teaching methods serve as mediating variables that reflect how teachers translate pedagogical leadership capital into specific teaching methods.
First, the Grammar-Translation Method (GTM) is the most widely used English teaching method in China (Na et al., 2016). GTM is mainly based on structuralist and behaviorist theories, and learns language by analyzing the structure and rules of language (Adebileje & Akinola, 2020; Bureković et al., 2023). GTM emphasizes grammar and translation, prioritizing reading and writing skills, but often overlooks speaking and communication skills (Ahmad, 2013). This means that even if learners pass English reading and writing tests, they may struggle to use English for authentic communication (Na et al., 2016). Second, the Direct Method (DM) is based on the natural acquisition theory, emphasizes oral communication skills, mimics native language acquisition, and uses visual tools, gestures, and demonstrations to aid interpretation and comprehension without focusing on grammatical rules (Khalil & Kholofelo Semono-Eke, 2020). The DM mimics the native language acquisition model with overemphasis on repetitive oral practice, which may neglect students' progress in comprehending English texts (Khalil & Kholofelo Semono-Eke, 2020).
Third, the Audio-lingual Method (ALM) is a teaching method that emphasizes contextual teaching and discourages the use of the native language (Khalil & Kholofelo Semono-Eke, 2020). ALM is based on behaviorist theory, in which students practice sentence patterns repeatedly through the mechanical habit of stimulus-response reinforcement, emphasizing the imitation of native English speakers in acquiring and using sentence patterns (Chen & Guo, 2020). However, overemphasis on mechanized sentence pattern practice and timely correction may cause psychological problems, such as aversion, lack of confidence, and psychological stress (Adebileje & Akinola, 2020; Bureković et al., 2023). Fourth, Communicative Language Teaching (CLT) is a student-centered teaching method based on interactive acquisition theory (Kosharna et al., 2022).<a name="_hlk172292375"></a> CLT focuses on functional and communicative language skills, and emphasizes the use of language teaching in authentic scenarios. This method emphasizes the development of students' communication skills, and includes role-playing simulations and puzzling activities. CLT does not require students to accurately master pronunciation, vocabulary, and grammar but rather to understand their meaning through communicative activities (Khalil & Kholofelo Semono-Eke, 2020). Although CLT emphasizes students' communicative competence, it neglects, to some extent, the understanding of language and literature (Ahmad, 2013).
<a name="_hlk172292123"></a>*The Mediating Role of English Teaching Methods*
<a name="_toc11434"></a><a name="_toc8479"></a><a name="_toc31434"></a><a name="_toc26198"></a>Pedagogical leadership is a model of leadership centered on teaching methods, and different teaching methods produce different student achievements (Gamarra-Mendoza & Brito-García, 2023; Grice et al., 2023; Palomino Fernández et al., 2023). In particular, the professional capital of pedagogical leadership provides a clear, engaging, and evidence-based definition of effective teaching methods and resources for teachers and school leaders to innovate and collaborate with teaching methods (Alonso-Nuez et al., 2024; Cole & Duffy, 2024; Fullan et al., 2015). Professional capital, which is reflected in teachers' mastery and utilization of teaching methods, is viewed as a mediating factor that connects pedagogical leadership to student achievement. According to Sergiovanni (1998), development of professional capital is a key mediating variable between pedagogical leadership and student achievement. <a name="_hlk172195045"></a>It enhances teachers' professional capital and improves student achievement through the support and foundation provided by social, academic, and intellectual capital in a reciprocal relationship between teachers and students. This study examines how pedagogical leadership affects student achievement through specific teaching methods with a focus on the critical role of professional capital.
<a name="_hlk171930917"></a><a name="_hlk172356876"></a><a name="_hlk172560153"></a>Sergiovanni (1998) emphasized that professional capital is the core embodiment of teachers' teaching methods, skills, and practices. Of the four dimensions of pedagogical leadership, professional capital is most directly related to teaching methods, while the other three capitals (social, academic, and intellectual) provide support and a foundation for the practice of teaching methods. Previous studies on professional capital suggest that teaching methods mediate the effect of professional capital on student achievement, and that teachers' teaching methods are key mediators in predicting the success of student achievement (Alonso-Nuez et al., 2024; Cole & Duffy, 2024; Sergiovanni, 1998; Supovitz, 2001). Therefore, there is a theoretical and empirical basis for the use of teaching methods as a mediating variable between professional capital and student achievement.
<a name="_hlk172293001"></a>However, in addition to the direct effects, the impact of teachers' <a name="_hlk172292649"></a>pedagogical leadership capital on student achievement can also be indirectly mediated through specific English teaching methods. These English teaching methods serve as mediating variables that reflect how teachers translate pedagogical leadership capital into specific teaching methods. <a name="_hlk172217592"></a>The mediating variable (i.e., English teaching methods) illuminates how teacher pedagogical leadership capital translates into the practice of specific teaching methods, which in turn affects student achievement. <a name="_hlk172356948"></a>Each teaching method is closely related to a specific teacher<a name="_hlk172292668"></a>'s pedagogical leadership capital and affects learning achievement through different mechanisms (see Table 1). Teachers' pedagogical leadership capital can have a differential impact on student achievement depending on the effectiveness of their chosen English teaching methods (Cole & Duffy, 2024; Datnow, 2012). Social capital provides interpersonal support for the teaching method, intellectual capital provides theoretical guidance for the teaching method, academic capital lays the linguistic and cultural foundation for the teaching method, and professional capital guarantees its effectiveness of the teaching method (Cheng & Kaplowitz, 2016; Daly et al., 2020; Flere et al., 2010). Specifically, GTM relies more on intellectual and academic capital, DM and CLT require social and professional capital, and ALM focuses more on intellectual and professional capital. Teachers can maximize the effectiveness of teaching by adopting appropriate teaching methods according to students' backgrounds, and by developing and using their four capitals in a targeted manner. Although all four English teaching methods are associated with all four capital types, specialized capital plays the most direct and significant role in the selection and implementation of teaching methods (Cole & Duffy, 2024; Sergiovanni, 1998; Supovitz, 2001). Therefore, we placed four mediating variables (GTM, DM, ALM, CLT) under "professional capital" to reflect the direct and mediating effects of professional capital on the selection and implementation of English teaching methods.
Based on the theory of action for teacher leadership, a hypothesis model was developed from Sergiovanni's (1998) pedagogical leadership model and Bureković et al.'s (2023) English teaching method model, which provides a robust theoretical foundation. Studies in Taiwan and other countries have shown that GTM, DM, ALM, and CLT mediate the effects of professional capital on student achievement (Cheng & Kaplowitz, 2016; Fullan et al., 2015; Zhang, 2021). <a name="_hlk172357681"></a>This study's research model conceptualizes teachers' pedagogical leadership through four key dimensions: social, academic, intellectual, and professional capital. These capitals represent different types of resources that teachers can mobilize and utilize in their teaching methods. This study explored how social capital (teachers' social interactions around teaching methods), academic capital (scholarly resources developed by teachers), intellectual capital (teaching experience accumulated by teachers), and professional capital (teaching method skills mastered by teachers) of teachers' pedagogical leadership are related to student achievement in English (Ahtiainen et al., 2021; Daly et al., 2020). Therefore, based on theories and findings from the literature, we developed a research hypothesis model of the direct effect of the four capital dimensions of pedagogical leadership on student achievement, and the mediating effect of English teaching methods (see Figure 1).
*H5*. The Grammar-Translation Method mediates the effect of professional capital on student achievement.
*H6*. The Direct Method mediates the effect of professional capital on student achievement.
*H7*. The Audio-lingual Method mediates the effect of professional capital on student achievement.
*H8*. Communicative Language Teaching mediates the effect of professional capital on student achievement.

<a name="_hlk172200351"></a>**Research context**
<a name="_hlk172378671"></a>Taian City, which is located in eastern China, is a famous historical tourist destination. The city's high schools are mainly located in different parts of the city, and students from rural and suburban areas must choose to board nearby high schools. High schools are classified as key senior high schools or ordinary high schools, based on their operational level and education quality. Key senior high schools receive better funding, have advanced facilities, and offer rich academic resources, whereas ordinary high schools have limited financial support and facilities (Huang et al., 2022; Qiao et al., 2017). Unlike the education systems in countries such as the United States, high school education in China is not compulsory. Students must undergo a competitive entrance <a name="_hlk172378323"></a>examination managed by municipal education bureaus. Admission to key senior high schools is based on examination scores, with high-achieving students attending key schools and average-achieving students attending regular schools (Hou et al., 2019).
<a name="_hlk172378728"></a><a name="_hlk172378741"></a>The Ministry of Education centrally manages China's high schools, setting uniform curriculum standards and exams. Staffing includes principals, assistant principals, department directors, grade directors, and teachers. Teachers must undergo specialized training to obtain certification. In Chinese high schools, it is common for teachers to serve as both English teachers and school leaders (Gonzalez Fernandez et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022; York-Barr & Duke, 2004). This dual role not only affects, to some extent, the allocation of teachers' teaching time and energy, but also provides them with opportunities to participate in school management and decision-making. The Taian Education Bureau supports teacher leadership development through various training sessions in each semester. Development varies by school, with key senior high schools generally offering greater leadership and autonomy to teachers than regular high schools (Huang et al., 2022; Leithwood & Mascall, 2008; Qiao et al., 2017).). Many key schools have professional development committees to support teachers' involvement in school management and decision making.
Chinese high school education spans three years, from grades 10 to 12, unlike K-12 systems elsewhere. The foreign languages that were taught included English, Korean, and Japanese. Taian high schools conduct two uniform examinations per semester: midterm and final. The Taian Education Bureau organizes citywide exams to ensure uniformity in standards, textbooks, questions, and times (Hou et al., 2019; Qiao et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2023). Schools and the Bureau rank student achievements based on citywide, school, and class averages. Teachers receive bonuses or face accountability based on these results (Wang et al., 2023).
<a name="_hlk172205864"></a>**Methodology**
To analyze the impact of pedagogical leadership on student achievement and the mediating effect of English teaching methods, this study used an online questionnaire to collect data from 968 participants in 18 high schools in Taian City, Shandong Province, China. The study was conducted with permission from the city's Department of Education and principals of the high schools involved. Their participation was strictly confidential and voluntary to alleviate concerns about anonymity. The questionnaires were translated into Chinese and validated by four English editorial experts. Content validity was verified by educational management experts (Werner & Campbell, 1970). 
*Sample*
In this study, G\*Power 3.1 was used to calculate the sample size (Kang, 2021). Considering the effects commonly observed in educational research and the amount of effects reported in similar educational studies (Faul et al., 2009; Fink, 2017; Kang, 2021), power analysis, "A-priori: Compute required sample size-given alpha, power and effect size" was selected for this research, where the effect size was fixed at 0.15, which represents a medium effect size, alpha error probability of 0.05, and power (1-β error probability) was set at 0.95. As the study included eight predictors, a minimum sample size of 160<a name="_hlk172399269"></a> was calculated; thus, 968 valid questionnaires were considered adequate.
<a name="_hlk172301316"></a><a name="_hlk172300874"></a><a name="_hlk172033874"></a>In this study, we employed a two-stage sampling strategy. First, cluster sampling was used to select high schools, followed by stratified sampling within each high school. First, we used a single-stage whole-cluster sampling to select a cluster of 18 high schools in Taian <a name="_hlk172301045"></a>City. This ensured that our sample was representative of high schools in Taian City. Second, within each selected high school, we used a proportional stratified random sampling technique to select English teachers, students, principals, and school leaders as study participants, ensuring representation across each group. Stratification was performed based on the <a name="_hlk172561662"></a>participants' roles (English teachers, students, and principals) to ensure that the sample was representative of the different roles in high schools. In particular, the school leaders comprised assistant principals, English department directors, and grade directors, who had leadership and mentoring responsibilities for English teachers and the management of teaching methods. We randomly selected 708 students to participate in the study to ensure their representation across various grades and classes. Within this <a name="_hlk172543100"></a>group of teachers, we distinguished between teachers and school leaders. This approach ensured that our sample reflected the perspectives of different high school roles regarding English teachers' pedagogical leadership and English teaching method practices.
<a name="_hlk172301969"></a>*Demographics*
This quantitative study was conducted in Taian City, which comprises 18 high schools. The study population consisted of principals, teachers, and students who worked in and were enrolled at high schools in Taian City during the 2022-2023 school year, totaling 968 participants. To collect valid data, 1000 questionnaires were distributed, and 968 valid responses (96% response rate) were received, satisfying statistical and theoretical generalizability. The sample included 18 principals, 242 English teachers (108 of whom served as both English teachers and school leaders), and 708 students. A total of 242 English teachers completed questionnaires on English teachers' pedagogical leadership and English teaching methods. Among the teacher participants, 47 (19.4%) were male, and 195 (80.6%) were female. Moreover, most participants had a bachelor’s degree (56.6%) and 3–5 years of teaching experience (53.3%). School leaders (e.g., English department directors and grade directors) accounted for 45% of the total number of English teachers. We received 708 valid questionnaires from the students (94% response rate). Of the student participants, 327 (46%) were male, and 381 (54%) were female. Therefore, this study focused on analyzing the impact and mediating effects of the variables through valid responses, and did not rely on a large number of participants to explain the generalizability of the study. 
<a name="_toc17957"></a><a name="_toc684762611"></a><a name="_toc888"></a><a name="_toc27837"></a>*Measures*
In this study, the survey scale developed by Varlık et al. (2021), based on Sergiovanni's (1998) pedagogical leadership model, was used to measure teachers' pedagogical leadership. It consists of 20 items (see Table 2). According to Sergiovanni's (1998) model, pedagogical leadership includes four dimensions: social, intellectual, academic, and professional capital. Orfan et al.'s (2021) survey scale, based on Bureković et al.'s (2023) English Teaching Methods Model, was used to measure the English teaching methods of English teachers. The English Teaching Method Survey Scale includes four major English teaching methods used in Chinese high schools: GTM, DM, ALM, and CLT. The initial scale consisted of 20 items, encompassing four primary English teaching methods: the Grammar-Translation Method (GTM), the Direct Method (DM), the Audio-Lingual Method (ALM), and Communicative Language Teaching (CLT). Exploratory factor and item analyses were conducted to enhance questionnaire efficiency. This process allowed us to select the three most representative items for each teaching method, resulting in a streamlined, 12-item scale (see Table 2). This refined instrument maintains a balanced representation of each method, while improving the overall effectiveness of the survey.
Participants rated their responses on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The scales have been validated in Ethiopia, Turkey, Cambodia, and China, and have shown strong reliability and validity (Berhanu, 2023; Llorent-Bedmar et al., 2021; Varlık et al., 2021).
<a name="_hlk172304603"></a>*Student Achievement Data*
Typically, <a name="_hlk171961787"></a>student achievement (SA) is measured using tests, specific tasks, course grades, or self-reported test results (Alp Christ et al., 2022; Li & Karanxha, 2022). This study used a comprehensive approach to measure students' English achievement using five items: four standardized examination scores (two midterm examination scores and two final examination scores), and one achievement satisfaction score (student, teacher, or school achievement satisfaction scores). The English achievement data were based on the 2022-2023 citywide English Public Examination, which consisted of two mid-term examinations and two final examinations. Data on English achievement were obtained from the Taian Examination System and individual high school websites. The high school English examination consists of listening, reading, and writing. Students' raw English achievement scores (0-100) were converted to a 5-point scale (from 1 "very poor" to 5 "excellent"), with corresponding grade ranges of 1 (30-40), 2 (41-55), 3 (56-70), 4 (71-85), and 5 (86-100). Higher scores indicate better grades (Nie et al., 2024; Zhao & Yang, 2023). The student achievement scores had a mean of 3.72 (SD = 0.89) and median of 4.
*Data collection*
<a name="_hlk172304404"></a>The English <a name="_hlk172311309"></a>teachers' pedagogical leadership and English teaching method questionnaires were completed by principals, English teachers, and students, who assessed teachers' pedagogical leadership and English teaching methods from the perspectives of their schools, teachers, and students, respectively. The student achievement questionnaire was completed by principals, English teachers, and students. Students completed four examination scores and one self-achievement satisfaction score; English teachers completed four class examination averages and one achievement satisfaction score based on their class's examination average scores; and principals completed four schoolwide examination average scores and one achievement satisfaction <a name="_hlk172303844"></a>score with the school's overall English achievement. Questionnaires on English teachers' pedagogical leadership, English teaching methods, and student achievement data were obtained from three groups: English teachers, principals, and students, who collectively answered the same questionnaire.
This study obtained data from multiple sources to create data triangulation to avoid the subjective bias of self-reported overrating or underrating (Hair et al., 2022; Yin, 2014). Data accuracy and consistency were verified by obtaining responses from various groups. <a name="_hlk172206816"></a>Specifically, if principals, teachers, and students perceive teachers' English teaching methods to be effective, this would increase the credibility of the findings. This approach allows for the integration and validation of responses, increases data reliability by reducing potential single-source bias, and effectively links student achievement data to <a name="_hlk172307344"></a>teachers' pedagogical leadership and English teaching method data (Hair et al., 2022).
<a name="_hlk172307629"></a>*Data Analysis Procedure*
This study aimed to analyze the direct effect of teachers' pedagogical leadership on student achievement. While considering the mediating effect of the English teaching method, we analyzed the mediating effects of GTM, DM, ALM, and CLT on the effects of professional capital on student achievement. We used a quantitative method to test the research hypotheses and analyzed the data using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) with SmartPLS 4.1.0.0. Despite gathering data from various populations, this study aimed to analyze the direct impact of teachers' pedagogical leadership on student achievement and the mediating role of English teaching methods, without exploring nested effects or variations across different levels. PLS-SEM is suitable for studying complex multiple indicators and constructing hypothesis analyses that can tolerate less than 500 study samples, and can also be used to study structural models with causal effects and predictors of multiple variables (Hair et al., 2022). 
The PLS-SEM analysis consisted of two phases: (a) assessment of the reflective measurement model and (b) assessment of the structural model. The measurement model assessed the reliability and validity of the scales and study models to ensure convergent validity and discriminant validity (Hair et al., 2022). For mediation analysis, the bootstrap method was used to detect indirect effects (Hayes, 2009).We used the heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlation (HTMT) to test discriminant validity (Henseler et al., 2015). In addition, the structural model reported a path analysis, including direct and indirect effects, and mediating effects, to demonstrate the structural relationship between the hypothesized model variables and the significance of the effects.
<a name="_hlk172207978"></a>**Results**
<a name="_toc669514972"></a>*Measurement Model Assessment*
In the first stage, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess the reliability and validity of the measurement constructs before testing the proposed hypotheses. The CFA examined the construct validity of the measurement model, and SEM examined the path between potential variables for direct and indirect effects. Model fit was evaluated by a series of indicators, such as NFI, SRMR, and rms-Theta. A path model with an SRMR of 0.08 is considered a good fit (Hayes, 2009; Hu & Bentler, 1999; Schreiber et al., 2006). The rms-Theta index below a threshold of 0.12 indicates a well-fit model (Henseler et al., 2014). The CFA results indicated a good model fit (NFI = 0.958; SRMR = 0.052; rms-Theta = 0.077) (see Table 2). Specifically, the reflective measurement model assessment validated indicator reliability by factor loadings greater than 0.70 (Hair et al., 2019). Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach's alpha greater than 0.70, composite reliability (CR) greater than 0.70, and convergent validity using average variance extracted (AVE) greater than 0.50 (Hair et al., 2022), while discriminant validity was tested using the Heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlation (HTMT) smaller than 0.9 criterion (Henseler et al., 2015).
<a name="_toc9022"></a>The factor loadings for all items had to be significant and greater than<a name="_hlk172208671"></a> 0.70 (Hair et al., 2017; Ramayah et al., 2018). Items with factor loadings below 0.70 were removed, and only items with loadings greater than 0.70 were retained. Table 2 shows that both the Cronbach's Alpha and CR values were greater than 0.7. In addition, the AVE of each construct was above the threshold of 0.50, and all the variables showed satisfactory discriminant validity and reliability (Hair et al., 2017; Ramayah et al., 2018). Therefore, our research model and indicators conform to the criteria for SEM model assessment and hypothesis testing.

The mediating effects test was used to analyze the mediating role of English teaching methods on pedagogical leadership and student achievement. <a name="_hlk172143641"></a>Figure 2 and Table 5 show the results of the indirect effects. The indirect effect of professional capital on student achievement mediated by DM was significant (*β* = 0.157, *p* < 0.001), indicating that DM played a mediating role in the effect of professional capital on student achievement. After determining the significance of the structural model, we assessed its explanatory power of the structural model using R<sup>2</sup> values, where the explanatory power was divided into three levels of explanatory power: significant (R<sup>2</sup> > 0.75), moderate (0.75 > R<sup>2</sup> > 0.50), and weak (R<sup>2</sup> < 0.25) explanatory power (Hair et al., 2022). As shown in Table 4, the R² value was 54% (0.537), indicating that the eight constructs, that is, the four dimensions of pedagogical leadership and the four English teaching methods, had a significant explanatory power for student achievement. Table 5 shows that the R² values for GTM, DM, ALM, and CLT were 60% (0.606), 37% (0.366), 44% (0.441), and 50% (0.500), respectively, indicating a moderate explanatory power.
The values of Q<sup>2</sup> as a relative measure of predictive correlation, 0.02, 0.15, and 0.35 indicate small, medium, and large predictive correlations of exogenous constructs on endogenous constructs, respectively (Hair et al., 2019). Table 5 shows that GTM, DM, ALM, and CLT had Q² values of 0.455, 0.245, 0.331, and 0.350, respectively, with GTM and CLT having strong predictive power, while DM and ALM had moderate predictive power.
The f<sup>2</sup> values were used to explain the effect sizes, where the values of 0.02, 0.15, and 0.35 represent small, medium, and large effects of exogenous latent variables, respectively (Hair et al., 2019). The results in Table 4 show that the f<sup>2</sup> value for professional capital is 0.470, indicating a large explanatory effect on student achievement. The f<sup>2</sup> values for the DM had a large explanatory effect on student achievement. In addition, f<sup>2</sup> values of 0.331 and 0.350 for the ALM and CLT, respectively, showed a moderate explanatory effect on student achievement.
This study assessed the strength of the mediating effects of DM, ALM, and CLT by computing the variance accounted for (VAF) (Hair et al., 2011). If the VAF is less than 20%, it indicates that the mediating effect is almost zero; between 20% and 80% is classified as partial mediation, and above 80% is considered full mediation (Hair et al., 2022). Table 5 shows that DM and CLT explained 21.1% and 20.8% of the variance, respectively, in terms of the strength of the mediating professional capital effect on student achievement. This means that DM and CLT partially mediate the effect of professional capital on student achievement. In addition, ALM explained 16.4% of the variance but showed no significant mediating effect.
<a name="_hlk172312295"></a>**Discussion**
<a name="_toc1721032441"></a>Supported by the theory of action for teacher leadership, specifically using Sergiovanni's (1998) pedagogical leadership theory and Bureković et al.'s (2023) English teaching methods model, this study analyzed the impact of teachers' pedagogical leadership on student achievement and the mediating impact of English teaching methods.
<a name="_hlk172311256"></a>*The Impact of Teachers' Pedagogical Leadership on Student Achievement*
This study found that <a name="_hlk172312121"></a>teachers' pedagogical leadership has a significant direct impact on student achievement, specifically in terms of academic and professional capital. These results are consistent with the theory of action for teacher leadership. This is consistent with the results of previous studies such as Fullan's (2015) Canadian accountability study and Cheng and Kaplowitz's (2016) structural equation study in Taiwan. The results indicate that teachers' professional capital, such as an in-depth understanding of subject matter content and effective teaching methods, has a significantly positive impact on student achievement. This means that teachers who have solid academic knowledge, effectively utilize a variety of teaching resources, actively engage in professional development, and provide support and guidance on individualized teaching methods based on the diverse needs of their students are more likely to be effective in improving student achievement (Cheng & Kaplowitz, 2016; Fullan et al., 2015; Palomino Fernández et al., 2023). The finding that academic and professional capital is directly linked to pedagogical leadership highlights the importance of teachers' pedagogical leadership in professional development and resource allocation (Fonsén et al., 2023; Hellström & Hagquist, 2021; Shen et al., 2020). <a name="_hlk172214044"></a><a name="_hlk172358504"></a>Thus, the greater the empowerment of pedagogical leadership, the greater the power of teachers to mobilize diverse pedagogical training strategies to achieve qualifications, and the higher the level of pedagogical leadership of teachers, the higher the quality of students' achievements (Gamarra-Mendoza & Brito-García, 2023).
However, social capital and intellectual capital did not have a positive effect on student achievement, which is similar to the findings of Côté and Levine (2000), Datnow (2012), Levine (2000), and Yang (2017), but different from the findings of Salloum (2017), Zhang (2021), and Nicolò et al. (2021). The differences in the results can be attributed to several factors such as differences in sample demographic characteristics, subject areas, data analysis methods, capital measures and levels, and educational policies. Specifically, the inconsistent results are due to sample demographics (Chinese high school students, American Midwestern high school students, Italian public university students), the scope and nature of the study (the impact of pedagogical leadership capital on students' English achievement; the relationship between equity in the distribution of social capital in schools and academic achievement; and the relationship between intellectual capital and academic achievement at the Italian public university), subject achievement (English teaching methods, mathematics, and reading), data analysis methods (structural equation modeling of SmartPLS, hierarchical linear modeling and structural equation modeling, SPSS multiple regression modeling), and capital measures (scale analysis of the four dimensions of pedagogical leadership, 11-item scale to measure social capital, capital performance report). 
School management also reflects the prevalence of collectivism and respect for authority in the Chinese cultural context (Bush, 2020; Hsieh et al., 2024). Principals often adopt a top-down management approach that may restrict teachers' autonomy and opportunities for collaboration, thereby hindering the full utilization of social and intellectual capital (Bush & Haiyan, 2000; Liu et al., 2022; Xin et al., 2019). For example, teachers may be afraid to express their views openly or be unwilling to share their teaching experiences for fear of being perceived as a challenging authority. This may lead to difficulties in fully utilizing the social and intellectual capital of schools, ultimately affecting students’ achievement. Teachers are less likely to use pedagogical leadership in schools when they are not closely linked to classroom teaching or leadership tasks (Peng & Chudy, 2021). Despite the increasing role of pedagogical leadership, teachers are limited by power distance and hierarchical management structures, and may regard pedagogical leadership as an extra task rather than empowerment, which may cause stress, ultimately affecting the improvement of students' achievement (Bush & Haiyan, 2000; Opazo et al., 2023; Xin et al., 2019). In addition, teachers' relatively limited leadership functions within and outside the school's capital, and the potentially unequal distribution of resources and power, affect the translation of social and intellectual capital into student achievement gains (Hsieh et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022; Xin et al., 2019). As stated in the theory of action for teacher leadership, if teachers feel neglected, excluded, or burdened by pedagogical leadership, they may exhibit negative behaviors and attitudes that ultimately affect student achievement (Bush & Haiyan, 2000; Xin et al., 2019). To address this issue, school leaders empower teachers, encourage their participation in school decision making, and provide them with more opportunities to collaborate.
In general, social and intellectual capital are not the only factors that determine student achievement (McNeal, 2015). Our study did not control for other factors that may have affected student achievement. For example, factors such as school size, school culture, school philosophy, and teaching policies may affect the allocation of school resources and the teaching and learning environment; factors such as teachers' teaching experience, teaching efficacy, and level of professional development may affect teachers' pedagogical leadership and choice of teaching methods; and factors such as students' learning motivation, learning ability, family background, and family involvement may affect student achievement (Côté & Levine, 2000; Hou et al., 2019; Warren, 2005). These factors may interact with pedagogical leadership and explain the discrepancies between our findings and those of previous studies. For example, students' socioeconomic status affects their learning resources, family environment, and learning motivation; school location and rankings affect school resources, community support, and student population characteristics; key senior high schools tend to have more resources; and regular high schools have a relatively lower level of resources, which will affect the implementation of teachers' pedagogical leadership and student achievement (Alonso-Yanez et al., 2021; Hou et al., 2019; Leana & Pil, 2006).
*The mediating role of English teaching methods*
Previous studies have validated that professional capital improves student achievement through effective English teaching methods (Alp Christ et al., 2022; Chen & Guo, 2020; Fullan et al., 2015). The results of the mediating effect of the English teaching method in this study are consistent with H6, H7, and H8, indicating that DM, ALM, and CLT are positively correlated with student achievement and play mediating roles. This result is consistent with those of international studies (Ghofur et al., 2017; Kosharna et al., 2022; Phoeun & Sengsri, 2021). Moreover, DM and CLT partially mediated the effect of professional capital on <a name="_hlk172312762"></a>student <a name="_hlk172312758"></a>achievement. The results show that teachers' pedagogical leadership does not only affect student achievement directly, but also indirectly affects student achievement through English teaching methods. This implies that teachers can effectively improve students' English achievement, in addition to the positive effects of pedagogical leadership capital in teaching, by using appropriate English teaching methods (Bureković et al., 2023; Na et al., 2016; Samur & Duman, 2011). This finding suggests that teachers should utilize a variety of effective teaching methods when implementing pedagogical leadership, and make flexible adjustments based on the actual teaching situation. Schools should provide teachers with professional development to help them acquire knowledge and skills using various teaching methods.
By contrast, GTM did not show a significant mediating effect, contrary to the findings of Samur and Duman (2011). However, the inconsistent results may be due to sample demographics (Chinese high school students versus Turkish middle school students), the scope and nature of the study (effectiveness of the GTM on student achievement versus investigating brain-based learning approaches to support e-learning strategies), different data analysis methods ( SmartPLS PLS-SEM versus stratified ordinary least squares calculations using SPSS), and different measures of pedagogical leadership (scale analysis of the four dimensions of pedagogical leadership versus experimental studies). However, GTM does not show a significant mediating effect, possibly because it overemphasizes grammatical rules and translation exercises, and neglects the practical use of language. Teachers should overcome the limitations of GTM and incorporate new teaching methods and strategies to improve their teaching practices continuously (Na et al., 2016).
To promote teachers' pedagogical leadership practices and enhance student achievement in schools as professional communities, school and teacher pedagogical leaders must collaborate in joint efforts (Fonsén & Ukkonen-Mikkola, 2019; Grice et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2020). Regarding teachers' pedagogical leadership, principals create a positive and democratic school climate through decentralization and delegation of authority, and provide teachers with opportunities to participate in school decision-making. They should also provide teachers with opportunities for professional development to enhance the rational allocation and utilization of their teaching capital resources.<a name="_hlk172214245"></a> Schools must reconcile the tension between teachers’ autonomy in pedagogical leadership and hierarchical management structures (Grice et al., 2023). When leadership is distributed, teachers can utilize their teaching methods of knowledge, enthusiasm, and imagination, which are critical for improving student achievement (Bøe et al., 2022; Fonsén et al., 2023; Opazo et al., 2023). Therefore, to alleviate the psychological stress and heavy teaching load of implementing pedagogical leadership, school leaders should share pedagogical leadership widely in their schools, and scheduling common times for teaching and learning discussions at teacher meetings can facilitate the development of pedagogical leadership among teachers(Peng & Chudy, 2021). 
In terms of English teaching methods, teachers are encouraged to innovate their teaching methods by providing an innovative teaching incentive fund to support them in trying new teaching methods and techniques (Phoeun & Sengsri, 2021). Specifically, when teachers constantly update their subject knowledge and apply it to classroom teaching, students become better equipped to understand and master it. Similarly, by attending professional development training and seminars, teachers who are up-to-date on the latest teaching methods can be more effective in helping students improve their performance. <a name="_hlk172214306"></a>Teachers implement different teaching methods based on their students' backgrounds, and providing effective feedback and advice can motivate them to achieve high quality student achievement. School leaders should encourage teachers to change from traditional teacher-centered teaching to student-centered teaching methods, such as DM, ALM, and CLT, to give teachers greater autonomy and encourage collaboration and communication (Fonsén et al., 2023; Grice et al., 2023; Johanna & Waniganayake, 2011). Therefore, to make English teaching methods more effective, it is recommended that teachers become more involved in the school community, address teaching method issues through communication and collaboration, and promote professional learning and sharing among teachers. Teachers should acquire the necessary teaching skills, especially in the application of teaching practices and the integration of multiple forms of teaching content and technology, which in turn will enhance their level of teachers' pedagogical leadership.
<a name="_hlk172215568"></a><a name="_hlk172317267"></a>**Implications**
*Theoretical implications*
This study contributes to theoretical and empirical literature on <a name="_hlk172315077"></a>teachers' pedagogical leadership. First, it incorporates the theory of action for teacher leadership and the use of Sergiovanni's (1998) pedagogical leadership theory and Bureković et al.'s (2023) English teaching method model, based on language teaching theory. This study employed a multidimensional systematic teachers' pedagogical leadership research model that integrates independent variables (social, intellectual, academic, and professional capital), mediating variables (GTM, DM, ALM, and CLT), and the dependent variable (student achievement). This study addresses a research gap regarding the lack of research on the multidimensional and mediating effects of teachers' pedagogical leadership. This contributes to a theoretical foundation that enhances understanding of the pathways and means by which pedagogical leadership influences educational outcomes, particularly in the areas of teachers' pedagogical leadership and English teaching methods. By integrating Western theories of pedagogical leadership with Chinese English teaching practices, this study offers targeted and actionable suggestions for reforming English teaching methods in Chinese high schools.
<a name="_hlk172315541"></a>Second, this study is unique in its use of Sergiovanni's (1998) four-dimensional questionnaire on pedagogical leadership, further enriching the literature on the multi-perspective understanding of teachers' pedagogical leadership. Specifically, the study found that academic and professional capital had a positive direct effect on student achievement and that the effect of professional capital on student achievement was mediated by DM, ALM, and CLT in the context of English teaching methods. Additionally, this study confirmed the mediating effects of four English teaching methods, demonstrating that DM, ALM, and CLT play significant mediating roles in the impact of teachers' pedagogical leadership professional capital on student achievement, positively influencing student achievement. These findings expand the horizons of English teaching theories and emphasize the differing impacts of various English teaching methods on student achievement.More importantly, the study reveals that different English teaching methods are not equally affected by the professional capital dimension of teachers' pedagogical leadership, providing an empirical basis for a deeper and more nuanced understanding of language teaching theory. This study contributes to the literature on the pathways and mediating effects of teachers' pedagogical leadership on student achievement and offers valuable insights into educational policy reform and teacher training in teaching methods.
Third, although many studies have analyzed the effectiveness of instructional leadership on student achievement, there is little evidence to support the positive impact of teachers' pedagogical leadership on student achievement (Berhanu, 2023; Varlık et al., 2021).. This study demonstrated that teachers' pedagogical leadership positively impacts student achievement, indicating that the effectiveness of pedagogical leadership on student achievement can complement instructional leadership. These findings are particularly important because they significantly improve high school teachers' teaching methods and increase their student achievement. Therefore, this study expands previous research that has focused solely on instructional leadership to enrich the understanding of the effective impact of different educational leadership styles on student achievement, especially in the Chinese context. The generalizability of the results to China is possible because each province or city adopts uniform examination standards, textbooks, and examination questions, including uniform examination times (Hou et al., 2019; Qiao et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2023). Moreover, this study provides a new research avenue for improving teachers' pedagogical leadership in English language teaching, thereby enhancing their teaching and student achievement.
Fourth, this study reaffirms the critical role of <a name="_hlk172317953"></a>teachers' pedagogical leadership in building strong professional capital communities (Belay et al., 2022). When school leaders foster a culture of collaboration, trust, and continuous improvement within the professional capital community, they help teachers share experiences, engage in teaching method practices, and enhance professional capital, which in turn improves student achievement. This suggests that teachers' professional capital, that is, their teaching methods, skills, and knowledge, does not automatically translate into better student achievement. These findings address this research gap and suggest that improving student achievement must include elements of both teachers' pedagogical leadership capital and their teaching methods, as these two factors work together to produce significant associations with student achievement. Teachers need to develop professional capital, including their skills and knowledge of teaching methods, to effectively implement pedagogical leadership and positively impact student achievement.
*Practical implications*
<a name="_hlk172320006"></a>The results of this study can be used to improve teachers' pedagogical leadership practices to enhance student achievement among high school teachers in Taian City. Additionally, the mediating effects of English teaching methods discussed in this study are closely related to teachers' pedagogical leadership. This suggests that effective use of English teaching methods is an integral part of teachers' pedagogical leadership. Teachers should ensure their ability to predict and improve student achievement, and professional capital should mediate effective improvements in teaching methods that lead to high-quality student achievement (Chen & Guo, 2020; Fullan et al., 2015). From a practical perspective, the results of this study, based on a survey of 968 respondents, provide useful information for educational policy reforms and teacher training in terms of teaching methods. For example, practicing pedagogical leadership and strategies to improve teaching methods can enhance student achievement and mitigate the negative psychological effects of polarization and dropout crises. Policymakers, school principals, and training departments should collaborate to improve teachers' pedagogical leadership and enhance teaching method practices (Opazo et al., 2023). As a research recommendation, it is suggested to link the theoretical practice of teachers' pedagogical leadership and teaching methods, because both models play an important role in promoting student achievement.
The results of this study have a positive impact on public attitudes and quality of life. First, the results of the study will lead the public to pay more attention to the central role of teachers in education reform, rather than solely focusing on principals or school leaders (Opazo et al., 2023). This will encourage the public to embrace and support more diverse and personalized teaching and learning styles, rather than adhering to a single model. Second, the study provides valuable data on the public's choice of high school and enhances trust and satisfaction within the school community (Gamarra-Mendoza & Brito-García, 2023). Third, the study's findings will help narrow the education gap, alleviate educational polarization and the dropout crisis, and provide equitable quality education for more students. This will improve the quality of education and life of students, helping them secure better employment opportunities and income levels, and ultimately enhancing their socioeconomic development (Fullan et al., 2015; Gamarra-Mendoza & Brito-García, 2023).
**Limitations of the study**
Despite the interesting results of this study, it has some limitations. First, this study was conducted in China, and the findings may not be applicable to other countries because of the diversity of the Chinese population, different school systems, and varying levels of pedagogical leadership. Future research should consider other parts of Asia and other regions as well. This study calls for further research and collaboration between scholars in the East and West to compare these findings with other educational leadership methods, such as distributed leadership, and to examine the different impacts of teachers' and principals' pedagogical leadership on student achievement. Second, this study focused only on English, so the findings may not be applicable to other subjects. Future studies should be conducted with other subjects to test the generalizability of these findings. Third, this study used a cross-sectional analysis, which could only obtain short-term results, and could not reveal the long-term effects of pedagogical leadership on student achievement. Future research could adopt a longitudinal research design to track changes in teachers' pedagogical leadership and its long-term effects on student achievement. Alternatively, experimental or qualitative methods can be used to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying the influence of pedagogical leadership. Fourth, the theoretical model used in this study can be improved further. This study did not consider the confounding effects of other control factors such as demographic variables, which may be one of the reasons for the inconsistency between the results of other studies. This study only examined the mediating role of English teaching methods in the relationship between teachers' pedagogical leadership and student achievement. Future research could expand the range of mediating variables, such as school culture, teacher empowerment, teacher teaching efficacy, students' socioeconomic status, learning motivation, learning ability, and study habits, to understand the factors that influence student achievement from multiple perspectives.
**Conclusion**
<a name="ole_link15"></a>Supported by the theory of action for teacher leadership, specifically using Sergiovanni's (1998) pedagogical leadership model and Bureković et al.'s (2023) English teaching methods model, this study analyzed the direct effects of social, intellectual, academic, and professional capital on student achievement as well as the mediating effects of GTM, DM, ALM, and CLT on the effects of professional capital on student achievement in teachers' pedagogical leadership. The results showed that academic and professional capital had significant direct effects on student achievement, among the four capital dimensions of teachers' pedagogical leadership. Additionally, the study found that DM, ALM, and CLT, among the four mediating factors had a significant mediating effect on the influence of professional capital on student achievement.
Effective teachers' pedagogical leadership can not only narrow the student achievement gap, but also achieve the goal of quality education (Alp Christ et al., 2022; Bureković et al., 2023; Gamarra-Mendoza & Brito-García, 2023). As pedagogical leaders, teachers need to engage in professional training in pedagogical leadership and effective teaching methods, and adopt different teaching methods according to students' English proficiency levels. Furthermore, it is recommended that teachers enhance the appropriate use of various pedagogical leadership resources, among which the development of academic capital, professional capital, DM, ALM, and CLT are effective in achieving high-quality student achievement. Teacher pedagogical leaders have tremendous pedagogical leadership capital to strengthen school communities, build trust, and collaborate effectively. By professionally raising school leadership and teacher capital, they can develop optimal student achievement (Belay et al., 2022; Fullan et al., 2015; Hargreaves & Fullan, 2015).
